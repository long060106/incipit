<p>Ragueneau, the duenna. Then Roxane, Cyrano, and two pages.</p>
<p class="verse">RAGUENEAU:<br> —And then, off she went, with a musketeer! Deserted and ruined too, I<br> would make an end of all, and so hanged myself. My last breath was drawn:—<br> then in comes Monsieur de Bergerac! He cuts me down, and begs his cousin to<br> take me for her steward.</p>
<p class="verse">THE DUENNA:<br> Well, but how came it about that you were thus ruined?</p>
<p class="verse">RAGUENEAU:<br> Oh! Lise loved the warriors, and I loved the poets! What cakes there were<br> that Apollo chanced to leave were quickly snapped up by Mars. Thus ruin was<br> not long a-coming.</p>
<p class="verse">THE DUENNA (rising, and calling up to the open window):<br> Roxane, are you ready? They wait for us!</p>
<p class="verse">ROXANE’S VOICE (from the window):<br> I will but put me on a cloak!</p>
<p class="verse">THE DUENNA (to Ragueneau, showing him the door opposite):<br> They wait us there opposite, at Clomire’s house. She receives them all<br> there to-day—the precieuses, the poets; they read a discourse on the Tender<br> Passion.</p>
<p class="verse">RAGUENEAU:<br> The Tender Passion?</p>
<p class="verse">THE DUENNA (in a mincing voice):<br> Ay, indeed!<br> (Calling up to the window):<br> Roxane, an you come not down quickly, we shall miss the discourse on the<br> Tender Passion!</p>
<p class="verse">ROXANE’S VOICE:<br> I come! I come!</p>
<p>(A sound of stringed instruments approaching.)</p>
<p class="verse">CYRANO’S VOICE (behind the scenes, singing):<br> La, la, la, la!</p>
<p class="verse">THE DUENNA (surprised):<br> They serenade us?</p>
<p class="verse">CYRANO (followed by two pages with arch-lutes):<br> I tell you they are demi-semi-quavers, demi-semi-fool!</p>
<p class="verse">FIRST PAGE (ironically):<br> You know then, Sir, to distinguish between semi-quavers and demi-semi-<br> quavers?</p>
<p class="verse">CYRANO:<br> Is not every disciple of Gassendi a musician?</p>
<p class="verse">THE PAGE (playing and singing):<br> La, la!</p>
<p class="verse">CYRANO (snatching the lute from him, and going on with the phrase):<br> In proof of which, I can continue! La, la, la, la!</p>
<p class="verse">ROXANE (appearing on the balcony):<br> What? ’Tis you?</p>
<p class="verse">CYRANO (going on with the air, and singing to it):<br> ’Tis I, who come to serenade your lilies, and pay my devoir to your ro-o-oses!</p>
<p class="verse">ROXANE:<br> I am coming down!</p>
<p class="verse">(She leaves the balcony.)<br> <br> THE DUENNA (pointing to the pages):<br> How come these two virtuosi here?</p>
<p class="verse">CYRANO:<br> ’Tis for a wager I won of D’Assoucy. We were disputing a nice point in<br> grammar; contradictions raged hotly—‘’Tis so!’ ‘Nay, ’tis so!’ when suddenly<br> he shows me these two long-shanks, whom he takes about with him as an escort,<br> and who are skillful in scratching lute-strings with their skinny claws! ‘I<br> will wager you a day’s music,’ says he!—And lost it! Thus, see you, till<br> Phoebus’ chariot starts once again, these lute-twangers are at my heels,<br> seeing all I do, hearing all I say, and accompanying all with melody. ’Twas<br> pleasant at the first, but i’ faith, I begin to weary of it already!<br> (To the musicians):<br> Ho there! go serenade Montfleury for me! Play a dance to him!<br> (The pages go toward the door. To the duenna):<br> I have come, as is my wont, nightly, to ask Roxane whether. . .<br> (To the pages, who are going out):<br> Play a long time,—and play out of tune!<br> (To the duenna):<br> . . .Whether her soul’s elected is ever the same, ever faultless!</p>
<p class="verse">ROXANE (coming out of the house):<br> Ah! How handsome he is, how brilliant a wit! And—how well I love him!</p>
<p class="verse">CYRANO (smiling):<br> Christian has so brilliant a wit?</p>
<p class="verse">ROXANE:<br> Brighter than even your own, cousin!</p>
<p class="verse">CYRANO:<br> Be it so, with all my heart!</p>
<p class="verse">ROXANE:<br> Ah! methinks ’twere impossible that there could breathe a man on this earth<br> skilled to say as sweetly as he all the pretty nothings that mean so much—<br> that mean all! At times his mind seems far away, the Muse says naught—and<br> then, presto! he speaks—bewitchingly! enchantingly!</p>
<p class="verse">CYRANO (incredulously):<br> No, no!</p>
<p class="verse">ROXANE:<br> Fie! That is ill said! But lo! men are ever thus! Because he is fair to<br> see, you would have it that he must be dull of speech.</p>
<p class="verse">CYRANO:<br> He hath an eloquent tongue in telling his love?</p>
<p class="verse">ROXANE:<br> In telling his love? why, ’tis not simple telling, ’tis dissertation, ’tis<br> analysis!</p>
<p class="verse">CYRANO:<br> How is he with the pen?</p>
<p class="verse">ROXANE:<br> Still better! Listen,—here:—<br> (Reciting):<br> ‘The more of my poor heart you take<br> The larger grows my heart!’<br> (Triumphantly to Cyrano):<br> How like you those lines?</p>
<p class="verse">CYRANO:<br> Pooh!</p>
<p class="verse">ROXANE:<br> And thus it goes on. . .<br> ‘And, since some target I must show<br> For Cupid’s cruel dart,<br> Oh, if mine own you deign to keep,<br> Then give me your sweet heart!’</p>
<p class="verse">CYRANO:<br> Lord! first he has too much, then anon not enough! How much heart does the<br> fellow want?</p>
<p class="verse">ROXANE:<br> You would vex a saint!. . .But ’tis your jealousy.</p>
<p class="verse">CYRANO (starting):<br> What mean you?</p>
<p class="verse">ROXANE:<br> Ay, your poet’s jealousy! Hark now, if this again be not tender-sweet?—<br> ‘My heart to yours sounds but one cry:<br> If kisses fast could flee<br> By letter, then with your sweet lips<br> My letters read should be!<br> If kisses could be writ with ink,<br> If kisses fast could flee!’</p>
<p class="verse">CYRANO (smiling approvingly in spite of himself):<br> Ha! those last lines are,—hm!. . .hm!. . .<br> (Correcting himself—contemptuously):<br> —They are paltry enough!</p>
<p class="verse">ROXANE:<br> And this. . .</p>
<p class="verse">CYRANO (enchanted):<br> Then you have his letters by heart?</p>
<p class="verse">ROXANE:<br> Every one of them!</p>
<p class="verse">CYRANO:<br> By all oaths that can be sworn,—’tis flattering!</p>
<p class="verse">ROXANE:<br> They are the lines of a master!</p>
<p class="verse">CYRANO (modestly):<br> Come, nay. . .a master?. . .</p>
<p class="verse">ROXANE:<br> Ay, I say it—a master!</p>
<p class="verse">CYRANO:<br> Good—be it so.</p>
<p class="verse">THE DUENNA (coming down quickly):<br> Here comes Monsieur de Guiche!<br> (To Cyrano, pushing him toward the house):<br> In with you! ’twere best he see you not; it might perchance put him on the scent. . .</p>
<p class="verse">ROXANE (to Cyrano):<br> Ay, of my own dear secret! He loves me, and is powerful, and, if he knew,<br> then all were lost! Marry! he could well deal a deathblow to my love!</p>
<p class="verse">CYRANO (entering the house):<br> Good! good!</p>
<p>(De Guiche appears.)</p>
