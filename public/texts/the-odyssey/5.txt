<p class="lede">Mercury despatched by Jupiter to Calypso with a message commanding her to send away Ulysses⁠—A raft constructed by Ulysses⁠—His departure on the raft⁠—A storm raised by Neptune, and the raft destroyed⁠—Escape of Ulysses from the tempest, and his landing on the isle of Scheria, inhabited by the Phaeacians.</p>
<p class="verse">Aurora, rising from her couch beside <br> The famed Tithonus, brought the light of day <br> To men and to immortals. Then the gods <br> Came to their seats in council. With them came <br> High-thundering Jupiter, amongst them all <br> The mightiest. Pallas, mindful of the past, <br> Spake of Ulysses and his many woes, <br> Grieved that he still was with the island nymph:⁠— <br> “O father Jove, and all ye blessed ones <br> Who live forever! let not sceptred king <br> Henceforth be gracious, mild, and merciful <br> And righteous; rather be he deaf to prayer <br> And prone to deeds of wrong, since no one now <br> Remembers the divine Ulysses more <br> Among the people over whom he ruled <br> Benignly, like a father. Still he lies, <br> Weighed down by many sorrows, in the isle <br> And dwelling of Calypso, who so long <br> Constrains his stay. To his dear native land <br> Depart he cannot; ship arrayed with oars <br> And seamen has he none, to bear him o’er <br> The breast of the broad ocean. Nay, even now, <br> Against his well-beloved son a plot <br> is laid, to slay him as he journeys home <br> From Pylos the divine, and from the walls <br> Of famous Sparta, whither he had gone <br> To gather tidings of his father’s fate.”</p>
<p class="verse">Then answered her the Ruler of the storms:⁠— <br> “My child, what words are these that pass thy lips? <br> Was not thy long-determined counsel this⁠— <br> That in good time Ulysses should return, <br> To be avenged? Guide, then, Telemachus <br> Wisely⁠—for so thou canst⁠—that, all unharmed, <br> He reach his native land, and, in their barques, <br> Homeward the suitor-train retrace their way.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake, and turned to Hermes, his dear son:⁠— <br> “Hermes⁠—for thou in this my messenger <br> Art, as in all things⁠—to the bright-haired nymph <br> Make known my steadfast purpose⁠—the return <br> Of suffering Ulysses. Neither gods <br> Nor men shall guide his voyage. On a raft, <br> Made firm with bands, he shall depart and reach, <br> After long hardships, on the twentieth day, <br> The fertile shore of Scheria, on whose isle <br> Dwell the Phaeacians, kinsmen of the gods. <br> They like a god shall honor him, and thence <br> Send him to his loved country in a ship, <br> With ample gifts of brass and gold, and store <br> Of raiment⁠—wealth like which he ne’er had brought <br> From conquered Ilion, had he reached his home <br> Safely, with all his portion of the spoil. <br> So is it preordained that he behold <br> His friends again, and stand once more within <br> His high-roofed palace, on his native soil.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake; the herald Argicide obeyed, <br> And hastily beneath his feet he bound <br> The fair, ambrosial golden sandals, worn <br> To bear him over ocean like the wind, <br> And o’er the boundless land. His wand he took, <br> Wherewith he softly seals the eyes of men, <br> And opens them at will from sleep. With this <br> In hand, the mighty Argus-queller flew, <br> And, lighting on Pieria, from the sky <br> Plunged downward to the deep, and skimmed its face <br> Like hovering seamew, that on the broad gulfs <br> Of the unfruitful ocean seeks her prey, <br> And often dips her pinions in the brine; <br> So Hermes flew along the waste of waves.</p>
<p class="verse">But when he reached that island, far away, <br> Forth from the dark-blue ocean-swell he stepped <br> Upon the sea-beach, walking till he came <br> To the vast cave in which the bright-haired nymph <br> Made her abode. He found the nymph within; <br> A fire blazed brightly on the hearth, and far <br> Was wafted o’er the isle the fragrant smoke <br> Of cloven cedar, burning in the flame, <br> And cypress-wood. Meanwhile, in her recess, <br> She sweetly sang, as busily she threw <br> The golden shuttle through the web she wove. <br> And all about the grotto alders grew, <br> And poplars, and sweet-smelling cypresses. <br> In a green forest, high among whose boughs <br> Birds of broad wing, wood-owls, and falcons built <br> Their nests, and crows, with voices sounding far, <br> All haunting for their food the ocean-side. <br> A vine, with downy leaves and clustering grapes, <br> Crept over all the cavern rock. Four springs <br> Poured forth their glittering waters in a row, <br> And here and there went wandering side by side. <br> Around were meadows of soft green, o’ergrown <br> With violets and parsley. ’Twas a spot <br> Where even an immortal might awhile <br> Linger, and gaze with wonder and delight. <br> The herald Argus-queller stood, and saw, <br> And marvelled; but as soon as he had viewed <br> The wonders of the place, he turned his steps, <br> Entering the broad-roofed cave. Calypso there, <br> The glorious goddess, saw him as he came, <br> And knew him; for the ever-living gods <br> Are to each other known, though one may dwell <br> Far from the rest. Ulysses, large of heart, <br> Was not within. Apart, upon the shore, <br> He sat and sorrowed, where he oft in tears <br> And sighs and vain repinings passed the hours, <br> Gazing with wet eyes on the barren deep. <br> Now, placing Hermes on a shining seat <br> Of state, Calypso, glorious goddess, said:</p>
<p class="verse">“Thou of the golden wand, revered and loved, <br> What, Hermes, brings thee hither? Passing few <br> Have been thy visits. Make thy pleasure known. <br> My heart enjoins me to obey, if aught <br> That thou commandest be within my power; <br> But first accept the offerings due a guest.”</p>
<p class="verse">The goddess, speaking thus, before him placed <br> A table, where the heaped ambrosia lay, <br> And mingled the red nectar. Ate and drank <br> The herald Argus-queller, and, refreshed, <br> Answered the nymph, and made his message known:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Art thou a goddess, and dost ask of me, <br> A god, why came I hither? Yet, since thou <br> Requirest, I will truly tell the cause. <br> I came unwillingly, at Jove’s command; <br> For who of choice would traverse the wide waste <br> Of the salt ocean, with no city near <br> Where men adore the gods with solemn rites <br> And chosen hecatombs. No god has power <br> To elude or to resist the purposes <br> Of aegis-bearing Jove. With thee abides, <br> He bids me say, the most unhappy man <br> Of all who round the city of Priam waged <br> The battle through nine years, and, in the tenth, <br> Laying it waste, departed for their homes. <br> But in their voyage they provoked the wrath <br> Of Pallas, who called up the furious winds <br> And angry waves against them. By his side <br> Sank all his gallant comrades in the deep. <br> Him did the winds and waves drive hither. Him <br> Jove bids thee send away with speed; for here <br> He must not perish, far from all he loves. <br> So is it preordained that he behold <br> His friends again, and stand once more within <br> His high-roofed palace, on his native soil.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake; Calypso, glorious goddess, heard, <br> And shuddered, and with winged words replied:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Ye are unjust, ye gods, and, envious far <br> Beyond all other beings, cannot bear <br> That ever goddess openly should make <br> A mortal man her consort. Thus it was <br> When once Aurora, rosy-fingered, took <br> Orion for her husband; ye were stung, <br> Amid your blissful lives, with envious hate, <br> Till chaste Diana, of the golden throne, <br> Smote him with silent arrows from her bow, <br> And slew him in Ortygia. Thus, again, <br> When bright-haired Ceres, swayed by her own heart, <br> In fields which bore three yearly harvests, met <br> Iäsion as a lover, this was known <br> Erelong to Jupiter, who flung from high <br> A flaming thunderbolt, and laid him dead. <br> And now ye envy me, that with me dwells <br> A mortal man. I saved him as he clung <br> Alone upon his floating keel; for Jove <br> Had cloven with a bolt of fire from heaven <br> His galley in the midst of the black sea, <br> And all his gallant comrades perished there. <br> Him kindly I received; I cherished him, <br> And promised him a life that ne’er should know <br> Decay or death. But since no god has power <br> To elude or to withstand the purposes <br> Of aegis-bearing Jove, let him depart⁠— <br> If so the sovereign moves him and commands⁠— <br> Over the barren deep. I send him not; <br> For neither ship arrayed with oars have I, <br> Nor seamen, o’er the boundless waste of waves <br> To bear him hence. My counsel I will give, <br> And nothing will I hide that he should know, <br> To place him safely on his native shore.”</p>
<p class="verse">The herald Argus-queller answered her:⁠— <br> “Dismiss him thus, and bear in mind the wrath <br> Of Jove, lest it be kindled against thee.”</p>
<p class="verse">Thus having said, the mighty Argicide <br> Departed; and the nymph, who now had heard <br> The doom of Jove, sought the greathearted man, <br> Ulysses. Him she found beside the deep, <br> Seated alone, with eyes from which the tears <br> Were never dried; for now no more the nymph <br> Delighted him; he wasted his sweet life <br> In yearning for his home. Night after night <br> He slept constrained within the hollow cave, <br> The unwilling by the fond; and day by day <br> He sat upon the rocks that edged the shore, <br> And in continual weeping and in sighs <br> And vain repinings wore the hours away, <br> Gazing through tears upon the barren deep. <br> The glorious goddess stood by him and spake:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Unhappy! sit no longer sorrowing here, <br> Nor waste life thus. Lo! I most willingly <br> Dismiss thee hence. Rise, hew down trees, and bind <br> Their trunks with brazen clamps into a raft, <br> And fasten planks above, a lofty floor, <br> That it may bear thee o’er the dark-blue deep. <br> Bread will I put on board, water, and wine⁠— <br> Red wine, that cheers the heart⁠—and wrap thee well <br> In garments, and send after thee the wind, <br> That safely thou attain thy native shore, <br> If so the gods permit thee, who abide <br> In the broad heaven above, and better know <br> By far than I, and far more wisely judge.”</p>
<p class="verse">Ulysses, the great sufferer, as she spake <br> Shuddered, and thus with winged words replied:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Some other purpose than to send me home <br> Is in thy heart, O goddess, bidding me <br> To cross this frightful sea upon a raft⁠— <br> This perilous sea, where never even ships <br> Pass with their rapid keels, though Jove bestow <br> The wind that glads the seaman. Nay, I climb <br> No raft, against thy wish, unless thou swear <br> The great oath of the gods that thou in this <br> Dost meditate no other harm to me.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake; Calypso, glorious goddess, smiled, <br> And smoothed his forehead with her hand, and said:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Perverse, and slow to see where guile is not! <br> How could thy heart permit thee thus to speak? <br> Now bear me witness, Earth, and ye broad Heavens <br> Above us, and ye waters of the Styx <br> That flow beneath us, mightiest oath of all, <br> And most revered by all the blessed gods, <br> That I design no other harm to thee, <br> But that I plan for thee, and counsel thee <br> What I would do were I in need like thine. <br> I bear a juster mind; my bosom holds <br> A pitying heart, and not a heart of steel.”</p>
<p class="verse">Thus having said, the glorious goddess moved <br> Away with hasty steps, and where she trod <br> He followed, till they reached the vaulted cave⁠— <br> The goddess and the hero. There he took <br> The seat whence Hermes had just risen. The nymph <br> Brought forth whatever mortals eat and drink <br> To set before him. She right opposite <br> To that of great Ulysses took her seat. <br> Ambrosia there her maidens laid, and there <br> Poured nectar. Both put forth their hands, and took <br> The ready viands, till at length the calls <br> Of hunger and of thirst were satisfied; <br> Calypso, glorious goddess, then began:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Son of Laertes, man of many wiles, <br> Highborn Ulysses! thus wilt thou depart <br> Home to thy native country? Then farewell; <br> But, couldst thou know the sufferings Fate ordains <br> For thee ere yet thou landest on its shore, <br> Thou wouldst remain to keep this home with me <br> And be immortal, strong as is thy wish <br> To see thy wife⁠—a wish that day by day <br> Possesses thee. I cannot deem myself <br> In face or form less beautiful than she; <br> For never with immortals can the race <br> Of mortal dames in form or face compare.”</p>
<p>Ulysses, the sagacious, answered her:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Bear with me, gracious goddess; well I know <br> All thou couldst say. The sage Penelope <br> In feature and in stature comes not nigh <br> To thee, for she is mortal⁠—deathless thou, <br> And ever young; yet day by day I long <br> To be at home once more, and pine to see <br> The hour of my return. Even though some god <br> Smite me on the black ocean, I shall bear <br> The stroke, for in my bosom dwells a mind <br> Patient of suffering; much have I endured, <br> And much survived, in tempests on the deep, <br> And in the battle; let this happen too.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake; the sun went down; the night came on; <br> And now the twain withdrew to a recess <br> Deep in the vaulted cave, where, side by side, <br> They took their rest. But when the child of Dawn, <br> Aurora, rosy-fingered, looked abroad, <br> Ulysses put his vest and mantle on; <br> The nymph too, in a robe of silver-white, <br> Ample, and delicate, and beautiful, <br> Arrayed herself, and round about her loins <br> Wound a fair golden girdle, drew a veil <br> Over her head, and planned to send away <br> Magnanimous Ulysses. She bestowed <br> A heavy axe, of steel and double-edged, <br> Well fitted to the hand, the handle wrought <br> Of olive-wood, firm set and beautiful. <br> A polished adze she gave him next, and led <br> The way to a far corner of the isle, <br> Where lofty trees, alders and poplars, stood, <br> And firs that reached the clouds, sapless and dry <br> Long since, and fitter thus to ride the waves. <br> Then, having shown where grew the tallest trees, <br> Calypso, glorious goddess, sought her home.</p>
<p class="verse">Trees then he felled, and soon the task was done. <br> Twenty in all he brought to earth, and squared <br> Their trunks with the sharp steel, and carefully <br> He smoothed their sides, and wrought them by a line. <br> Calypso, gracious goddess, having brought <br> Wimbles, he bored the beams, and, fitting them <br> Together, made them fast with nails and clamps. <br> As when some builder, skilful in his art, <br> Frames for a ship of burden the broad keel, <br> Such ample breadth Ulysses gave the raft. <br> Upon the massy beams he reared a deck, <br> And floored it with long planks from end to end. <br> On this a mast he raised, and to the mast <br> Fitted a yard; he shaped a rudder next, <br> To guide the raft along her course, and round <br> With woven work of willow-boughs he fenced <br> Her sides against the dashings of the sea. <br> Calypso, gracious goddess, brought him store <br> Of canvas, which he fitly shaped to sails, <br> And, rigging her with cords and ropes and stays, <br> Heaved her with levers into the great deep.</p>
<p class="verse">’Twas the fourth day. His labors now were done, <br> And on the fifth the goddess from her isle <br> Dismissed him, newly from the bath, arrayed <br> In garments given by her, that shed perfumes. <br> A skin of dark red wine she put on board, <br> A larger one of water, and for food <br> A basket, stored with viands such as please <br> The appetite. A friendly wind and soft <br> She sent before. The great Ulysses spread <br> His canvas joyfully to catch the breeze, <br> And sat and guided with nice care the helm, <br> Gazing with fixed eye on the Pleiades, <br> Boötes setting late, and the Great Bear, <br> By others called the Wain, which, wheeling round, <br> Looks ever toward Orion, and alone <br> Dips not into the waters of the deep. <br> For so Calypso, glorious goddess, bade <br> That on his ocean journey he should keep <br> That constellation ever on his left. <br> Now seventeen days were in the voyage past, <br> And on the eighteenth shadowy heights appeared, <br> The nearest point of the Phaeacian land, <br> Lying on the dark ocean like a shield.</p>
<p class="verse">But mighty Neptune, coming from among <br> The Ethiopians, saw him. Far away <br> He saw, from mountain-heights of Solyma, <br> The voyager, and burned with fiercer wrath, <br> And shook his head, and said within himself:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Strange! now I see the gods have new designs <br> For this Ulysses, formed while I was yet <br> In Ethiopia. He draws near the land <br> Of the Phaeacians, where it is decreed <br> He shall o’erpass the boundary of his woes; <br> But first, I think, he will have much to bear.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake, and round about him called the clouds <br> And roused the ocean⁠—wielding in his hand <br> The trident⁠—summoned all the hurricanes <br> Of all the winds, and covered earth and sky <br> At once with mists, while from above the night <br> Fell suddenly. The east wind and the south <br> Pushed forth at once, with the strong-blowing west, <br> And the clear north rolled up his mighty waves. <br> Ulysses trembled in his knees and heart, <br> And thus to his great soul, lamenting, said:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“What will become of me? unhappy man! <br> I fear that all the goddess said was true, <br> Foretelling what disasters should o’ertake <br> My voyage ere I reach my native land. <br> Now are her words fulfilled. How Jupiter <br> Wraps the great heaven in clouds and stirs the deep <br> To tumult! Wilder grow the hurricanes <br> Of all the winds, and now my fate is sure. <br> Thrice happy, four times happy, they who fell <br> On Troy’s wide field, warring for Atreus’ sons: <br> O, had I met my fate and perished there, <br> That very day on which the Trojan host, <br> Around the dead Achilles, hurled at me <br> Their brazen javelins, I had then received <br> Due burial, and great glory with the Greeks; <br> Now must I die a miserable death.”</p>
<p class="verse">As thus he spake, upon him, from on high, <br> A huge and frightful billow broke; it whirled <br> The raft around, and far from it he fell. <br> His hands let go the rudder; a fierce rush <br> Of all the winds together snapped in twain <br> The mast; far off the yard and canvas flew <br> Into the deep; the billow held him long <br> Beneath the waters, and he strove in vain <br> Quickly to rise to air from that huge swell <br> Of ocean, for the garments weighed him down <br> Which fair Calypso gave him. But at length <br> Emerging, he rejected from his throat <br> The bitter brine that down his forehead streamed. <br> Even then, though hopeless with dismay, his thought <br> Was on the raft; and, struggling through the waves, <br> He seized it, sprang on board, and, seated there, <br> Escaped the threatened death. Still to and fro <br> The rolling billows drave it. As the wind <br> In autumn sweeps the thistles o’er the field, <br> Clinging together, so the blasts of heaven <br> Hither and thither drove it o’er the sea. <br> And now the south wind flung it to the north <br> To buffet; now the east wind to the west.</p>
<p class="verse">Ino Leucothea saw him clinging there⁠— <br> The delicate-footed child of Cadmus, once <br> A mortal, speaking with a mortal voice, <br> Though now within the ocean gulfs she shares <br> The honors of the gods. With pity she <br> Beheld Ulysses struggling thus distressed, <br> And, rising from the abyss below, in form <br> A cormorant, the sea-nymph took her perch <br> On the well-banded raft, and thus she said:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Ah, luckless man! how hast thou angered thus <br> Earthshaking Neptune, that he visits thee <br> With these disasters? Yet he cannot take, <br> Although he seek it earnestly, thy life. <br> Now do my bidding, for thou seemest wise. <br> Laying aside thy garments, let the raft <br> Drift with the winds, while thou, by strength of arm, <br> Makest thy way in swimming to the land <br> Of the Phaeacians, where thy safety lies. <br> Receive this veil, and bind its heavenly woof <br> Beneath thy breast, and have no further fear <br> Of hardship or of danger. But, as soon <br> As thou shalt touch the island, take it off, <br> And turn away thy face, and fling it far <br> From where thou standest into the black deep.”</p>
<p class="verse">The goddess gave the veil as thus she spoke, <br> And to the tossing deep went down, in form <br> A cormorant; the black wave covered her. <br> But still Ulysses, mighty sufferer, <br> Pondered, and thus to his great soul he said:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Ah me! perhaps some god is planning here <br> Some other fraud against me, bidding me <br> Forsake my raft. I will not yet obey, <br> For still far off I see the land in which <br> ’Tis said my refuge lies. This will I do, <br> For this seems wisest. While the fastenings last <br> That hold these timbers, I will keep my place <br> And bide the tempest here; but when the waves <br> Shall dash my raft in pieces, I will swim, <br> For nothing better will remain to do.”</p>
<p class="verse">As he revolved this purpose in his mind, <br> Earthshaking Neptune sent a mighty wave, <br> Horrid and huge and high, and where he sat <br> It smote him. As a violent wind uplifts <br> The dry chaff heaped upon a threshing-floor, <br> And sends it scattered through the air abroad, <br> So did that wave fling loose the ponderous beams. <br> To one of these, Ulysses, clinging fast, <br> Bestrode it, like a horseman on his steed; <br> And now he took the garments off, bestowed <br> By fair Calypso, binding round his breast <br> The veil, and forward plunged into the deep, <br> With palms outspread, prepared to swim. Meanwhile <br> Neptune beheld him⁠—Neptune, mighty king⁠— <br> And shook his head, and said within himself:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Go thus, and laden with mischances roam <br> The waters till thou come among the race <br> Cherished by Jupiter, but well I deem <br> Thou wilt not find thy share of suffering light.”</p>
<p class="verse">Thus having said he urged his coursers on, <br> With their fair-flowing manes, until he came <br> To Aegae, where his glorious palace stands.</p>
<p class="verse">But Pallas, child of Jove, had other thoughts. <br> She stayed the course of every wind beside, <br> And bade them rest, and lulled them into sleep, <br> But summoned the swift north to break the waves, <br> That so Ulysses, the highborn, escaped <br> From death and from the fates, might be the guest <br> Of the Phaeacians⁠—men who love the sea. <br> Two days and nights among the mighty waves <br> He floated, oft his heart foreboding death. <br> But when the bright-haired Eos had fulfilled <br> The third day’s course, and all the winds were laid, <br> And calm was on the watery waste, he saw <br> That land was near, as, lifted on the crest <br> Of a huge swell, he looked with sharpened sight; <br> And as a father’s life preserved makes glad <br> His children’s hearts, when long time he has lain <br> Sick, wrung with pain, and wasting by the power <br> Of some malignant genius, till at length <br> The gracious gods bestow a welcome cure, <br> So welcome to Ulysses was the sight <br> Of woods and fields. By swimming on he thought <br> To climb and tread the shore; but when he drew <br> So near that one who shouted could be heard <br> From land, the sound of ocean on the rocks <br> Came to his ear⁠—for there huge breakers roared <br> And spouted fearfully, and all around <br> Was covered with the sea-foam. Haven here <br> Was none for ships, nor sheltering creek, but shores <br> Beetling from high, and crags and walls of rock. <br> Ulysses trembled both in knees and heart, <br> And thus to his great soul, lamenting, said:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Now woe is me! as soon as Jove has shown <br> What I had little hoped to see, the land, <br> And I through all these waves have ploughed my way, <br> I find no issue from the hoary deep. <br> For sharp rocks border it, and all around <br> Roar the wild surges; slippery cliffs arise <br> Close to deep gulfs, and footing there is none <br> Where I might plant my steps and thus escape. <br> All effort now were fruitless to resist <br> The mighty billow hurrying me away <br> To dash me on the pointed rocks. If yet <br> I strive, by swimming further, to descry <br> Some sloping shore or harbor of the isle, <br> I fear the tempest, lest it hurl me back, <br> Heavily groaning, to the fishy deep; <br> Or huge sea-monster, from the multitude <br> Which sovereign Amphitritè feeds, be sent <br> Against me by some god⁠—for well I know <br> The power who shakes the shores is wroth with me.”</p>
<p class="verse">While he revolved these doubts within his mind, <br> A huge wave hurled him toward the rugged coast. <br> Then had his limbs been flayed, and all his bones <br> Broken at once, had not the blue-eyed maid, <br> Minerva, prompted him. Borne toward the rock, <br> He clutched it instantly with both his hands, <br> And panting clung till that huge wave rolled by, <br> And so escaped its fury. Back it came, <br> And smote him once again, and flung him far <br> Seaward. As to the claws of Polypus, <br> Plucked from its bed, the pebbles thickly cling, <br> So flakes of skin, from off his powerful hands, <br> Were left upon the rock. The mighty surge <br> O’erwhelmed him; he had perished ere his time⁠— <br> Hapless Ulysses!⁠—but the blue-eyed maid, <br> Pallas, informed his mind with forecast. Straight <br> Emerging from the wave that shoreward rolled, <br> He swam along the coast and eyed it well, <br> In hope of sloping beach or sheltered creek. <br> But when, in swimming, he had reached the mouth <br> Of a soft-flowing river, here appeared <br> The spot he wished for, smooth, without a rock, <br> And here was shelter from the wind. He felt <br> The current’s flow, and thus devoutly prayed:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Hear me, O sovereign power, whoe’er thou art! <br> To thee, the long-desired, I come. I seek <br> Escape from Neptune’s threatenings on the sea. <br> The deathless gods respect the prayer of him <br> Who looks to them for help, a fugitive, <br> As I am now, when to thy stream I come, <br> And to thy knees, from many a hardship past. <br> O thou that here art ruler, I declare <br> Myself thy suppliant; be thou merciful.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spoke: the river stayed his current, checked <br> The billows, smoothed them to a calm, and gave <br> The swimmer a safe landing at his mouth. <br> Then dropped his knees and sinewy arms at once, <br> Unstrung, for faint with struggling was his heart. <br> His body was all swol’n; the brine gushed forth <br> From mouth and nostrils; all unnerved he lay, <br> Breathless and speechless; utter weariness <br> O’ermastered him. But when he breathed again, <br> And his flown senses had returned, he loosed <br> The veil that Ino gave him from his breast, <br> And to the salt flood cast it. A great wave <br> Bore it far down the stream; the goddess there <br> In her own hands received it. He, meanwhile, <br> Withdrawing from the brink, lay down among <br> The reeds, and kissed the harvest-bearing earth, <br> And thus to his great soul, lamenting, said:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Ah me! what must I suffer more? what yet <br> Will happen to me? If by the river’s side <br> I pass the unfriendly watches of the night, <br> The cruel cold and dews that steep the bank <br> May, in this weakness, end me utterly, <br> For chilly blows this river-air at dawn; <br> But should I climb this hill, to sleep within <br> The shadowy wood, among thick shrubs, if cold <br> And weariness allow me, then I fear, <br> That, while the pleasant slumbers o’er me steal, <br> I may become the prey of savage beasts.”</p>
<p class="verse">Yet, as he longer pondered, this seemed best. <br> He rose, and sought the wood, and found it near <br> The water, on a height, o’erlooking far <br> The region round. Between two shrubs that sprang <br> Both from one spot he entered⁠—olive-trees, <br> One wild, one fruitful. The damp-blowing wind <br> Ne’er pierced their covert; never blazing sun <br> Darted his beams within, nor pelting shower <br> Beat through, so closely intertwined they grew. <br> Here entering, Ulysses heaped a bed <br> Of leaves with his own hands; he made it broad <br> And high, for thick the leaves had fallen around. <br> Two men and three, in that abundant store, <br> Might bide the winter storm, though keen the cold. <br> Ulysses, the great sufferer, on his couch <br> Looked and rejoiced, and placed himself within, <br> And heaped the leaves high o’er him and around, <br> As one who, dwelling in the distant fields, <br> Without a neighbor near him, hides a brand <br> In the dark ashes, keeping carefully <br> The seeds of fire alive, lest he, perforce, <br> To light his hearth must bring them from afar; <br> So did Ulysses in that pile of leaves <br> Bury himself, while Pallas o’er his eyes <br> Poured sleep, and closed his lids, that he might take, <br> After his painful toils, the fitting rest.</p>
