<p class="lede">A general council of the Phaeacians, in which it is determined to send Ulysses home to Ithaca⁠—A solemn feast⁠—Lay of the Trojan War, sung by Demodocus, the minstrel⁠—Public games⁠—Ulysses conquers in throwing the discus⁠—The amour of Mars and Venus sung by Demodocus⁠—His song of the Trojan horse and the fall of Troy.</p>
<p class="verse">When Morn appeared, the rosy-fingered child <br> Of Dawn, Alcinoüs, mighty and revered, <br> Rose from his bed. Ulysses, noble chief, <br> Spoiler of cities, also left his couch. <br> Alcinoüs, mighty and revered, went forth <br> Before, and led him to the marketplace <br> Of the Phaeacians, built beside the fleet, <br> And there on polished stones they took their seats <br> Near to each other. Pallas, who now seemed <br> A herald of the wise Alcinoüs, went <br> Through all the city, planning how to send <br> Magnanimous Ulysses to his home, <br> And came and stood by every chief and said:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Leaders and chiefs of the Phaeacians, come <br> Speedily to the marketplace, and there <br> Hear of the stranger who from wandering o’er <br> The deep has come where wise Alcinoüs holds <br> His court; in aspect he is like the gods.”</p>
<p class="verse">She spake, and every mind and heart was moved, <br> And all the marketplace and all its seats <br> Were quickly filled with people. Many gazed, <br> Admiring, on Laertes’ well-graced son; <br> For on his face and form had Pallas shed <br> A glory, and had made him seem more tall <br> And of an ampler bulk, that he might find <br> Favor with the Phaeacians, and be deemed <br> Worthy of awe and able to achieve <br> The many feats which the Phaeacian chiefs, <br> To try the stranger’s prowess, might propose.</p>
<p class="verse">And now when all the summoned had arrived, <br> Alcinoüs to the full assembly spake:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Princes and chiefs of the Phaeacians, hear: <br> I speak the promptings of my heart. This guest⁠— <br> I know him not⁠—has come to my abode, <br> A wanderer⁠—haply from the tribes who dwell <br> In the far East, or haply from the West⁠— <br> And asked an escort and safe-conduct home; <br> And let us make them ready, as our wont <br> Has ever been. No stranger ever comes <br> Across my threshold who is suffered long <br> To pine for his departure. Let us draw <br> A dark-hulled ship down to the holy sea <br> On her first voyage. Let us choose her crew <br> Among the people, two-and-fifty youths <br> Of our best seamen. Then make fast the oars <br> Beside the benches, leave them there, and come <br> Into our palace and partake in haste <br> A feast which I will liberally spread <br> For all of you. This I command the youths; <br> But you, ye sceptred princes, come at once <br> To my fair palace, that we there may pay <br> The honors due our guest; let none refuse. <br> Call also the divine Demodocus, <br> The bard, on whom a deity bestowed <br> In ample measure the sweet gift of song, <br> Delightful when the spirit prompts the lay.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake, and led the way; the sceptred train <br> Of princes followed him. The herald sought <br> Meantime the sacred bard. The chosen youths <br> Fifty-and-two betook them to the marge <br> Of the unfruitful sea; and when they reached <br> The ship and beach they drew the dark hull down <br> To the deep water, put the mast on board <br> And the ship’s sails, and fitted well the oars <br> Into the leathern rings, and, having moored <br> Their barque in the deep water, went with speed <br> To their wise monarch in his spacious halls. <br> There portico and court and hall were thronged <br> With people, young and old in multitude; <br> And there Alcinoüs sacrificed twelve sheep, <br> Eight white-toothed swine, and two splayfooted beeves. <br> And these they flayed, and duly dressed, and made <br> A noble banquet ready. Then appeared <br> The herald, leading the sweet singer in, <br> Him whom the Muse with an exceeding love <br> Had cherished, and had visited with good <br> And evil, quenched his eyesight and bestowed <br> Sweetness of song. Pontonoüs mid the guests <br> Placed for the bard a silver-studded throne, <br> Against a lofty column hung his harp <br> Above his head, and taught him how to find <br> And take it down. Near him the herald set <br> A basket and fair table, and a cup <br> Of wine, that he might drink when he desired; <br> Then all put forth their hands and shared the feast.</p>
<p class="verse">And when their thirst and hunger were allayed, <br> The Muse inspired the bard to sing the praise <br> Of heroes; ’twas a song whose fame had reached <br> To the high heaven, a story of the strife <br> Between Ulysses and Achilles, son <br> Of Peleus, wrangling at a solemn feast <br> Made for the gods. They strove with angry words, <br> And Agamemnon, king of men, rejoiced <br> To hear the noblest of the Achaian host <br> Contending; for all this had been foretold <br> To him in sacred Pythia by the voice <br> Of Phoebus, when the monarch to inquire <br> At the oracle had crossed the rock which formed <br> Its threshold. Then began the train of woes <br> Which at the will of sovereign Jupiter <br> Befell the sons of Ilium and of Greece.</p>
<p class="verse">So sang renowned Demodocus. Meanwhile <br> Ulysses took into his brawny hands <br> An ample veil of purple, drawing it <br> Around his head to hide his noble face, <br> Ashamed that the Phaeacians should behold <br> The tears that flowed so freely from his lids. <br> But when the sacred bard had ceased his song, <br> He wiped the tears away and laid the veil <br> Aside, and took a double beaker filled <br> With wine, and poured libations to the gods. <br> Yet when again the minstrel sang, and all <br> The chiefs of the Phaeacian people, charmed <br> To hear his music, bade the strain proceed, <br> Again Ulysses hid his face and wept. <br> No other eye beheld the tears he shed. <br> Alcinoüs only watched him, and perceived <br> His grief, and heard the sighs he drew, and spake <br> To the Phaeacians, lovers of the sea:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Now that we all, to our content, have shared <br> The feast and heard the harp, whose notes so well <br> Suit with a liberal banquet, let us forth <br> And try our skill in games, that this our guest, <br> Returning to his country, may relate <br> How in the boxing and the wrestling match, <br> In leaping and in running, we excel.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake, and went before; they followed him. <br> Then did the herald hang the clear-toned harp <br> Again on high, and taking by the hand <br> Demodocus, he led him from the place, <br> Guiding him in the way which just before <br> The princes of Phaeacia trod to see <br> The public games. Into the marketplace <br> They went; a vast innumerable crowd <br> Pressed after. Then did many a valiant youth <br> Arise⁠—Acroneus and Ocyalus, <br> Elatreus, Nauteus, Prymneus, after whom <br> Upstood Anchialus, and by his side <br> Eretmeus, Ponteus, Proreus, Thoön, rose; <br> Anabasineüs and Amphialus, <br> A son of Polyneius, Tecton’s son; <br> Then rose the son of Naubolus, like Mars <br> In warlike port, Euryalus by name, <br> And goodliest both in feature and in form <br> Of all Phaeacia’s sons save one alone, <br> Laodamas the faultless. Next three sons <br> Of King Alcinoüs rose: Laodamas, <br> Halius, and Clytoneius, like a god <br> In aspect. Some of these began the games, <br> Contending in the race. For them a course <br> Was marked from goal to goal. They darted forth <br> At once and swiftly, raising, as they ran, <br> The dust along the plain. The swiftest there <br> Was Clytoneius in the race. As far <br> As mules, in furrowing the fallow ground, <br> Gain on the steers, he ran before the rest, <br> And reached the crowd, and left them all behind. <br> Others in wrestling strove laboriously⁠— <br> And here Euryalus excelled them all; <br> But in the leap Amphialus was first; <br> Elatreus flung the quoit with firmest hand; <br> And in the boxer’s art Laodamas, <br> The monarch’s valiant son, was conqueror.</p>
<p class="verse">This when the admiring multitude had seen, <br> Thus spake the monarch’s son, Laodamas:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“And now, my friends, inquire we of our guest <br> If he has learned and practised feats like these. <br> For he is not ill-made in legs and thighs <br> And in both arms, in firmly planted neck <br> And strong-built frame; nor does he seem to lack <br> A certain youthful vigor, though impaired <br> By many hardships⁠—for I know of naught <br> That more severely tries the strongest man, <br> And breaks him down, than perils of the sea.”</p>
<p class="verse">Euryalus replied: “Laodamas, <br> Well hast thou said, and rightly: go thou now <br> And speak to him thyself, and challenge him.”</p>
<p class="verse">The son of King Alcinoüs, as he heard, <br> Came forward, and bespake Ulysses thus:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Thou also, guest and father, try these feats, <br> If thou perchance wert trained to them. I think <br> Thou must be skilled in games, since there is not <br> A greater glory for a man while yet <br> He lives on earth than what he hath wrought out, <br> By strenuous effort, with his feet and hands. <br> Try, then, thy skill, and give no place to grief. <br> Not long will thy departure be delayed; <br> Thy barque is launched; the crew are ready here.”</p>
<p class="verse">Ulysses, the sagacious, answered thus:⁠— <br> “Why press me, O Laodamas! to try <br> These feats, when all my thoughts are of my woes, <br> And not of games? I, who have borne so much <br> Of pain and toil, sit pining for my home <br> In your assembly, supplicating here <br> Your king and all the people of your land.”</p>
<p class="verse">Then spake Euryalus with chiding words:⁠— <br> “Stranger, I well perceive thou canst not boast, <br> As many others can, of skill in games; <br> But thou art one of those who dwell in ships <br> With many benches, rulers o’er a crew <br> Of sailors⁠—a mere trader looking out <br> For freight, and watching o’er the wares that form <br> The cargo. Thou hast doubtless gathered wealth <br> By rapine, and art surely no athlete.”</p>
<p class="verse">Ulysses, the sagacious, frowned and said:⁠— <br> “Stranger, thou speakest not becomingly, <br> But like a man who recks not what he says. <br> The gods bestow not equally on all <br> The gifts that men desire⁠—the grace of form, <br> The mind, the eloquence. One man to sight <br> Is undistinguished, but on him the gods <br> Bestow the power of words. All look on him <br> Gladly; he knows whereof he speaks; his speech <br> Is mild and modest; he is eminent <br> In all assemblies, and, whene’er he walks <br> The city, men regard him as a god. <br> Another in the form he wears is like <br> The immortals, yet has he no power to speak <br> Becoming words. So thou hast comely looks⁠— <br> A god would not have shaped thee otherwise <br> Than we behold thee⁠—yet thy wit is small, <br> And thy unmannerly words have angered me <br> Even to the heart. Not quite unskilled am I <br> In games, as thou dost idly talk, and once, <br> When I could trust my youth and my strong arms, <br> I think that in these contests I was deemed <br> Among the first. But I am now pressed down <br> With toil and sorrow; much have I endured <br> In wars with heroes and on stormy seas. <br> Yet even thus, a sufferer as I am, <br> Will I essay these feats; for sharp have been <br> Thy words, and they provoke me to the proof.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake, and rising with his mantle on <br> He seized a broader, thicker, heavier quoit, <br> By no small odds, than the Phaeacians used, <br> And swinging it around with vigorous arm <br> He sent it forth; it sounded as it went; <br> And the Phaeacians, skilful with the oar <br> And sail, bent low as o’er them, from his hand, <br> Flew the swift stone beyond the other marks. <br> And Pallas, in a human form, set up <br> A mark where it descended, and exclaimed:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Stranger! a blind man, groping here, could find <br> Thy mark full easily, since it is not <br> Among the many, but beyond them all. <br> Then fear thou nothing in this game at least; <br> For no Phaeacian here can throw the quoit <br> As far as thou, much less exceed thy cast.”</p>
<p class="verse">She spake; Ulysses the great sufferer <br> Heard, and rejoiced to know he had a friend <br> In that great circle. With a lighter heart <br> Thus said the chief to the Phaeacian crowd:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Follow that cast, young men, and I will send <br> Another stone, at once, as far, perchance, <br> Or further still. If there are others yet <br> Who feel the wish, let them come forward here⁠— <br> For much your words have chafed me⁠—let them try <br> With me the boxing or the wrestling match, <br> Or footrace; there is naught that I refuse⁠— <br> Any of the Phaeacians. I except <br> Laodamas; he is my host, and who <br> Would enter such a contest with a friend? <br> A senseless, worthless man is he who seeks <br> A strife like this with one who shelters him <br> In a strange land; he mars the welcome given. <br> As for the rest, there is no rival here <br> Whom I reject or scorn; for I would know <br> Their prowess, and would try my own with theirs <br> Before you all. At any of the games <br> Practised among mankind I am not ill, <br> Whatever they may be. The polished bow <br> I well know how to handle. I should be <br> The first to strike a foe by arrows sent <br> Among a hostile squadron, though there stood <br> A crowd of fellow-warriors by my side <br> And also aimed their shafts. The only one <br> Whose skill in archery excelled my own, <br> When we Achaians drew the bow at Troy, <br> Was Philoctetes; to all other men <br> On earth that live by bread I hold myself <br> Superior. Yet I claim no rivalry <br> With men of ancient times⁠—with Hercules <br> And Eurytus the Oechalian, who defied <br> The immortals to a contest with the bow. <br> Therefore was mighty Eurytus cut off. <br> Apollo, angry to be challenged, slew <br> The hero. I can hurl a spear beyond <br> Where others send an arrow. All my fear <br> Is for my feet, so weakened have I been <br> Among the stormy waves with want of food <br> At sea, and thus my limbs have lost their strength.”</p>
<p class="verse">He ended here, and all the assembly sat <br> In silence; King Alcinoüs only spake:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Stranger, since thou dost speak without offence, <br> And but to assert the prowess of thine arm, <br> Indignant that amid the public games <br> This man should rail at thee, and since thy wish <br> Is only that all others who can speak <br> Becomingly may not in time to come <br> Dispraise that prowess, now, then, heed my words, <br> And speak of them within thy palace halls <br> To other heroes when thou banquetest <br> Beside thy wife and children, and dost think <br> Of things that we excel in⁠—arts which Jove <br> Gives us, transmitted from our ancestors. <br> In boxing and in wrestling small renown <br> Have we, but we are swift of foot; we guide <br> Our galleys bravely o’er the deep; we take <br> Delight in feasts; we love the harp, the dance, <br> And change of raiment, the warm bath and bed. <br> Rise, then, Phaeacian masters of the dance, <br> And tread your measures, that our guest may tell <br> His friends at home how greatly we surpass <br> All other men in seamanship, the race, <br> The dance, the art of song. Go, one of you, <br> And bring Demodocus his clear-toned harp, <br> That somewhere in our palace has been left.”</p>
<p class="verse">Thus spake the godlike king. The herald rose <br> To bring the sweet harp from the royal house. <br> Then the nine umpires also rose, who ruled <br> The games; they smoothed the floor, and made the ring <br> Of gazers wider. Next the herald came, <br> And brought Demodocus the clear-toned harp. <br> The minstrel went into the midst, and there <br> Gathered the graceful dancers; they were youths <br> In life’s first bloom. With even steps they smote <br> The sacred floor. Ulysses, gazing, saw <br> The twinkle of their feet and was amazed. <br> The minstrel struck the chords and gracefully <br> Began the lay: he sang the loves of Mars <br> And Venus of the glittering crown, who first <br> Had met each other stealthily beneath <br> The roof of Vulcan. Mars with many gifts <br> Won her, and wronged her spouse, the King of Fire; <br> But from the Sun, who saw their guilt, there came <br> A messenger to Vulcan. When he heard <br> The unwelcome tidings, planning his revenge, <br> He hastened to his smithy, where he forged <br> Chains that no power might loosen or might break, <br> Made to hold fast forever. When the snare <br> In all its parts was finished, he repaired, <br> Angry with Mars, to where the marriage-bed <br> Stood in his chamber. To the posts he tied <br> The encircling chains on every side, and made <br> Fast to the ceiling many, like the threads <br> Spun by the spider, which no eye could see, <br> Not even of the gods, so artfully <br> He wrought them. Then, as soon as he had wrapped <br> The snare about the bed, he feigned to go <br> To Lemnos nobly built, most dear to him <br> Of all the lands. But Mars, the god who holds <br> The shining reins, had kept no careless watch, <br> And when he saw the great artificer <br> Depart he went with speed to Vulcan’s house, <br> Drawn thither by the love of her who wears <br> The glittering crown. There Cytherea sat, <br> Arrived that moment from a visit paid. <br> Entering, he took her by the hand and said:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Come, my beloved, let us to the couch. <br> Vulcan is here no longer; he is gone, <br> And is among the Sintians, men who speak <br> A barbarous tongue, in Lemnos far away.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake, and she approved his words, and both <br> Lay down upon the bed, when suddenly <br> The network, wrought by Vulcan’s skilful hand, <br> Caught them, and clasped them round, nor could they lift <br> Or move a limb, and saw that no escape <br> Was possible. And now approached the King <br> Of Fire, returning ere he reached the isle <br> Of Lemnos, for the Sun in his behalf <br> Kept watch and told him all. He hastened home <br> In bitterness of heart, but when he reached <br> The threshold stopped. A fury without bounds <br> Possessed him, and he shouted terribly, <br> And called aloud on all the gods of heaven:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“O Father Jove, and all ye blessed ones, <br> And deathless! Come, for here is what will move <br> Your laughter, yet is not to be endured. <br> Jove’s daughter, Venus, thus dishonors me, <br> Lame as I am, and loves the butcher Mars; <br> For he is well to look at, and is sound <br> Of foot, while I am weakly⁠—but for this <br> Are none but my two parents to be blamed, <br> Who never should have given me birth. Behold <br> Where lie embraced the lovers in my bed⁠— <br> A hateful sight. Yet they will hardly take <br> Even a short slumber there, though side by side, <br> Enamored as they are; nor will they both <br> Be drowsy very soon. The net and chains <br> Will hold them till her father shall restore <br> All the large gifts which, on our marriage-day, <br> I gave him to possess the impudent minx <br> His daughter, who is fair, indeed, but false.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake, and to the brazen palace flocked <br> The gods; there Neptune came, who shakes the earth; <br> There came beneficent Hermes; there too came <br> Apollo, archer-god; the goddesses, <br> Through womanly reserve, remained at home. <br> Meantime the gods, the givers of all good, <br> Stood in the entrance; and as they beheld <br> The cunning snare of Vulcan, there arose <br> Infinite laughter from the blessed ones, <br> And one of them bespake his neighbor thus:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Wrong prospers not; the slow o’ertakes the swift. <br> Vulcan the slow has trapped the fleetest god <br> Upon Olympus, Mars; though lame himself, <br> His net has taken the adulterer, <br> Who now must pay the forfeit of his crime.”</p>
<p class="verse">So talked they with each other. Then the son <br> Of Jove, Apollo, thus to Hermes said:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Hermes, thou son and messenger of Jove, <br> And bountiful of gifts, couldst thou endure, <br> Fettered with such strong chains as these, to lie <br> Upon a couch with Venus at thy side?”</p>
<p class="verse">The herald-god, the Argus-queller, thus <br> Made answer: “Nay, I would that it were so, <br> O archer-king, Apollo; I could bear <br> Chains thrice as many, and of infinite strength, <br> And all the gods and all the goddesses <br> Might come to look upon me, I would keep <br> My place with golden Venus at my side.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake, and all the immortals laughed to hear. <br> Neptune alone laughed not, but earnestly <br> Prayed Vulcan, the renowned artificer, <br> To set Mars free, and spake these winged words:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Release thy prisoner. What thou dost require <br> I promise here⁠—that he shall make to thee <br> Due recompense in presence of the gods.”</p>
<p class="verse">Illustrious Vulcan answered: “Do not lay, <br> Earthshaking Neptune, this command on me, <br> Since little is the worth of pledges given <br> For worthless debtors. How could I demand <br> My right from thee among the assembled gods, <br> If Mars, set free, escape from debt and chains?”</p>
<p class="verse">Again the god who shakes the earth replied:⁠— <br> “Vulcan, though Mars deny the forfeit due, <br> And take to flight, it shall be paid by me.”</p>
<p class="verse">Again illustrious Vulcan said: “Thy word <br> I ought not and I seek not to decline.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake, and then the might of Vulcan loosed <br> The net, and, freed from those strong fetters, both <br> The prisoners sprang away. Mars flew to Thrace, <br> And laughter-loving Venus to the isle <br> Of Cyprus, where at Paphos stand her grove <br> And perfumed altar. Here the Graces gave <br> The bath, anointed with ambrosial oil <br> Her limbs⁠—such oil as to the eternal gods <br> Lends a fresh beauty, and arrayed her last <br> In graceful robes, a marvel to behold.</p>
<p class="verse">So sang the famous bard, while inly pleased <br> Ulysses heard, and pleased were all the rest, <br> Phaeacia’s sons, expert with oar and sail.</p>
<p class="verse">Alcinoüs called his sons Laodamas <br> And Halius forth, and bade them dance alone, <br> For none of all the others equalled them. <br> Then taking a fair purple ball, the work <br> Of skilful Polybus, and, bending back, <br> One flung it toward the shadowy clouds on high, <br> The other springing upward easily <br> Grasped it before he touched the ground again. <br> And when they thus had tossed the ball awhile, <br> They danced upon the nourishing earth, and oft <br> Changed places with each other, while the youths, <br> That stood within the circle filled the air <br> With their applauses; mighty was the din. <br> Then great Ulysses to Alcinoüs said:⁠— <br> “O King Alcinoüs! mightiest of the race <br> For whom thou hast engaged that they excel <br> All others in the dance, what thou hast said <br> Is amply proved. I look and am amazed.”</p>
<p class="verse">Well pleased Alcinoüs the mighty heard, <br> And thus to his seafaring people spake:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Leaders and chiefs of the Phaeacians, hear! <br> Wise seems the stranger. Haste we to bestow <br> Gifts that may well beseem his liberal hests. <br> Twelve honored princes in our land bear sway, <br> The thirteenth prince am I. Let each one bring <br> A well-bleached cloak, a tunic, and beside <br> Of precious gold a talent. Let them all <br> Be brought at once, that, having seen them here, <br> Our guest may with a cheerful heart partake <br> The evening meal. And let Euryalus, <br> Who spake but now so unbecomingly, <br> Appease him both with words and with a gift.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake; they all approved, and each one sent <br> His herald with a charge to bring the gifts, <br> And thus Euryalus addressed the king:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“O King Alcinoüs, mightiest of our race, <br> I will obey thee, and will seek to appease <br> Our guest. This sword of brass will I bestow, <br> With hilt of silver, and an ivory sheath <br> New wrought, which he may deem a gift of price.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake, and gave the silver-studded sword <br> Into his hand, and spake these winged words:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Stranger and father, hail! If any word <br> That hath been uttered gave offence, may storms <br> Sweep it away forever. May the gods <br> Give thee to see thy wife again, and reach <br> Thy native land, where all thy sufferings <br> And this long absence from thy friends shall end!”</p>
<p class="verse">Ulysses, the sagacious, thus replied:⁠— <br> “Hail also, friend! and may the gods confer <br> On thee all happiness, and may the time <br> Never arrive when thou shalt miss the sword <br> Placed in my hands with reconciling word!”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake, and slung the silver-studded sword <br> Upon his shoulders. Now the sun went down, <br> And the rich presents were already brought. <br> The noble heralds came and carried them <br> Into the palace of Alcinoüs, where <br> His blameless sons received and ranged them all <br> In fair array before the queenly dame <br> Their mother. Meantime had the mighty king <br> Alcinoüs to his palace led the way, <br> Where they who followed took the lofty seats, <br> And thus Alcinoüs to Aretè said:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Bring now a coffer hither, fairly shaped, <br> The best we have, and lay a well-bleached cloak <br> And tunic in it; set upon the fire <br> A brazen cauldron for our guest, to warm <br> The water of his bath, that having bathed <br> And viewed the gifts which the Phaeacian chiefs <br> Have brought him, ranged in order, he may sit <br> Delighted at the banquet and enjoy <br> The music. I will give this beautiful cup <br> Of gold, that he, in memory of me, <br> May daily in his palace pour to Jove <br> Libations, and to all the other gods.” <br> He spake; Aretè bade her maidens haste <br> To place an ample tripod on the fire. <br> Forthwith upon the blazing fire they set <br> A laver with three feet, and in it poured <br> Water, and heaped fresh fuel on the flames. <br> The flames crept up the vessel’s swelling sides, <br> And warmed the water. Meantime from her room <br> Aretè brought a beautiful chest, in which <br> She laid the presents destined for her guest⁠— <br> Garments and gold which the Phaeacians gave⁠— <br> And laid the cloak and tunic with the rest, <br> And thus in winged words addressed the chief:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Look to the lid thyself, and cast a cord <br> Around it, lest, upon thy voyage home, <br> Thou suffer loss, when haply thou shalt take <br> A pleasant slumber in the dark-hulled ship.”</p>
<p class="verse">Ulysses, the sagacious, heard, and straight <br> He fitted to its place the lid, and wound <br> And knotted artfully around the chest <br> A cord, as queenly Circè long before <br> Had taught him. Then to call him to the bath <br> The housewife of the palace came. He saw <br> Gladly the steaming laver, for not oft <br> Had he been cared for thus, since he had left <br> The dwelling of the nymph with amber hair, <br> Calypso, though attended while with her <br> As if he were a god. Now when the maids <br> Had seen him bathed, and had anointed him <br> With oil, and put his sumptuous mantle on, <br> And tunic, forth he issued from the bath, <br> And came to those who sat before their wine. <br> Nausicaä, goddess-like in beauty, stood <br> Beside a pillar of that noble roof, <br> And looking on Ulysses as he passed, <br> Admired, and said to him in winged words:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Stranger, farewell, and in thy native land <br> Remember thou hast owed thy life to me.”</p>
<p class="verse">Ulysses, the sagacious, answering said:⁠— <br> “Nausicaä, daughter of the large-souled king <br> Alcinoüs! so may Jove, the Thunderer, <br> Husband of Juno, grant that I behold <br> My home, returning safe, as I will make <br> To thee as to a goddess day by day <br> My prayer; for, lady, thou hast saved my life.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake, and near Alcinoüs took his place <br> Upon a throne. And now they served the feast <br> To each, and mingled wine. A herald led <br> Thither the gentle bard Demodocus, <br> Whom all the people honored. Him they placed <br> Amidst the assembly, where he leaned against <br> A lofty column. Sage Ulysses then <br> Carved from the broad loin of a white-tusked boar <br> A part, where yet a mass of flesh remained <br> Bordered with fat, and to the herald said:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Bear this, O herald, to Demodocus, <br> That he may eat. Him, even in my grief, <br> Will I embrace, for worthily the bards <br> Are honored and revered o’er all the earth <br> By every race of men. The Muse herself <br> Hath taught them song; she loves the minstrel tribe.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake; the herald laid the flesh before <br> Demodocus the hero, who received <br> The gift well pleased. Then all the guests put forth <br> Their hands and shared the viands on the board; <br> And when their thirst and hunger were allayed, <br> Thus to the minstrel sage Ulysses spake:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Demodocus, above all other men <br> I give thee praise, for either has the Muse, <br> Jove’s daughter, or Apollo, visited <br> And taught thee. Truly hast thou sung the fate <br> Of the Achaian warriors⁠—what they did <br> And suffered⁠—all their labors as if thou <br> Hadst been among them, or hadst heard the tale <br> From an eyewitness. Now, I pray, proceed, <br> And sing the invention of the wooden horse <br> Made by Epeius with Minerva’s aid, <br> And by the chief Ulysses artfully <br> Conveyed into the Trojan citadel, <br> With armed warriors in its womb to lay <br> The city waste. And I, if thou relate <br> The story rightly, will at once declare <br> To all that largely hath some bounteous god <br> Bestowed on thee the holy gift of song.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake; the poet felt the inspiring god, <br> And sang, beginning where the Argives hurled <br> Firebrands among their tents, and sailed away <br> In their good galleys, save the band that sat <br> Beside renowned Ulysses in the horse, <br> Concealed from sight, amid the Trojan crowd, <br> Who now had drawn it to the citadel. <br> So there it stood, while, sitting round it, talked <br> The men of Troy, and wist not what to do. <br> By turns three counsels pleased them⁠—to hew down <br> The hollow trunk with the remorseless steel; <br> Or drag it to a height, and cast it thence <br> Headlong among the rocks; or, lastly, leave <br> The enormous image standing and unharmed, <br> An offering to appease the gods. And this <br> At last was done; for so had fate decreed <br> That they should be destroyed whene’er their town <br> Should hold within its walls the horse of wood, <br> In which the mightiest of the Argives came <br> Among the sons of Troy to smite and slay. <br> Then sang the bard how, issuing from the womb <br> Of that deceitful horse, the sons of Greece <br> Laid Ilium waste; how each in different ways <br> Ravaged the town, while, terrible as Mars, <br> Ulysses, joined with Menelaus, sought <br> The palace of Deiphobus, and there <br> Maintained a desperate battle, till the aid <br> Of mighty Pallas made the victory his.</p>
<p class="verse">So sang renowned Demodocus; the strain <br> Melted to tears Ulysses, from whose lids <br> They dropped and wet his cheeks. As when a wife <br> Weeps her beloved husband, slain before <br> His town and people, fighting to defend <br> Them and his own dear babes from deadly harm, <br> She sees him gasp and die, and at the sight <br> She falls with piercing cries upon his corpse, <br> Meantime the victors beat her on the back <br> And shoulders with their spears, and bear her off <br> To toil and grieve in slavery, where her cheeks <br> In that long bitter sorrow lose their bloom; <br> So from the eyelids of Ulysses fell <br> The tears, yet fell unnoticed by them all <br> Save that Alcinoüs, sitting at his side, <br> Saw them, and heard his heavy sighs, and thus <br> Bespake his people, masters of the oar:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Princes and chiefs of the Phaeacian race, <br> Give ear. Let now Demodocus lay by <br> His clear-toned harp. The matter of his song <br> Delights not all alike. Since first we sat <br> At meat, and since our noble bard began <br> His lay, our guest has never ceased to grieve; <br> Some mighty sorrow weighs upon his heart. <br> Now let the bard refrain, that we may all <br> Enjoy the banquet, both our guest and we <br> Who welcome him, for it is fitting thus. <br> And now are all things for our worthy guest <br> Made ready, both the escort and these gifts, <br> The pledges of our kind regard. A guest, <br> A suppliant, is a brother, even to him <br> Who bears a heart not easy to be moved. <br> No longer, then, keep back with studied art <br> What I shall ask; ’twere better far to speak <br> With freedom. Tell the name thy mother gave, <br> Thy father, and all those who dwell within, <br> And round thy city. For no living man <br> Is nameless from the time that he is born. <br> Humble or high in station, at their birth <br> The parents give them names. Declare thy land, <br> Thy people, and thy city, that our ships <br> May learn, and bear thee to the place; for here <br> In our Phaeacian ships no pilots are, <br> Nor rudders, as in ships of other lands. <br> Ours know the thoughts and the intents of men. <br> To them all cities and all fertile coasts <br> Inhabited by men are known; they cross <br> The great sea scudding fast, involved in mist <br> And darkness, with no fear of perishing <br> Or meeting harm. I heard Nausithoüs, <br> My father, say that Neptune was displeased <br> With us for safely bearing to their homes <br> So many men, and that he would destroy <br> In after time some good Phaeacian ship, <br> Returning from a convoy, in the waves <br> Of the dark sea, and leave her planted there, <br> A mountain huge and high, before our town. <br> So did the aged chieftain prophesy; <br> The god, as best may please him, will fulfil <br> My father’s words, or leave them unfulfilled. <br> Now tell me truly whither thou hast roamed, <br> And what the tribes of men that thou hast seen; <br> Tell which of them are savage, rude, unjust, <br> And which are hospitable and revere <br> The blessed gods. Declare why thou didst weep <br> And sigh when hearing what unhappy fate <br> Befell the Argive and Achaian host <br> And town of Troy. The gods decreed it; they <br> Ordain destruction to the sons of men, <br> A theme of song thereafter. Hadst thou not <br> Some valiant kinsman who was slain at Troy? <br> A son-in-law? the father of thy wife? <br> Nearest of all are they to us, save those <br> Of our own blood. Or haply might it be <br> Some bosom-friend, one eminently graced <br> With all that wins our love; for not less dear <br> Than if he were a brother should we hold <br> The wise and gentle man who is our friend.”</p>
