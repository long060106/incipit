<p class="lede">A council of the gods⁠—Deliberations concerning Ulysses⁠—Mercury despatched to Calypso, to bid her send Ulysses to Ithaca⁠—Visit of Pallas, in the shape of Mentor, to Telemachus, advising him to repair to Pylos and Sparta in quest of his father, Ulysses⁠—Revels of the suitors of Penelope⁠—Phemius, the minstrel, and his song of the return of the Grecians⁠—The suitors rebuked by Telemachus.</p>
<p class="verse">Tell me, O Muse, of that sagacious man <br> Who, having overthrown the sacred town <br> Of Ilium, wandered far and visited <br> The capitals of many nations, learned <br> The customs of their dwellers, and endured <br> Great suffering on the deep: his life was oft <br> In peril, as he labored to bring back <br> His comrades to their homes. He saved them not, <br> Though earnestly he strove; they perished all, <br> Through their own folly; for they banqueted, <br> Madmen! upon the oxen of the Sun⁠— <br> The all-o’erlooking Sun, who cut them off <br> From their return. O goddess, virgin-child <br> Of Jove, relate some part of this to me.</p>
<p class="verse">Now all the rest, as many as escaped <br> The cruel doom of death, were at their homes <br> Safe from the perils of the war and sea, <br> While him alone, who pined to see his home <br> And wife again, Calypso, queenly nymph, <br> Great among goddesses, detained within <br> Her spacious grot, in hope that he might yet <br> Become her husband. Even when the years <br> Brought round the time in which the gods decreed <br> That he should reach again his dwelling-place <br> In Ithaca, though he was with his friends, <br> His toils were not yet ended. Of the gods <br> All pitied him save Neptune, who pursued <br> With wrath implacable the godlike chief, <br> Ulysses, even to his native land.</p>
<p class="verse">Among the Ethiopians was the god <br> Far off⁠—the Ethiopians most remote <br> Of men. Two tribes there are; one dwells beneath <br> The rising, one beneath the setting sun. <br> He went to grace a hecatomb of beeves <br> And lambs, and sat delighted at the feast; <br> While in the palace of Olympian Jove <br> The other gods assembled, and to them <br> The father of immortals and of men <br> Was speaking. To his mind arose the thought <br> Of that Aegisthus whom the famous son <br> Of Agamemnon, Prince Orestes, slew. <br> Of him he thought and thus bespake the gods:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“How strange it is that mortals blame the gods <br> And say that we inflict the ills they bear, <br> When they, by their own folly and against <br> The will of fate, bring sorrow on themselves! <br> As late Aegisthus, unconstrained by fate, <br> Married the queen of Atreus’ son and slew <br> The husband just returned from war. Yet well <br> He knew the bitter penalty, for we <br> Warned him. We sent the herald Argicide, <br> Bidding him neither slay the chief nor woo <br> His queen, for that Orestes, when he came <br> To manhood and might claim his heritage, <br> Would take due vengeance for Atrides slain. <br> So Hermes said; his prudent words moved not <br> The purpose of Aegisthus who now pays <br> The forfeit of his many crimes at once.”</p>
<p class="verse">Pallas, the blue-eyed goddess, thus replied:⁠— <br> “O father, son of Saturn, king of kings! <br> Well he deserved his death. So perish all <br> Guilty of deeds like his! But I am grieved <br> For sage Ulysses, that most wretched man, <br> So long detained, repining, and afar <br> From those he loves, upon a distant isle <br> Girt by the waters of the central deep⁠— <br> A forest isle, where dwells a deity <br> The daughter of wise Atlas, him who knows <br> The ocean to its utmost depths, and holds <br> Upright the lofty columns which divide <br> The earth from heaven. The daughter there detains <br> The unhappy chieftain, and with flattering words <br> Would win him to forget his Ithaca. <br> Meanwhile, impatient to behold the smokes <br> That rise from hearths in his own land, he pines <br> And willingly would die. Is not thy heart, <br> Olympics, touched by this? And did he not <br> Pay grateful sacrifice to thee beside <br> The Argive fleet in the broad realm of Troy? <br> Why then, O Jove, art thou so wroth with him?”</p>
<p class="verse">Then answered cloud-compelling Jove: “My child, <br> What words have passed thy lips? Can I forget <br> Godlike Ulysses, who in gifts of mind <br> Excels all other men, and who has brought <br> Large offerings to the gods that dwell in heaven? <br> Yet he who holds the earth in his embrace, <br> Neptune, pursues him with perpetual hate <br> Because of Polypheme, the Cyclops, strong <br> Beyond all others of his giant race, <br> Whose eye Ulysses had put out. The nymph <br> Thoosa brought him forth⁠—a daughter she <br> Of Phorcys, ruling in the barren deep⁠— <br> And in the covert of o’erhanging rocks <br> She met with Neptune. For this cause the god <br> Who shakes the shores, although he slay him not, <br> Sends forth Ulysses wandering far away <br> From his own country. Let us now consult <br> Together and provide for his return, <br> And Neptune will lay by his wrath, for vain <br> It were for one like him to strive alone <br> Against the might of all the immortal gods.”</p>
<p class="verse">And then the blue-eyed Pallas spake again:⁠— <br> “O father! son of Saturn, king of kings! <br> If such the pleasure of the blessed gods <br> That now the wise Ulysses shall return <br> To his own land, let us at once despatch <br> Hermes, the Argicide, our messenger, <br> Down to Ogygia, to the bright-haired nymph, <br> And make our steadfast purpose known to bring <br> The sufferer Ulysses to his home, <br> And I will haste to Ithaca, and move <br> His son, that with a resolute heart he call <br> The long-haired Greeks together and forbid <br> The excesses of the suitor train, who slay <br> His flocks and slow-paced beeves with crooked horns. <br> To Sparta I will send him and the sands <br> Of Pylos, to inquire for the return <br> Of his dear father. So a glorious fame <br> Shall gather round him in the eyes of men.”</p>
<p class="verse">She spake, and fastened underneath her feet <br> The fair, ambrosial golden sandals worn <br> To bear her over ocean like the wind, <br> And o’er the boundless land. In hand she took, <br> Well tipped with trenchant brass, the mighty spear, <br> Heavy and huge and strong, with which she bears <br> Whole phalanxes of heroes to the earth, <br> When she, the daughter of a mighty sire, <br> Is angered. From the Olympian heights she plunged, <br> And stood among the men of Ithaca, <br> Just at the porch and threshold of their chief, <br> Ulysses. In her hand she bore the spear, <br> And seemed the stranger Mentes, he who led <br> The Taphians. There before the gate she found <br> The haughty suitors. Some beguiled the time <br> With draughts, while sitting on the hides of beeves <br> Which they had slaughtered. Heralds were with them, <br> And busy menials: some who in the bowls <br> Tempered the wine with water, some who cleansed <br> The tables with light sponges, and who set <br> The banquet forth and carved the meats for all.</p>
<p class="verse">Telemachus the godlike was the first <br> To see the goddess as he sat among <br> The crowd of suitors, sad at heart, and thought <br> Of his illustrious father, who might come <br> And scatter those who filled his palace halls, <br> And win new honor, and regain the rule <br> Over his own. As thus he sat and mused <br> Among the suitors, he beheld where stood <br> Pallas, and forth he sprang; he could not bear <br> To keep a stranger waiting at his door. <br> He came, and taking her right hand received <br> The brazen spear, and spake these winged words:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Hail, stranger! thou art truly welcome here, <br> First come and share our feast and be refreshed, <br> Then say what thou requirest at our hands.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake and led the way, and in his steps <br> Pallas Athenè followed. Entering then <br> The lofty halls, he set the spear upright <br> By a tall column, in the armory <br> With polished walls, where rested many a lance <br> Of the large-souled Ulysses. Then he placed <br> His guest upon a throne, o’er which he spread <br> A covering many-hued and beautiful, <br> And gave her feet a footstool. Near to her <br> He drew his parti-colored seat, aloof <br> From where the suitors sat; that so his guest <br> Might not amid those haughty revellers <br> Be wearied with the tumult and enjoy <br> His meal the less, and that himself might ask <br> News of his absent father. In a bowl <br> Of silver, from a shapely ewer of gold, <br> A maid poured water for the hands, and set <br> A polished table near them. Then approached <br> A venerable matron bringing bread <br> And delicacies gathered from the board; <br> And he who served the feast before them placed <br> Chargers with various meats, and cups of gold; <br> While round the board a herald moved, and poured <br> Wine for the guests. The haughty suitors now <br> Came in, and took their places on the thrones <br> And couches; heralds poured upon their hands <br> The water; maidens heaped the canisters <br> With bread, and all put forth their hands to share <br> The banquet on the board, while to the brim <br> Boys filled the beakers. When the calls of thirst <br> And hunger were appeased, the suitors thought <br> Of other things that well become a feast⁠— <br> Song and the dance. And then a herald brought <br> A shapely harp, and gave it to the hands <br> Of Phemius, who had only by constraint <br> Sung to the suitors. On the chords he struck <br> A prelude to his lay, while, as he played, <br> Telemachus, that others might not hear, <br> Leaned forward, and to blue-eyed Pallas spake:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“My friend and guest, wilt thou take no offence <br> At what I say? These revellers enjoy <br> The harp and song, for at no cost of theirs <br> They waste the substance of another man, <br> Whose white bones now are mouldering in the rain <br> Upon some mainland, or are tossed about <br> By ocean billows. Should they see him once <br> In Ithaca, their prayers would rather rise <br> For swifter feet than richer stores of gold <br> And raiment. But an evil fate is his, <br> And he has perished. Even should we hear <br> From any of the dwellers upon earth <br> That he is near at hand, we could not hope. <br> For him is no return. But now, I pray, <br> Tell me, and frankly tell me, who thou art, <br> And of what race of men, and where thy home, <br> And who thy parents; how the mariners <br> Brought thee to Ithaca, and who they claim <br> To be, for well I deem thou couldst not come <br> Hither on foot. All this, I pray, relate <br> Truly, that I may know the whole. Art thou <br> For the first time arrived, or hast thou been <br> My father’s guest? for many a stranger once <br> Resorted to our palace, and he knew <br> The way to win the kind regard of men.”</p>
<p class="verse">Pallas, the blue-eyed goddess, answered thus:⁠— <br> “I will tell all and truly. I am named <br> Mentes; my father was the great in war <br> Anchialus. I rule a people skilled <br> To wield the oar, the Taphians, and I come <br> With ship and crew across the dark blue deep <br> To Temesè, and to a race whose speech <br> Is different from my own, in quest of brass, <br> And bringing bright steel with me. I have left <br> Moored at the field behind the town my barque, <br> Within the bay of Reithrus, and beneath <br> The woods of Neius. We claim to be <br> Guests by descent, and from our fathers’ time, <br> As thou wilt learn if thou shouldst meet and ask <br> Laertes, the old hero. It is said <br> He comes no more within the city walls, <br> But in the fields dwells sadly by himself, <br> Where an old handmaid sets upon his board <br> His food and drink when weariness unnerves <br> His limbs in creeping o’er the fertile soil <br> Of his rich vineyard. I am come because <br> I heard thy father had at last returned, <br> And now am certain that the gods delay <br> His journey hither; for the illustrious man <br> Cannot have died, but is detained alone <br> Somewhere upon the ocean, in some spot <br> Girt by the waters. There do cruel men <br> And savage keep him, pining to depart. <br> Now let me speak of what the gods reveal, <br> And what I deem will surely come to pass, <br> Although I am no seer and have no skill <br> In omens drawn from birds. Not long the chief <br> Will be an exile from his own dear land, <br> Though fettered to his place by links of steel; <br> For he has large invention, and will plan <br> A way for his escape. Now tell me this, <br> And truly; tall in stature as thou art, <br> Art thou in fact Ulysses’ son? In face <br> And glorious eyes thou dost resemble him <br> Exceedingly; for he and I of yore <br> Were oftentimes companions, ere he sailed <br> For Ilium, whither also went the best <br> Among the Argives in their roomy ships, <br> Nor have we seen each other since that day.”</p>
<p class="verse">Telemachus, the prudent, spake: “O guest, <br> True answer shalt thou have. My mother says <br> I am his son; I know not; never man <br> Knew his own father. Would I were the son <br> Of one whose happier lot it was to meet <br> Amidst his own estates the approach of age. <br> Now the most wretched of the sons of men <br> Is he to whom they say I owe my birth. <br> Thus is thy question answered.” Then again <br> Spake blue-eyed Pallas: “Of a truth, the gods <br> Ordain not that thy race, in years to come, <br> Should be inglorious, since Penelope <br> Hath borne thee such as I behold thee now. <br> But frankly answer me⁠—what feast is here, <br> And what is this assembly? What may be <br> The occasion? is a banquet given? is this <br> A wedding? A collation, where the guests <br> Furnish the meats, I think it cannot be, <br> So riotously goes the revel on <br> Throughout the palace. A well-judging man, <br> If he should come among them, would be moved <br> With anger at the shameful things they do.”</p>
<p class="verse">Again Telemachus, the prudent, spake:⁠— <br> “Since thou dost ask me, stranger, know that once <br> Rich and illustrious might this house be called <br> While yet the chief was here. But now the gods <br> Have grown unkind and willed it otherwise, <br> They make his fate a mystery beyond <br> The fate of other men. I should not grieve <br> So deeply for his loss if he had fallen <br> With his companions on the field of Troy, <br> Or midst his kindred when the war was o’er. <br> Then all the Greeks had built his monument, <br> And he had left his son a heritage <br> Of glory. Now has he become the prey <br> Of Harpies, perishing ingloriously, <br> Unseen, his fate unheard of, and has left <br> Mourning and grief, my portion. Not for him <br> Alone I grieve; the gods have cast on me <br> Yet other hardships. All the chiefs who rule <br> The isles, Dulichium, Samos, and the groves <br> That shade Zacynthus, and who bear the sway <br> In rugged Ithaca, have come to woo <br> My mother, and from day to day consume <br> My substance. She rejects not utterly <br> Their hateful suit, and yet she cannot bear <br> To end it by a marriage. Thus they waste <br> My heritage, and soon will seek my life.”</p>
<p class="verse">Again in grief and anger Pallas spake:⁠— <br> “Yea, greatly dost thou need the absent chief <br> Ulysses here, that he might lay his hands <br> Upon these shameless suitors. Were he now <br> To come and stand before the palace gate <br> With helm and buckler and two spears, as first <br> I saw him in our house, when drinking wine <br> And feasting, just returned from Ephyrè <br> Where Ilus dwelt, the son of Mermerus⁠— <br> For thither went Ulysses in a barque, <br> To seek a deadly drug with which to taint <br> His brazen arrows; Ilus gave it not; <br> He feared the immortal gods; my father gave <br> The poison, for exceedingly he loved <br> His guest⁠—could now Ulysses, in such guise, <br> Once meet the suitors, short would be their lives <br> And bitter would the marriage banquet be. <br> Yet whether he return or not to take <br> Vengeance, in his own palace, on this crew <br> Of wassailers, rests only with the gods. <br> Now let me counsel thee to think betimes <br> How thou shalt thrust them from thy palace gates. <br> Observe me, and attend to what I say: <br> Tomorrow thou shalt call the Achaian chiefs <br> To an assembly; speak before them all, <br> And be the gods thy witnesses. Command <br> The suitors all to separate for their homes; <br> And if thy mother’s mind be bent to wed, <br> Let her return to where her father dwells, <br> A mighty prince, and there they will appoint <br> Magnificent nuptials, and an ample dower <br> Such as should honor a beloved child. <br> And now, if thou wilt heed me, I will give <br> A counsel for thy good. Man thy best ship <br> With twenty rowers, and go forth to seek <br> News of thy absent father. Thou shalt hear <br> Haply of him from someone of the sons <br> Of men, or else some word of rumor sent <br> By Jove, revealing what mankind should know. <br> First shape thy course for Pylos, and inquire <br> Of noble Nestor; then, at Sparta, ask <br> Of fair-haired Menelaus, for he came <br> Last of the mailed Achaians to his home. <br> And shouldst thou learn that yet thy father lives, <br> And will return, have patience yet a year, <br> However hard it seem. But shouldst thou find <br> That he is now no more, return forthwith <br> To thy own native land, and pile on high <br> His monument, and let the funeral rites <br> Be sumptuously performed as may become <br> The dead, and let thy mother wed again. <br> And when all this is fully brought to pass, <br> Take counsel with thy spirit and thy heart <br> How to destroy the suitor crew that haunt <br> Thy palace, whether by a secret snare <br> Or open force. No longer shouldst thou act <br> As if thou wert a boy; thou hast outgrown <br> The age of childish sports. Hast thou not heard <br> What honor the divine Orestes gained <br> With all men, when he slew the murderer, <br> The crafty wretch Aegisthus, by whose hand <br> The illustrious father of Orestes died? <br> And then, my friend⁠—for I perceive that thou <br> Art of a manly and a stately growth⁠— <br> Be also bold, that men hereafter born <br> May give thee praise. And now must I depart <br> To my good ship, and to my friends who wait, <br> Too anxiously perhaps, for my return. <br> Act wisely now, and bear my words in mind.”</p>
<p class="verse">The prudent youth Telemachus rejoined:⁠— <br> “Well hast thou spoken, and with kind intent, <br> O stranger! like a father to a son; <br> And ne’er shall I forget what thou hast said. <br> Yet stay, I pray thee, though in haste, and bathe <br> And be refreshed, and take to thy good ship <br> Some gift with thee, such as may please thee well, <br> Precious and rare, which thou mayst ever keep <br> In memory of me⁠—a gift like those <br> Which friendly hosts bestow upon their guests.”</p>
<p class="verse">Then spake the blue-eyed Pallas: “Stay me not, <br> For now would I depart. Whatever gift <br> Thy heart may prompt thee to bestow, reserve <br> Till I come back, that I may bear it home, <br> And thou shalt take some precious thing in turn.”</p>
<p class="verse">So spake the blue-eyed Pallas, and withdrew, <br> Ascending like a bird. She filled his heart <br> With strength and courage, waking vividly <br> His father’s memory. Then the noble youth <br> Went forth among the suitors. Silent all <br> They sat and listened to the illustrious bard, <br> Who sang of the calamitous return <br> Of the Greek host from Troy, at the command <br> Of Pallas. From her chamber o’er the hall <br> The daughter of Icarius, the sage queen <br> Penelope, had heard the heavenly strain, <br> And knew its theme. Down by the lofty stairs <br> She came, but not alone; there followed her <br> Two maidens. When the glorious lady reached <br> The threshold of the strong-built hall, where sat <br> The suitors, holding up a delicate veil <br> Before her face, and with a gush of tears, <br> The queen bespake the sacred minstrel thus:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Phemius! thou knowest many a pleasing theme⁠— <br> The deeds of gods and heroes, such as bards <br> Are wont to celebrate. Take then thy place <br> And sing of one of these, and let the guests <br> In silence drink the wine; but cease this strain; <br> It is too sad; it cuts me to the heart, <br> And wakes a sorrow without bounds⁠—such grief <br> I bear for him, my lord, of whom I think <br> Continually; whose glory is abroad <br> Through Hellas and through Argos, everywhere.”</p>
<p class="verse">And then Telemachus, the prudent, spake:⁠— <br> “Why, O my mother! canst thou not endure <br> That thus the well-graced poet should delight <br> His hearers with a theme to which his mind <br> Is inly moved? The bards deserve no blame; <br> Jove is the cause, for he at will inspires <br> The lay that each must sing. Reprove not, then, <br> The minstrel who relates the unhappy fate <br> Of the Greek warriors. All men most applaud <br> The song that has the newest theme; and thou⁠— <br> Strengthen thy heart to hear it. Keep in mind <br> That not alone Ulysses is cut off <br> From his return, but that with him at Troy <br> Have many others perished. Now withdraw <br> Into thy chamber; ply thy household tasks, <br> The loom, the spindle; bid thy maidens speed <br> Their work. To say what words beseem a feast <br> Belongs to man, and most to me; for here <br> Within these walls the authority is mine.”</p>
<p class="verse">The matron, wondering at his words, withdrew <br> To her own place, but in her heart laid up <br> Her son’s wise sayings. When she now had reached, <br> With her attendant maids, the upper rooms, <br> She mourned Ulysses, her beloved spouse, <br> And wept, till blue-eyed Pallas closed her lids <br> In gentle slumbers. Noisily, meanwhile, <br> The suitors revelled in the shadowy halls; <br> And thus Telemachus, the prudent, spake:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Ye suitors of my mother, insolent <br> And overbearing; cheerful be our feast, <br> Not riotous. It would become us well <br> To listen to the lay of such a bard, <br> So like the gods in voice. I bid you all <br> Meet in full council with the morrow morn, <br> That I may give you warning to depart <br> From out my palace, and to seek your feasts <br> Elsewhere at your own charge⁠—haply to hold <br> Your daily banquets at each other’s homes. <br> But if it seem to you the better way <br> To plunder one man’s goods, go on to waste <br> My substance; I will call the immortal gods <br> To aid me, and if Jupiter allow <br> Fit retribution for your deeds, ye die, <br> Within this very palace, unavenged.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake; the suitors bit their close-pressed lips, <br> Astonished at the youth’s courageous words. <br> And thus Antinoüs, Eupeithes’ son, <br> Made answer: “Most assuredly the gods, <br> Telemachus, have taught thee how to frame <br> Grand sentences and gallantly harangue. <br> Ne’er may the son of Saturn make thee king <br> Over the seagirt Ithaca, whose isle <br> Is thy inheritance by claim of birth.”</p>
<p class="verse">Telemachus, the prudent, thus rejoined:⁠— <br> “Wilt thou be angry at the word I speak, <br> Antinoüs? I would willingly accept <br> The kingly station if conferred by Jove. <br> Dost thou indeed regard it as the worst <br> Of all conditions of mankind? Not so <br> For him who reigns; his house grows opulent, <br> And he the more is honored. Many kings <br> Within the bounds of seagirt Ithaca <br> There are, both young and old, let anyone <br> Bear rule, since great Ulysses is no more; <br> But I will be the lord of mine own house, <br> And o’er my servants whom the godlike chief, <br> Ulysses, brought from war, his share of spoil.”</p>
<p class="verse">Eurymachus, the son of Polybus, <br> Addressed the youth in turn: “Assuredly, <br> What man hereafter, of the Achaian race, <br> Shall bear the rule o’er seagirt Ithaca <br> Rests with the gods. But thou shalt keep thy wealth, <br> And may no son of violence come to make <br> A spoil of thy possessions while men dwell <br> In Ithaca. And now, my friend, I ask <br> Who was thy guest; whence came he, of what land <br> Claims he to be, where do his kindred dwell, <br> And where his patrimonial acres lie? <br> With tidings of thy father’s near return <br> Came he, or to receive a debt? How swift <br> Was his departure, waiting not for us <br> To know him! yet in aspect and in air <br> He seemed to be no man of vulgar note.”</p>
<p class="verse">Telemachus, the prudent, answered thus:⁠— <br> “My father’s coming, O Eurymachus, <br> Is to be hoped no more; nor can I trust <br> Tidings from whatsoever part they come, <br> Nor pay regard to oracles, although <br> My mother send to bring a soothsayer <br> Within the palace, and inquire of him. <br> But this man was my father’s guest; he comes <br> From Taphos; Mentes is his name, a son <br> Of the brave chief Anchialus; he reigns <br> Over the Taphians, men who love the sea.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake, but in his secret heart he knew <br> The immortal goddess. Then the suitors turned, <br> Delighted, to the dance and cheerful song, <br> And waited for the evening. On their sports <br> The evening with its shadowy blackness came; <br> Then each to his own home withdrew to sleep, <br> While to his lofty chamber, in full view, <br> Built high in that magnificent palace home, <br> Telemachus went up, and sought his couch, <br> Intent on many thoughts. The chaste and sage <br> Dame Eurycleia by his side went up <br> With lighted torches⁠—she a child of Ops, <br> Pisenor’s son. Her, in her early bloom, <br> Laertes purchased for a hundred beeves, <br> And in his palace honored equally <br> With his chaste wife; yet never sought her bed. <br> He would not wrong his queen. ’Twas she who bore <br> The torches with Telemachus. She loved <br> Her young lord more than all the other maids, <br> And she had nursed him in his tender years. <br> He opened now the chamber door and sat <br> Upon the couch, put his soft tunic off <br> And placed it in the prudent matron’s hands. <br> She folded it and smoothed it, hung it near <br> To that fair bed, and, going quickly forth, <br> Pulled at the silver ring to close the door, <br> And drew the thong that moved the fastening bolt. <br> He, lapped in the soft fleeces, all night long. <br> Thought of the voyage Pallas had ordained.</p>
