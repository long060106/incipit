<p class="lede">Disorderly conduct of the serving-women⁠—Prayer of Ulysses for a favorable omen⁠—Its fulfillment⁠—Preparations for a feast of the suitors in the palace⁠—The feast⁠—Ulysses insulted by Ctesippus, who is reproved by Telemachus⁠—Strange prodigies observed by Theoclymenus, who leaves the hall.</p>
<p class="verse">The noble chief, Ulysses, in the porch <br> Lay down to rest. An undressed bullock’s hide <br> Was under him, and over that the skins <br> Of sheep, which for the daily sacrifice <br> The Achaians slew. Eurynomè had spread <br> A cloak above him. There he lay awake, <br> And meditated how he yet should smite <br> The suitors down. Meantime, with cries of mirth <br> And laughter, came the women forth to seek <br> The suitors’ arms. Ulysses, inly moved <br> With anger, pondered whether he should rise <br> And put them all to death, or give their shame <br> A respite for another night, the last. <br> His heart raged in his bosom. As a hound <br> Growls, walking round her whelps, when she beholds <br> A stranger, and is eager for the attack, <br> So growled his heart within him, and so fierce <br> Was his impatience with that shameless crew. <br> He smote his breast, and thus he chid his heart:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Endure it, heart! thou didst bear worse than this. <br> When the grim Cyclops of resistless strength <br> Devoured thy brave companions, thou couldst still <br> Endure, till thou by stratagem didst leave <br> The cave in which it seemed that thou must die.”</p>
<p class="verse">Thus he rebuked his heart, and, growing calm, <br> His heart submitted; but the hero tossed <br> From side to side. As when one turns and turns <br> The stomach of a bullock filled with fat <br> And blood before a fiercely blazing fire <br> And wishes it were done, so did the chief <br> Shift oft from side to side, while pondering how <br> To lay a strong hand on the multitude <br> Of shameless suitors⁠—he but one, and they <br> So many. Meantime Pallas, sliding down <br> From heaven, in form a woman, came, and there <br> Beside his bed stood over him, and spake:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Why, most unhappy of the sons of men, <br> Art thou still sleepless? This is thine abode, <br> And here thou hast thy consort and a son <br> Whom any man might covet for his own.”</p>
<p class="verse">Ulysses, the sagacious, answered thus: <br> “Truly, O goddess, all that thou hast said <br> Is rightly spoken. This perplexes me⁠— <br> How to lay hands upon these shameless men, <br> When I am only one, and they a throng <br> That fill the palace. Yet another thought, <br> And mightier still⁠—if, by thy aid and Jove’s, <br> I slay the suitors, how shall I myself <br> Be safe thereafter? Think, I pray, of this.”</p>
<p class="verse">And thus in turn the blue-eyed Pallas said: <br> “O faint of spirit! in an humbler friend <br> Than I am, in a friend of mortal birth <br> And less farseeing, one might put his trust; <br> But I am born a goddess, and protect <br> Thy life in every danger. Let me say, <br> And plainly say, if fifty armed bands <br> Of men should gather round us, eager all <br> To take thy life, thou mightest drive away, <br> Unharmed by them, their herds and pampered flocks. <br> But give thyself to sleep. To wake and watch <br> All night is most unwholesome. Thou shalt find <br> A happy issue from thy troubles yet.”</p>
<p class="verse">She spake, and, shedding slumber on his lids, <br> Upward the glorious goddess took her way <br> Back to Olympus, when she saw that sleep <br> Had seized him, making him forget all care <br> And slackening every limb. His faithful wife <br> Was still awake, and sat upright and wept <br> On her soft couch, and after many tears <br> The glorious lady prayed to Dian thus:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Goddess august! Diana, child of Jove! <br> I would that thou wouldst send into my heart <br> A shaft to take my life, or that a storm <br> Would seize and hurl me through the paths of air, <br> And cast me into ocean’s restless streams, <br> As once a storm, descending, swept away <br> The daughters born to Pandarus. The gods <br> Had slain their parents, and they dwelt alone <br> As orphans in their palace, nourished there <br> By blessed Venus with the curds of milk, <br> And honey, and sweet wine, while Juno gave <br> Beauty and wit beyond all womankind, <br> And chaste Diana dignity of form, <br> And Pallas every art that graces life. <br> Then, as the blessed Venus went to ask <br> For them, of Jove the Thunderer, on the heights <br> Of his Olympian mount, the crowning gift <br> Of happy marriage⁠—for to Jove is known <br> Whatever comes to pass, and what shall be <br> The fortune, good or ill, of mortal men⁠— <br> The Harpies came meantime, bore off the maids, <br> And gave them to the hateful sisterhood <br> Of Furies as their servants. So may those <br> Who dwell upon Olympus make an end <br> Of me, or fair-haired Dian strike me down, <br> That, with the image of Ulysses still <br> Before my mind, I may not seek to please <br> One of less worth. This evil might be borne <br> By one who weeps all day, and feels at heart <br> A settled sorrow, yet can sleep at night. <br> For sleep, when once it weighs the eyelids down, <br> Makes men unmindful both of good and ill, <br> And all things else. But me some deity <br> Visits with fearful dreams. There lay by me, <br> This very night, one like him, as he was <br> When with his armed men he sailed for Troy; <br> And I was glad, for certainly I deemed <br> It was a real presence, and no dream.”</p>
<p class="verse">She spake. Just then, upon her car of gold, <br> Appeared the Morn. The great Ulysses heard <br> That voice of lamentation; anxiously <br> He mused; it seemed to him as if the queen <br> Stood over him and knew him. Gathering up <br> In haste the cloak and skins on which he slept, <br> He laid them in the palace on a seat, <br> But bore the bull’s hide forth in open air, <br> And lifted up his hands and prayed to Jove:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“O Father Jove, and all the gods! if ye <br> Have led me graciously, o’er land and deep, <br> Across the earth, and, after suffering much, <br> To mine own isle, let one of those who watch <br> Within the palace speak some ominous word, <br> And grant a sign from thee without these walls.”</p>
<p class="verse">So prayed he. All-providing Jupiter <br> Hearkened, and thundered from the clouds around <br> The bright Olympian peaks. Ulysses heard <br> With gladness. From a room within the house, <br> In which the mills of the king’s household stood, <br> A woman, laboring at the quern, gave forth <br> An omen also. There were twelve who toiled <br> In making flour of barley and of wheat⁠— <br> The strength of man. The rest were all asleep; <br> Their tasks were done; one only, of less strength <br> Than any other there, kept toiling on. <br> She paused a moment, stopped the whirling stone, <br> And spake these words⁠—a portent for the king:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“O Father Jove, the king of gods and men! <br> Thou hast just thundered from the starry heaven, <br> And yet there is no cloud. To someone here <br> It is a portent. O perform for me, <br> All helpless as I am, this one request! <br> Let now the suitors in this palace take <br> Their last and final pleasant feast today⁠— <br> These men who make my limbs, with constant toil, <br> In grinding corn for them, to lose their strength, <br> Once let them banquet here, and then no more.”</p>
<p class="verse">She spake; the omen of the woman’s words <br> And Jove’s loud thunder pleased Ulysses well; <br> And now he deemed he should avenge himself <br> Upon the guilty ones. The other maids <br> Of that fair palace of Ulysses woke <br> And came together, and upon the hearth <br> Kindled a steady fire. Telemachus <br> Rose from his bed in presence like a god, <br> Put on his garments, hung his trenchant sword <br> Upon his shoulder, tied to his fair feet <br> The shapely sandals, took his massive spear <br> Tipped with sharp brass, and, stopping as he reached <br> The threshold, spake to Eurycleia thus:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Dear nurse, have ye with honor fed and lodged <br> Our guest, or have ye suffered him to find <br> A lodging where he might, without your care? <br> Discerning as she is, my mother pays <br> High honor to the worse among her guests, <br> And sends the nobler man unhonored hence.”</p>
<p class="verse">And thus the prudent Eurycleia said: <br> “My child, blame not thy mother; she deserves <br> No blame. The stranger sat and drank his wine, <br> All that he would, and said, when pressed to eat, <br> That he desired no more. And when he thought <br> Of sleep, she bade her maidens spread his couch; <br> But he refused a bed and rugs, like one <br> Inured to misery, and beneath the porch <br> Slept on an undressed bull’s hide and the skins <br> Of sheep, and over him we cast a cloak.”</p>
<p class="verse">She spake; Telemachus, his spear in hand, <br> Went forth, his fleet dogs following him. He sought <br> The council where the well-greaved Greeks were met. <br> Meantime the noble Eurycleia, child <br> Of Ops, Pisenor’s son, bespake the maids:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Come, some of you, at once, and sweep the floor, <br> And sprinkle it, and on the shapely thrones <br> Spread coverings of purple tapestry; <br> Let others wipe the tables with a sponge, <br> And cleanse the beakers and the double cups, <br> While others go for water to the fount, <br> And bring it quickly, for not long today <br> The suitors will be absent from these halls. <br> They will come early to the general feast.”</p>
<p class="verse">She spake; the handmaids hearkened and obeyed, <br> And twenty went to the dark well to draw <br> The water, while the others busily <br> Bestirred themselves about the house. Then came <br> The servants of the chiefs, and set themselves <br> Neatly to cleave the wood. Then also came <br> The women from the well. The swineherd last <br> Came with three swine, the fattest of the herd. <br> In that fair court he let them feed, and sought <br> Ulysses, greeting him with courteous words:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Hast thou, O stranger, found among these Greeks <br> More reverence? Art thou still their mark of scorn?”</p>
<p class="verse">Ulysses, the sagacious, answered thus: <br> “O that the gods, Eumaeus, would avenge <br> The insolence of those who meditate <br> Violent deeds, and make another’s house <br> Their plotting-place, and feel no touch of shame!”</p>
<p class="verse">So talked they with each other. Now appeared <br> Melanthius, keeper of the goats. He brought <br> Goats for the suitors’ banquet; they were choice <br> Beyond all others. With him also came <br> Two goatherds. In the echoing portico <br> He bound his goats. He saw Ulysses there, <br> And thus accosted him with railing words:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Stranger, art thou still here, the palace pest, <br> And begging still, and wilt thou ne’er depart? <br> We shall not end this quarrel, I perceive, <br> Till thou hast tried the flavor of my fist. <br> It is not decent to be begging here <br> Continually; the Greeks have other feasts.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake; Ulysses answered not, but shook <br> His head in silence, planning fearful things.</p>
<p class="verse">Philoetius now, a master-herdsman, came, <br> And for the banquet of the suitors led <br> A heifer that had never yeaned, and goats <br> The fatlings of the flock; they came across <br> The ferry, brought by those whose office is <br> To bear whoever comes from shore to shore. <br> He bound his animals in the sounding porch, <br> And went and, standing by the swineherd, said:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Who, swineherd, is the stranger newly come <br> To this our palace? of what parents born, <br> And of what race, and where his native land? <br> Unhappy seemingly, yet like a king <br> In person. Sorrowful must be the lot <br> Of men who wander to and fro on earth, <br> When even to kings the gods appoint distress.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake, and, greeting with his offered hand <br> Ulysses, said in winged words aloud:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Stranger and father, hail! and mayst thou yet <br> Be happy in the years to come at least, <br> Though held in thrall by many sorrows now. <br> Yet thou, All-father Jove! art most austere <br> Of all the gods, not sparing even those <br> Who have their birth from thee, but bringing them <br> To grief and pain. The sweat is on my brow <br> When I behold this stranger, and my eyes <br> Are filled with tears when to my mind comes back <br> The image of Ulysses, who must now, <br> I think, be wandering, clothed in rags like thee, <br> Among the abodes of men, if yet indeed <br> He lives and sees the sweet light of the sun. <br> But if that he be dead, and in the abode <br> Of Pluto, woe is me for his dear sake! <br> The blameless chief, who when I was a boy <br> Gave to me, in the Cephalenian fields, <br> The charge of all his beeves; and they are now <br> Innumerable; the broad-fronted race <br> Of cattle never would have multiplied <br> So largely under other care than mine. <br> Now other masters bid me bring my beeves <br> For their own feasts. They little heed his son, <br> The palace-heir; as little do they dread <br> The vengeance of the gods; they long to share <br> Among them the possessions of the king, <br> So many years unheard from. But this thought <br> Comes to my mind again, and yet again: <br> Wrong were it, while the son is yet alive, <br> To drive the cattle to a foreign land, <br> Where alien men inhabit; yet ’tis worse <br> To stay and tend another’s beeves, and bear <br> This spoil. And long ago would I have fled <br> To some large-minded monarch, since this waste <br> Is not to be endured, but that I think <br> Still of my suffering lord, and hope that yet <br> He may return and drive the suitors hence.”</p>
<p class="verse">Ulysses, the sagacious, answering, said: <br> “Herdsman, since thou dost seem not ill inclined, <br> Nor yet unwise, and I perceive in thee <br> A well-discerning mind, I therefore say, <br> And pledge my solemn oath⁠—Jove, first of gods, <br> Be witness, and this hospitable board <br> And hearth of good Ulysses, which has here <br> Received me⁠—while thou art within these halls <br> Ulysses will assuredly return, <br> And, if thou choose to look, thine eyes shall see <br> The suitors slain, who play the master here.”</p>
<p class="verse">And thus the master of the herds rejoined: <br> “Stranger, may Jupiter make good thy words! <br> Then shalt thou see what strength is in my arm.”</p>
<p class="verse">Eumaeus also prayed to all the gods, <br> That now the wise Ulysses might return. <br> So talked they with each other, while apart <br> The suitors doomed Telemachus to death, <br> And plotted how to take his life. Just then <br> A bird⁠—an eagle⁠—on the left flew by, <br> High up; his talons held a timid dove. <br> And then Amphinomus bespake the rest:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“O friends, this plan to slay Telemachus <br> Must fail. And now repair we to the feast.”</p>
<p class="verse">So spake Amphinomus, and to his words <br> They all gave heed, and hastened to the halls <br> Of the divine Ulysses, where they laid <br> Their cloaks upon the benches and the thrones, <br> And slaughtering the choice sheep, and fading goats, <br> And porkers, and a heifer from the herd, <br> Roasted the entrails, and distributed <br> A share to each. Next mingled they the wine <br> In the large bowls. The swineherd brought a cup <br> To everyone. Philoetius, chief among <br> The servants, gave from shapely canisters <br> The bread to each. Melanthius poured the wine. <br> Then putting forth their hands, they all partook <br> The ready banquet. With a wise design, <br> Telemachus near the stone threshold placed <br> Ulysses, on a shabby seat, beside <br> A little table, but within the walls <br> Of that strong-pillared pile. He gave him there <br> Part of the entrails, and poured out for him <br> The wine into a cup of gold, and said:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Sit here, and drink thy wine among the rest, <br> And from the insults and assaults of these <br> It shall be mine to guard thee. For this house <br> Is not the common property of all; <br> Ulysses first acquired it, and for me⁠— <br> And you, ye suitors, keep your tongues from taunts <br> And hands from force, lest there be wrath and strife.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake; the suitors, as they heard him, bit <br> Their pressed lips, wondering at Telemachus, <br> Who uttered such bold words. Antinoüs then, <br> Eupeithes’ son, bespake his fellows thus:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Harsh as they are, let us, O Greeks, endure <br> These speeches of Telemachus. He makes <br> High threats, but had Saturnian Jove allowed, <br> We should, ere this, and in these very halls, <br> Have quieted our loud-tongued orator.”</p>
<p class="verse">So spake the suitor, but Telemachus <br> Heeded him not. Then through the city came <br> The heralds with a hallowed hecatomb, <br> Due to the gods. The long-haired people thronged <br> The shady grove of Phoebus, archer-god.</p>
<p class="verse">Now when the flesh was roasted and was drawn <br> From off the spits, and each was given his share, <br> They held high festival. The men who served <br> The banquet gave Ulysses, where he sat, <br> A portion equal to their own, for so <br> His own dear son Telemachus enjoined.</p>
<p class="verse">Yet did not Pallas cause the haughty crew <br> Of suitors to refrain from stinging taunts, <br> That so the spirit of Laertes’ son <br> Might be more deeply wounded. One there was <br> Among the suitors, a low-thoughted wretch; <br> Ctesippus was his name, and his abode <br> Was Samos. Trusting in his father’s wealth, <br> He wooed the wife of the long-absent king <br> Ulysses. To his insolent mates he said:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Hear me, ye noble suitors, while I speak. <br> This stranger has received an equal share, <br> As is becoming; for it were not just <br> Nor seemly to pass by, in such a feast, <br> The guests, whoe’er they may be, that resort <br> To this fair mansion of Telemachus. <br> I also will bestow on him a gift <br> Of hospitality, and he in turn <br> May give it to the keeper of the bath, <br> Or any other of the menial train <br> That serve the household of Ulysses here.”</p>
<p class="verse">So speaking, with his strong right hand he flung <br> A bullock’s foot, which from a canister <br> Hard by he plucked. Ulysses gently bowed <br> His head, and shunned the blow, and grimly smiled. <br> The missile struck the solid wall, and then <br> Telemachus rebuked the suitor thus:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Ctesippus, well hast thou escaped with life, <br> Not having hit the stranger, who himself <br> Shrank from the blow; else had I pinned thee through <br> With my sharp spear. Instead of wedding feast, <br> Thy father would have celebrated here <br> Thy funeral rites. Let no man in these halls <br> Bear himself insolently in my sight <br> Hereafter, for my reason now is ripe <br> To know the right from wrong. I was of late <br> A child, and now it is enough to bear <br> That ye should slay our sheep, and drink our wine, <br> And eat our bread⁠—for what can one man do <br> Against so many? Cease this petty war <br> Of wrong and hatred; but if ye desire <br> To take my life, ’tis well; ’twere better so. <br> And rather would I die by violence <br> Than live to see these most unmanly deeds⁠— <br> Guests driven away, and women-servants hauled <br> Through these fair rooms by brutal wassailers.”</p>
<p class="verse">He ended, and the assembly all sat mute <br> Till Agelaüs spake, Damastor’s son:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“O friends! let no man here with carping words <br> Gainsay what is so rightly said, nor yet <br> Insult the stranger more, nor one of those <br> Who serve the household of the godlike chief <br> Ulysses in his palace. I would say <br> This word in kindness to Telemachus <br> And to his mother; may it please them both! <br> While yet the hope was cherished in your hearts <br> That wise Ulysses would return, no blame <br> Could fasten on the queen that she remained <br> Unwedded, and resisted those who came <br> To woo her in the palace. Better so, <br> Had he come home again. Yet now, ’tis clear, <br> He comes no more. Go then, Telemachus, <br> And, sitting by thy mother, bid her wed <br> The noblest of her wooers, and the one <br> Who brings the richest gifts; and thou possess <br> Thy father’s wealth in peace, and eat and drink <br> At will, while she shall find another home.”</p>
<p class="verse">And thus discreet Telemachus replied: <br> “Nay, Agelaüs, for I swear by Jove, <br> And by my father’s sufferings, who has died, <br> Or yet is wandering, far from Ithaca, <br> That I do nothing to delay the choice <br> And marriage of my mother. I consent <br> That she become the wife of whom she list, <br> And him who offers most. But I should feel <br> Great shame to thrust her forth against her will, <br> And with unfilial speeches; God forbid!”</p>
<p class="verse">He ended here, and Pallas, as he spake, <br> To inextinguishable laughter moved <br> The suitors. There they sat with wandering minds; <br> They swallowed morsels foul with blood; their eyes <br> Were filled with tears; their hearts foreboded woe. <br> Then spake the godlike Theoclymenus:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Unhappy men! what may this evil be <br> That overtakes you? Every brow and face <br> And each one’s lower limbs are wrapped in night, <br> And moans arise, and tears are on your cheeks. <br> The walls and all the graceful cornices <br> Between the pillars are bedropped with blood, <br> The portico is full, these halls are full <br> Of shadows, hastening down to Erebus <br> Amid the gloom. The sun is blotted out <br> From heaven, and fearful darkness covers all.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake, and loud they laughed. Eurymachus, <br> The son of Polybus, in answer said:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“The stranger prattles idly; he is come <br> From some far land. Conduct him through the door, <br> Young men, and send him to the marketplace, <br> Since all things here are darkened to his eyes.”</p>
<p class="verse">Then spake the godlike Theoclymenus: <br> “Eurymachus, from thee I ask no guide, <br> For I have eyes and ears, and two good feet, <br> And in my breast a mind as sound as they, <br> And by the aid of these I mean to make <br> My way without; for clearly I perceive <br> A coming evil, which no suitor here <br> Will yet escape⁠—no one who, in these halls <br> Of the great chief, Ulysses, treats with scorn <br> His fellow-man, and broods o’er guilty plans.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake, and, hastening from that noble pile, <br> Came to Piraeus, in whose house he found <br> A welcome. All the suitors, as he went, <br> Looked at each other, and, the more to vex <br> Telemachus, kept laughing at his guests. <br> And thus an insolent youth among them said:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“No man had ever a worse set of guests <br> Than thou, Telemachus. For what a wretch <br> That wandering beggar is, who always wants <br> His bread and wine, and is unfit for work, <br> And has no strength; in truth, a useless load <br> Upon the earth he treads. The other guest <br> Rises to play the prophet. If thou take <br> My counsel, which I give thee for thy good, <br> Let them at once be put on board a barque <br> Of many oars, and we will send them hence <br> To the Sicilians; they will bring a price.”</p>
<p class="verse">So talked the suitors, but he heeded not <br> Their words, and, looking toward his father, held <br> His peace, expecting when he would lay hands <br> Upon that insolent crew. Penelope, <br> Sage daughter of Icarius, took her place <br> Right opposite upon a sumptuous seat, <br> And heard the words of every man who spake <br> Within the hall. They held that midday feast <br> With laughter⁠—a luxurious feast it was, <br> And mirthful; many victims had been slain <br> To furnish forth the tables; but no feast <br> Could be more bitter than the later one, <br> To which the goddess and that valiant man <br> Would bid the guilty crew of plotters soon.</p>
