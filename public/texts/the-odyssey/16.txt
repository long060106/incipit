<p class="lede">Reception of Telemachus by Eumaeus, who is sent to inform Penelope of her son’s arrival⁠—interview of Ulysses and Telemachus, in which, at the command of Pallas, Ulysses discovers himself⁠—Return of the disappointed suitors from lying in wait for Telemachus.</p>
<p class="verse">Meantime Ulysses and that noble hind <br> The swineherd, in the lodge, at early dawn, <br> Lighted a fire, prepared a meal, and sent <br> The herdsmen forth to drive the swine afield. <br> The dogs, so apt to bark, came fawning round, <br> And barked not as Telemachus drew near. <br> Ulysses heard the sound of coming feet, <br> And marked the crouching dogs, and suddenly <br> Bespake Eumaeus thus with winged words:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Eumaeus, without doubt some friend of thine, <br> Or someone known familiarly, is near. <br> There is no barking of the dogs; they fawn <br> Around him, and I hear the sound of feet.”</p>
<p class="verse">Scarce had he spoken, when within the porch <br> Stood his dear son. The swineherd starting up, <br> Surprised, let fall the vessels from his hands <br> In which he mingled the rich wines, and flew <br> To meet his master; kissed him on the brow; <br> Kissed both his shining eyes and both his hands, <br> With many tears. As when a father takes <br> Into his arms a son whom tenderly <br> He loves, returning from a distant land <br> In the tenth year⁠—his only son, the child <br> Of his old age, for whom he long has borne <br> Hardship and grief⁠—so to Telemachus <br> The swineherd clung, and kissed him o’er and o’er, <br> As one escaped from death, and, shedding still <br> Warm tears, bespake him thus with winged words:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Thou comest, O Telemachus! the light <br> Is not more sweet to me. I never thought <br> To see thee more when thou hadst once embarked <br> For Pylos. Now come in, beloved child, <br> And let my heart rejoice that once again <br> I have thee here, so newly come from far. <br> For ’tis not often that thou visitest <br> Herdsmen and fields, but dwellest in the town⁠— <br> Such is thy will⁠—beholding day by day <br> The wasteful pillage of the suitor-train.”</p>
<p class="verse">And thus discreet Telemachus replied: <br> “So be it, father; for thy sake I came <br> To see thee with these eyes, and hear thee speak <br> And tell me if my mother dwells within <br> The palace yet; or has some wooer led <br> The queen away, his bride, and does the couch <br> Of great Ulysses lie untapestried, <br> With ugly cobwebs gathering over it?”</p>
<p class="verse">And then the master swineherd spake in turn: <br> “Most true it is that with a constant mind <br> The queen inhabits yet thy palace halls, <br> And wastes in tears her wretched nights and days.”</p>
<p class="verse">So speaking he received his brazen lance, <br> And over the stone threshold passed the prince <br> Into the lodge. Ulysses yielded up <br> His seat to him; Telemachus forbade.</p>
<p class="verse">“Nay, stranger, sit; it shall be ours to find <br> Elsewhere a seat in this our lodge, and he <br> Who should provide it is already here.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake; Ulysses turned, and took again <br> His place; the swineherd made a pile of twigs <br> And covered it with skins, on which sat down <br> The dear son of Ulysses. Next he brought <br> Dishes of roasted meats which yet remained, <br> Part of the banquet of the day before, <br> And heaped the canisters with bread, and mixed <br> The rich wines in a wooden bowl. He sat <br> Right opposite Ulysses. All put forth <br> Their hands and shared the meats upon the board; <br> And when the calls of thirst and hunger ceased, <br> Thus to the swineherd said Telemachus:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Whence, father, is this stranger, and how brought <br> By seamen to the coast of Ithaca? <br> And who are they that brought him?⁠—for I deem <br> He came not over to our isle on foot.”</p>
<p class="verse">And thus, Eumaeus, thou didst make reply: <br> “True answer will I make to all. He claims <br> To be a son of the broad isle of Crete, <br> And says that in his wanderings he has passed <br> Through many cities of the world, for so <br> Some god ordained; and now, escaped by flight <br> From a Thesprotian galley, he has sought <br> A refuge in my lodge. Into thy hands <br> I give him; deal thou with him as thou wilt. <br> He is thy suppliant, and makes suit to thee.”</p>
<p class="verse">Then spake discreet Telemachus again: <br> “Eumaeus, thou hast uttered words that pierce <br> My heart with pain; for how can I receive <br> A stranger at my house? I am a youth <br> Who never yet has trusted in his arm <br> To beat the offerer of an insult back. <br> And in my mother’s mind the choice is yet <br> Uncertain whether to remain with me <br> The mistress of my household, keeping still <br> Her constant reverence for her husband’s bed, <br> And still obedient to the people’s voice, <br> Or whether she shall follow as a bride <br> Him of the Achaian suitors in my halls <br> Who is accounted worthiest, and who brings <br> The richest gifts. Now, as to this thy guest, <br> Since he has sought thy lodge, I give to him <br> A cloak and tunic, seemly of their kind, <br> A two-edged sword, and sandals for his feet. <br> And I will send him to whatever coast <br> He may desire to go. Yet, if thou wilt, <br> Lodge him beneath thy roof, and I will send <br> Raiment and food, that he may be no charge <br> To thee or thy companions. To my house <br> Among the suitor-train I cannot bear <br> That he should go. Those men are insolent <br> Beyond all measure; they would scoff at him, <br> And greatly should I grieve. The boldest man <br> Against so many might contend in vain, <br> And greater is their power by far than mine.”</p>
<p class="verse">Then spake Ulysses, the great sufferer: <br> “O friend⁠—since I have liberty to speak⁠— <br> My very heart is wounded when I hear <br> What wrongs the suitors practise in thy halls <br> Against a youth like thee. But give me leave <br> To ask if thou submittest willingly, <br> Or do thy people, hearkening to some god, <br> Hate thee with open hatred? Dost thou blame <br> Thy brothers?⁠—for in brothers men confide <br> Even in a desperate conflict. Would that I <br> Were young again, and with the will I have, <br> Or that I could become Ulysses’ son, <br> Or were that chief himself returned at last <br> From all his wanderings⁠—and there yet is hope <br> Of his return⁠—then might another strike <br> My head off if I would not instantly <br> Enter the house of Laertiades <br> And make myself a mischief to them all. <br> But should they overcome me, thus alone <br> Contending with such numbers, I would choose <br> Rather in mine own palace to be slain <br> Than every day behold such shameful deeds⁠— <br> Insulted guests, maidservants foully dragged <br> Through those fair palace chambers, wine-casks drained, <br> And gluttons feasting idly, wastefully, <br> And others toiling for them without end.”</p>
<p class="verse">Then spake again discreet Telemachus: <br> “Stranger, thou shalt be answered faithfully. <br> Know, then, the people are by no means wroth <br> With me, nor have I brothers to accuse, <br> Though in a desperate conflict men rely <br> Upon a brother’s aid. Saturnian Jove <br> Confines our lineage to a single head. <br> The king Arcesius had an only son, <br> Laertes, and to him was only born <br> Ulysses; and Ulysses left me here, <br> The only scion of his house, and he <br> Had little joy of me. Our halls are filled <br> With enemies, the chief men of the isles⁠— <br> Dulichium, Samos, and Zacynthus dark <br> With forests, and the rugged Ithaca⁠— <br> So many woo my mother and consume <br> Our substance. She rejects not utterly <br> Their hateful suit, nor yet will give consent <br> And end it. They go on to waste my wealth, <br> And soon will end me also; but the event <br> Rests with the gods.⁠—And go thou now with speed, <br> Eumaeus, father, to Penelope, <br> And say that I am safe, and just returned <br> From Pylos. I remain within the lodge. <br> And then come back as soon as thou hast told <br> The queen alone. Let none of all the Greeks <br> Hear aught; for they are plotting harm to me.”</p>
<p class="verse">Then thus, Eumaeus, thou didst make reply: <br> “Enough, I see it all, thy words are said <br> To one who understands them. But, I pray, <br> Direct me whether in my way to take <br> A message to Laertes, the distressed. <br> While sorrowing for Ulysses he o’ersaw <br> The labors of the field, and ate and drank, <br> As he had appetite, with those who wrought. <br> But since thy voyage to the Pylian coast <br> They say he never takes his daily meals <br> As he was wont, nor oversees the work, <br> But sits and mourns and sighs and pines away, <br> Until his limbs are shrivelled to the bone.”</p>
<p class="verse">Then spake discreet Telemachus again: <br> “ ’Tis sad, but we must leave him to his grief <br> A little while. Could everything be made <br> To happen as we mortals wish, I then <br> Would first desire my father’s safe return. <br> But thou, when thou hast given thy message, haste <br> Hither again, nor wander through the fields <br> To him; but let my mother send at once <br> The matron of her household, privately, <br> To bear the tidings to the aged man.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake to speed the swineherd, who took up <br> His sandals, bound them on, and bent his way <br> Townward. Not unperceived by Pallas went <br> Eumaeus from the lodge. She came in shape <br> A woman beautiful and stately, skilled <br> In household arts, the noblest. Near the gate <br> She stood, right opposite. Ulysses saw; <br> Telemachus beheld her not; the gods <br> Not always manifest themselves to all. <br> Ulysses and the mastiffs saw; the dogs <br> Barked not, but, whimpering, fled from her and sought <br> The stalls within. She beckoned with her brows; <br> Ulysses knew her meaning and came forth, <br> And passed the great wall of the court, and there <br> Stood near to Pallas, who bespake him thus:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Son of Laertes, nobly born and wise, <br> Speak with thy son; conceal from him the truth <br> No longer, that, prepared to make an end <br> Of that vile suitor-crew, ye may go up <br> Into the royal town. Nor long will I <br> Be absent; I am ready for the assault.”</p>
<p class="verse">Thus spake the goddess. Putting forth a wand <br> Of gold, she touched the chief. Beneath that touch <br> His breast was covered with a new-blanched robe <br> And tunic. To his frame it gave new strength; <br> His swarthy color came again, his cheeks <br> Grew full, and the beard darkened on his chin. <br> This done, she disappeared. Ulysses came <br> Into the lodge again; his son beheld <br> Amazed and overawed, and turned his eyes <br> Away, as if in presence of a god, <br> And thus bespake the chief with winged words:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“O stranger, thou art other than thou wert; <br> Thy garb is not the same, nor are thy looks; <br> Thou surely art some deity of those <br> Whose habitation is the ample heaven. <br> Be gracious to us, let us bring to thee <br> Such sacrifices as thou wilt accept <br> And gifts of graven gold; be merciful.”</p>
<p class="verse">Ulysses, the great sufferer, thus replied: <br> “I am no god; how am I like the gods? <br> I am thy father, he for whom thy sighs <br> Are breathed, and sorrows borne, and wrongs endured.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake and kissed his son, and from his lids <br> Tears fell to earth, that long had been restrained. <br> And then Telemachus, who could not think <br> The stranger was his father, answered thus:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Nay, thou art not my father, thou art not <br> Ulysses; rather hath some deity <br> Sought to deceive me, that my grief may be <br> The sharper; for no mortal man would do <br> What has been done, unless some god should come <br> To aid him, and to make him young or old <br> At pleasure; for thou wert a moment since <br> An aged man, and sordidly arrayed, <br> And now art like the gods of the wide heaven.”</p>
<p class="verse">Ulysses, the sagacious, answered thus: <br> “It is not well, Telemachus, to greet <br> With boundless wonder and astonishment <br> Thy father in this lodge. Be sure of this, <br> That no Ulysses other than myself <br> Will ever enter here. I, who am he, <br> Have suffered greatly and have wandered far, <br> And in the twentieth year am come again <br> To mine own land. Thou hast beheld today <br> A wonder wrought by Pallas, huntress-queen, <br> Who makes me what she will, such power is hers⁠— <br> Sometimes to seem a beggar, and in turn <br> A young man in a comely garb. The gods <br> Whose home is in the heavens can easily <br> Exalt a mortal man, or bring him low.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake and sat him down. Telemachus <br> Around his glorious father threw his arms, <br> And shed a shower of tears. Both felt at heart <br> A passionate desire to weep; they wept <br> Aloud⁠—and louder were their cries than those <br> Of eagles, or the sharp-clawed vulture tribe, <br> Whose young the hinds have stolen, yet unfledged. <br> Still flowed their tears abundantly; the sun <br> Would have gone down and left them weeping still <br> Had not Telemachus at length inquired:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Dear father, tell me in what galley came <br> The mariners who brought thee. Of what race <br> Claim they to be? For certainly, I think, <br> Thou cam’st not hither travelling on foot.”</p>
<p class="verse">Ulysses, the great sufferer, thus replied: <br> “My son, thou shalt be answered faithfully. <br> Men of a race renowned for seamanship, <br> Phaeacians, brought me hither. They convey <br> Abroad the strangers coming to their isle, <br> And, bearing me in one of their swift barques <br> Across the sea, they landed me asleep <br> In Ithaca. Rich were the gifts they gave⁠— <br> Much brass and gold, and garments from the loom; <br> These, so the gods have counselled, lie concealed <br> Among the hollow rocks, and I am come, <br> Obeying Pallas, to consult with thee <br> How to destroy our enemies. Give now <br> The number of the suitors; let me know <br> How many there may be, and who they are, <br> That with a careful judgment I may weigh <br> The question whether we shall fall on them⁠— <br> We two alone⁠—or must we seek allies.”</p>
<p class="verse">Then spake discreet Telemachus again: <br> “O father, I have heard of thy great fame <br> My whole life long⁠—how mighty is thy arm, <br> How wise thy counsels. Thou hast said great things, <br> And I am thunderstruck. It cannot be <br> That two alone should stand before a crowd <br> Of valiant men. They are not merely ten⁠— <br> These suitors⁠—nor twice ten, but many more; <br> Hear, then, their number. From Dulichium come <br> Fifty and two, the flower of all its youth, <br> With whom are six attendants. Samos sends <br> Twice twelve, and twenty more Achaian chiefs <br> Come from Zacynthus. Twelve from Ithaca; <br> The noblest of the isle are these⁠—with whom <br> Medon the herald comes⁠—a bard whose song <br> Is heavenly⁠—and two servants skilled to spread <br> The banquet. Should we in the palace halls <br> Assault all these, I fear lest the revenge <br> For all thy wrongs would end most bitterly <br> And grievously for thee. Now, if thy thought <br> Be turned to some ally, bethink thee who <br> Will combat for us with a willing heart.”</p>
<p class="verse">Again Ulysses, the great sufferer, spake: <br> “Then will I tell thee; listen, and give heed. <br> Think whether Pallas and her father, Jove, <br> Suffice not for us. Need we more allies?”</p>
<p class="verse">And then discreet Telemachus rejoined: <br> “Assuredly the twain whom thou hast named <br> Are mighty as allies; for though they sit <br> On high among the clouds, they yet bear rule <br> Both o’er mankind and o’er the living gods.”</p>
<p class="verse">Once more Ulysses, the great sufferer, spake: <br> “Not long will they avoid the fierce affray <br> When in my halls the strength of war is tried <br> Between me and the suitor crew. Now go <br> With early morning to thy home, and there <br> Mingle among the suitors. As for me, <br> The swineherd afterward shall lead me hence <br> To town, a wretched beggar seemingly, <br> And very old. If there they scoff at me <br> In mine own palace, let thy faithful heart <br> Endure it, though I suffer; though they seize <br> My feet, and by them drag me to the door, <br> Or strike at me with weapon-blades, look on <br> And bear it; yet reprove with gentle words <br> Their folly. They will never heed reproof; <br> The day of their destruction is at hand. <br> And this I tell thee further, and be sure <br> To keep my words in memory. As soon <br> As Pallas, goddess of wise counsel, gives <br> The warning, I shall nod to thee, and thou, <br> When thou perceivest it, remove at once <br> All weapons from my halls to a recess <br> High in an upper chamber. With soft words <br> Quiet the suitors when they ask thee why. <br> Say, ‘I would take them where there comes no smoke, <br> Since now they seem no longer like to those <br> Left by Ulysses when he sailed for Troy, <br> But soiled and tarnished by the breath of fire. <br> This graver reason, also, Saturn’s son <br> Hath forced upon my mind⁠—that ye by chance, <br> When full of wine and quarrelling, may wound <br> Each other, and disgrace the feast, and bring <br> Shame on your wooing; for the sight of steel <br> Draws men to bloodshed.’ Say but this, and leave <br> Two swords for us, two spears, two oxhide shields, <br> Against the day of combat. Pallas then, <br> And Jove the All-disposer, will unman <br> Their hearts. Moreover, let me say to thee⁠— <br> And keep my words in memory⁠—if thou be <br> My son, and of my blood, let no man hear <br> That now Ulysses is within the isle; <br> Let not Laertes hear of it, nor him <br> Who keeps the swine, nor any of the train <br> Of servants, nor Penelope herself, <br> While thou and I alone search out and prove <br> The women of the household, and no less <br> The serving-men, to know who honors us, <br> And bears us reverence in his heart, and who <br> Contemns us, and dishonors even thee.”</p>
<p class="verse">Then answered his illustrious son and said: <br> “Father, thou yet wilt know my heart, and find <br> That of a careless and too easy mood <br> I am not; but a search like this, I think, <br> Would profit neither of us, and I pray <br> That thou wilt well consider it. Long time <br> Wouldst thou go wandering from place to place, <br> O’er thy estates, to prove the loyalty <br> Of everyone, while in thy halls at ease <br> The suitors wastefully consume thy wealth. <br> Yet would I counsel that the women’s faith <br> Be proved, that the disloyal may be marked <br> And the innocent go free. As for the men, <br> I would not now inquire from farm to farm; <br> That may be done hereafter, if indeed <br> Thou hast a sign from aegis-bearing Jove.”</p>
<p class="verse">So talked they with each other. The good ship <br> Which brought Telemachus and all his friends <br> From Pylos kept meantime upon its way <br> To Ithaca. There, entering the deep port, <br> The seamen hauled the black ship up the beach; <br> And then the ready servants took away <br> The arms, and to the house of Clytius bore <br> The costly gifts. A herald from the ship <br> Went forward to the palace of the king <br> With tidings to the sage Penelope <br> That now her son was come and in the fields, <br> And that the ship at his command had reached <br> The city, lest the royal dame might feel <br> Fear for his safety, and give way to tears. <br> The herald and the noble swineherd met, <br> Each bearing the same message to the queen. <br> Entering the palace of the godlike king, <br> And standing midst the maids, the herald said:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“O lady, thy beloved son is come.” <br> But close beside the queen the swineherd stood, <br> And told her everything which her dear son <br> Had bid him say; and, having thus fulfilled <br> His errand, left the palace and its court.</p>
<p class="verse">Then were the suitors vexed and sorrowful, <br> And going from the palace, and without <br> The great wall that enclosed the court, sat down <br> Before the gates, and there Eurymachus, <br> The son of Polybus, harangued the throng:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Behold, my friends, Telemachus has done <br> A marvellous thing; this voyage, which we thought <br> He could not make, is made. Now let us launch <br> A ship, the best that we can find, and man <br> With fishermen the benches, sending it <br> To find our friends, and hasten their return.”</p>
<p class="verse">Scarce had he spoken when Amphinomus, <br> In turning where he stood, beheld a barque <br> Enter the port’s deep waters, with a crew <br> That furled the sails and held the oars in hand. <br> He laughed, well pleased, and to the suitors said:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“There needs no message to be sent, for they <br> Are here already. Haply hath some god <br> Given them the knowledge, or perchance they saw, <br> But could not overtake, the prince’s ship.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake; they rose and hastened to the strand, <br> And quickly drew the galley up the beach. <br> The ready servants bore the arms away; <br> Then met they all in council, suffering none <br> Save of the suitor-train to meet with them⁠— <br> None, either young or old. Eupeithes’ son, <br> Antinoüs, standing forth, bespake them thus:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“How strangely do the gods protect this man <br> From evil! All day long spy after spy <br> Has sat and watched upon the airy heights, <br> And when the sun was set we never slept <br> On land, but ever in our gallant ship <br> Sailed, waiting for the holy morn, and lay <br> In constant ambush for Telemachus, <br> To seize and to destroy him. Yet behold, <br> Some deity has brought him home. And now <br> Frame we a plan to cut off utterly <br> Telemachus, and leave him no escape; <br> For certainly I think that while he lives <br> The end we aim at cannot be attained. <br> Shrewd is the youth in counsel and device, <br> And we no longer have, as once we had, <br> The people’s favor. Let us quickly act, <br> Ere he can call a council of the Greeks. <br> That he will do without delay, and there <br> Will rise in wrath to tell them how we planned <br> His death by violence, and failed; and they <br> Who hear assuredly will not approve <br> The plotted mischief. They may drive us forth <br> With outrage from our country to a land <br> Of strangers. Let us be the first to strike, <br> And slay him in the fields or on the way, <br> And, taking his possessions to ourselves, <br> Share equally his wealth. Then may we give <br> This palace to his mother, and the man <br> Whom she shall wed, whoever he may be. <br> Or if this plan mislike you, and ye choose <br> That he should live, and keep the fair estate <br> That was his father’s, let us not go on <br> Thronging the palace to consume his wealth <br> In revelry, but each with liberal gifts <br> Woo her from his own dwelling; and let him <br> Who gives most generously, and whom fate <br> Most favors, take the lady as his bride.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake, and all were mute. Amphinomus, <br> The illustrious son of royal Nisus, rose. <br> The grandson of Aretias, it was he <br> Who led the suitors from Dulichium’s fields, <br> Grassy and rich in corn. Penelope <br> Liked best his words, for generous was his thought, <br> And with a generous purpose thus he spake:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Nay, friends, not mine is the advice to slay <br> Telemachus. It is a fearful thing <br> To take a royal life. Then let us first <br> Inquire the pleasure of the gods. For if <br> The oracles of mighty Jupiter <br> Approve it, I would do the deed myself, <br> Or bid another do it; but if they <br> Consent not, ’tis my counsel to forbear.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake, and all approved. At once they rose, <br> And, entering the palace, sat them down <br> On shining thrones. Meantime Penelope <br> Had formed the purpose to appear before <br> The arrogant suitors, for the news was brought <br> Into her chamber of the plot to slay <br> Her son; the herald Medon overheard, <br> And told her all. So to the hall she went <br> With her attendant maids. The glorious dame <br> Drew near the suitor-train, and took her stand <br> Beside a column of the stately pile, <br> And with a delicate veil before her cheeks <br> Began to speak, and chid Antinoüs thus:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Antinoüs, mischief-plotter, insolent! <br> The rumor is that thou excellest all <br> Of thy own age among the Ithacans <br> In understanding and in speech. Yet such <br> Thou never wert. Ferocious as thou art, <br> Why seek the death of my Telemachus, <br> And treat with scorn the suppliants of whose prayer <br> Jove is the witness? An unholy thing <br> It is when men against their fellow-men <br> Plot mischief. Dost thou then forget that once <br> Thy father came to us a fugitive, <br> In terror of the people, who were wroth <br> Because he joined the Taphian pirate-race, <br> And plundered the Thesprotians, our allies. <br> The people would have slain him, and have torn <br> His heart out, and have pillaged his large wealth; <br> Ulysses checked their rage, and held them back, <br> Fierce as they were. Now thou dost waste his goods <br> Most shamefully, and woo his wife, and slay <br> His son, and multiply my woes. Cease now, <br> I charge thee, and persuade the rest to cease.”</p>
<p class="verse">Eurymachus, the son of Polybus, <br> Replied: “O daughter of Icarius, sage <br> Penelope, take heart; let no such thought <br> Possess thy mind. There is no man on earth, <br> Nor will there be, who shall lay violent hands <br> Upon Telemachus, thy son, while I <br> Am living, and yet keep the gift of sight. <br> I say, and will perform it⁠—his black blood <br> Shall flow and bathe my spear. Ulysses oft, <br> Spoiler of realms, would take me on his knee, <br> And put the roasted meats into my hands, <br> And give me ruddy wine. I therefore hold <br> Telemachus of all mankind most dear, <br> And I will bid him have no fear of death <br> From any of the suitors. If it come, <br> Sent by the gods, he cannot then escape.”</p>
<p class="verse">So spake he to appease her, while he planned, <br> The murder of her son. The queen went up <br> To the fair upper chambers, and there wept <br> Ulysses, her dear spouse, till o’er her lids <br> The blue-eyed Pallas poured the balm of sleep.</p>
<p class="verse">At evening to Ulysses and his son <br> The noble swineherd went, while busily <br> They made the supper ready, having slain <br> A porker one year old. Then instantly <br> Stood Pallas by Ulysses, and put forth <br> Her wand and touched him, making him again <br> Old, and clad sordidly in beggar’s weeds, <br> Lest that the swineherd, knowing at a look <br> His master, might not keep the knowledge locked <br> In his own breast, but, hastening forth, betray <br> The secret to the chaste Penelope.</p>
<p class="verse">Then to the swineherd said Telemachus: <br> “Noble Eumaeus, welcome; what reports <br> Are in the town? Have those large-minded men, <br> The suitors, left their ambush and returned, <br> Or are they waiting yet for me to pass?”</p>
<p class="verse">And thus, Eumaeus, thou didst make reply: <br> “Of that, indeed, I never thought to ask, <br> In going through the town. My only care <br> Was to return, as soon as I had given <br> My message, with such speed as I could make. <br> I met a messenger, a herald sent <br> By thy companions, who was first to tell <br> Thy mother of thy safe return. Yet this <br> I know, for I beheld it with my eyes. <br> When outside of the city, where the hill <br> Of Hermes stands, I saw a gallant barque <br> Entering the port, and carrying many men. <br> Heavy it was with shields and two-edged spears; <br> ’Twas they, I thought, and yet I cannot tell.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake; Telemachus the valiant looked <br> Upon his father with a smile unmarked <br> By good Eumaeus. When their task was done, <br> And the board spread, they feasted. No one lacked <br> His portion of the common meal. Their thirst <br> And hunger satisfied, they laid them down <br> To rest, and so received the gift of sleep.</p>
