<p class="lede">The adventures of Ulysses after the fall of Troy related by him at the request of Alcinoüs⁠—His attack on the Ciconians and the destruction of their city⁠—Rally and reinforcement of the Ciconians, who slaughter many of the companions of Ulysses⁠—The lotus-eaters, who subsist on flowers⁠—Arrival of Ulysses at the land of the cyclops⁠—Polyphemus and his barbarities⁠—Revenge of Ulysses, who puts out the single eye of Polyphemus and escapes.</p>
<p class="verse">Ulysses, the sagacious, answered thus:⁠— <br> “O King Alcinoüs, most renowned of men! <br> A pleasant thing it is to hear a bard <br> Like this, endowed with such a voice, so like <br> The voices of the gods. Nor can I deem <br> Aught more delightful than the general joy <br> Of a whole people when the assembled guests <br> Seated in order in the royal halls <br> Are listening to the minstrel, while the board <br> Is spread with bread and meats, and from the jars <br> The cupbearer draws wine and fills the cups. <br> To me there is no more delightful sight.</p>
<p class="verse">“But now thy mind is moved to ask of me <br> The story of the sufferings I have borne, <br> And that will wake my grief anew. What first, <br> What next, shall I relate? what last of all? <br> For manifold are the misfortunes cast <br> Upon me by the immortals. Let me first <br> Declare my name, that ye may know, and I <br> Perchance, before my day of death shall come, <br> May be your host, though dwelling far away. <br> I am Ulysses, and my father’s name <br> Laertes; widely am I known to men <br> As quick in shrewd devices, and my fame <br> Hath reached to heaven. In sunny Ithaca <br> I dwell, where high Neritus, seen afar, <br> Rustles with woods. Around are many isles, <br> Well peopled, near each other. Samos there <br> Lies, with Dulichium, and Zacynthus dark <br> With forests. Ithaca, with its low shores, <br> Lies highest toward the setting sun; the rest <br> Are on the side where first the morning breaks. <br> A rugged region ’tis, but nourishes <br> Nobly its youths, nor have I ever seen <br> A sweeter spot on earth. Calypso late, <br> That glorious goddess, in her grotto long <br> Detained me from it, and desired that I <br> Should be her husband; in her royal home <br> Aeëan Circè, mistress of strange arts, <br> Detained me also, and desired that I <br> Should be her husband⁠—yet they could not move <br> The purpose of my heart. For there is naught <br> More sweet and dear than our own native land <br> And parents, though perchance our lot be cast <br> In a rich home, yet far from our own kin <br> And in a foreign land. Now let me speak <br> Of the calamitous voyage which the will <br> Of Jove ordained on my return from Troy.</p>
<p class="verse">“The wind that blew me from the Trojan shore <br> Bore me to the Ciconians, who abode <br> In Ismarus. I laid the city waste <br> And slew its dwellers, carried off their wives <br> And all their wealth and parted them among <br> My men, that none might want an equal share. <br> And then I warned them with all haste to leave <br> The region. Madmen! they obeyed me not.</p>
<p class="verse">“And there they drank much wine, and on the beach <br> Slew many sheep and many slow-paced steers <br> With crumpled horns. Then the Ciconians called <br> To their Ciconian neighbors, braver men <br> Than they, and more in number, whose abode <br> Was on the mainland, trained to fight from steeds, <br> Or, if need were, on foot. In swarms they came, <br> Thick as new leaves or morning flowers in spring. <br> Then fell on our unhappy company <br> An evil fate from Jove, and many griefs. <br> They formed their lines, and fought at our good ships, <br> Where man encountered man with brazen spears. <br> While yet ’twas morning, and the holy light <br> Of day waxed brighter, we withstood the assault <br> And kept our ground, although more numerous they. <br> But when the sun was sloping toward the west <br> The enemy prevailed; the Achaian band <br> Was routed, and was made to flee. That day <br> There perished from each galley of our fleet <br> Six valiant men; the rest escaped with life.</p>
<p class="verse">“Onward we sailed, lamenting bitterly <br> Our comrades slain, yet happy to escape <br> From death ourselves. Nor did we put to sea <br> In our good ships until we thrice had called <br> Aloud by name each one of our poor friends <br> Who fell in battle by Ciconian hands. <br> The Cloud-compeller, Jove, against us sent <br> The north-wind in a hurricane, and wrapped <br> The earth and heaven in clouds, and from the skies <br> Fell suddenly the night. With stooping masts <br> Our galleys scudded; the strong tempest split <br> And tore the sails; we drew and laid them down <br> Within the ships, in fear of utter wreck, <br> And toward the mainland eagerly we turned <br> The rudders. There we lay two days and nights, <br> Worn out with grief and hardship. When at length <br> The fair-haired Morning brought the third day round, <br> We raised the masts, and, spreading the white sails <br> To take the wind, we sat us down. The wind <br> Carried us forward with the pilot’s aid; <br> And then should I have reached my native land <br> Safely, had not the currents and the waves <br> Of ocean and the north-wind driven me back, <br> What time I strove to pass Maleia’s cape, <br> And swept me to Cytherae from my course.</p>
<p class="verse">“Still onward driven before those baleful winds <br> Across the fishy deep for nine whole days, <br> On the tenth day we reached the land where dwell <br> The Lotus-eaters, men whose food is flowers. <br> We landed on the mainland, and our crews <br> Near the fleet galleys took their evening meal. <br> And when we all had eaten and had drunk <br> I sent explorers forth⁠—two chosen men, <br> A herald was the third⁠—to learn what race <br> Of mortals nourished by the fruits of earth <br> Possessed the land. They went and found themselves <br> Among the Lotus-eaters soon, who used <br> No violence against their lives, but gave <br> Into their hands the lotus plant to taste. <br> Whoever tasted once of that sweet food <br> Wished not to see his native country more, <br> Nor give his friends the knowledge of his fate. <br> And then my messengers desired to dwell <br> Among the Lotus-eaters, and to feed <br> Upon the lotus, never to return. <br> By force I led them weeping to the fleet, <br> And bound them in the hollow ships beneath <br> The benches. Then I ordered all the rest <br> Of my beloved comrades to embark <br> In haste, lest, tasting of the lotus, they <br> Should think no more of home. All straightway went <br> On board, and on the benches took their place, <br> And smote the hoary ocean with their oars.</p>
<p class="verse">“Onward we sailed with sorrowing hearts, and reached <br> The country of the Cyclops, an untamed <br> And lawless race, who, trusting to the gods, <br> Plant not, nor plough the fields, but all things spring <br> For them untended⁠—barley, wheat, and vines <br> Yielding large clusters filled with wine, and nursed <br> By showers from Jove. No laws have they; they hold <br> No councils. On the mountain heights they dwell <br> In vaulted caves, where each one rules his wives <br> And children as he pleases; none give heed <br> To what the others do. Before the port <br> Of that Cyclopean land there is an isle, <br> Low-lying, neither near nor yet remote⁠— <br> A woodland region, where the wild goats breed <br> Innumerable; for the foot of man <br> Disturbs them not, and huntsmen toiling through <br> Thick woods, or wandering over mountain heights, <br> Enter not here. The fields are never grazed <br> By sheep, nor furrowed by the plough, but lie <br> Untilled, unsown, and uninhabited <br> By man, and only feed the bleating goats. <br> The Cyclops have no barques with crimson prows, <br> Nor shipwrights skilled to frame a galley’s deck <br> With benches for the rowers, and equipped <br> For any service, voyaging by turns <br> To all the cities, as is often done <br> By men who cross the deep from place to place, <br> And make a prosperous region of an isle. <br> No meagre soil is there; it well might bear <br> All fruits in their due time. Along the shore <br> Of the gray deep are meadows smooth and moist. <br> The vine would flourish long; the ploughman’s task <br> Is easy, and the husbandman would reap <br> Large harvests, for the mould is rich below. <br> And there is a safe haven, where no need <br> Of cable is; no anchor there is cast, <br> Nor hawsers fastened to the strand, but they <br> Who enter there remain until it please <br> The mariners, with favorable wind, <br> To put to sea again. A limpid stream <br> Flows from a fount beneath a hollow rock <br> Into that harbor at its further end, <br> And poplars grow around it. Thither went <br> Our fleet; some deity had guided us <br> Through the dark night, for nothing had we seen. <br> Thick was the gloom around our barques; the moon <br> Shone not in heaven, the clouds had quenched her light. <br> No eye discerned the isle, nor the long waves <br> That rolled against the shore, till our good ships <br> Touched land, and, disembarking there, we gave <br> Ourselves to sleep upon the waterside <br> And waited for the holy Morn to rise.</p>
<p class="verse">“And when at length the daughter of the Dawn, <br> The rosy-fingered Morn, appeared, we walked <br> Around the isle, admiring as we went. <br> Meanwhile the nymphs, the daughters of the God <br> Who bears the aegis, roused the mountain goats, <br> That so our crews might make their morning meal. <br> And straightway from our ships we took in hand <br> Our crooked bows and our long-bladed spears.</p>
<p class="verse">“ ‘Let all the rest of my beloved friends <br> Remain, while I, with my own barque and crew, <br> Go forth to learn what race of men are these, <br> Whether ill-mannered, savage, and unjust, <br> Or kind to guests and reverent toward the gods.’ <br> “I spake, and, having ordered all my crew <br> To go on board and cast the hawsers loose, <br> Embarked on my own ship. They all obeyed, <br> And manned the benches, sitting there in rows, <br> And smote the hoary ocean with their oars. <br> But when we came upon that neighboring coast, <br> We saw upon its verge beside the sea <br> A cave high vaulted, overbrowed with shrubs <br> Of laurel. There much cattle lay at rest, <br> Both sheep and goats. Around it was a court, <br> A high enclosure of hewn stone, and pines <br> Tall stemmed, and towering oaks. Here dwelt a man <br> Of giant bulk, who by himself, alone, <br> Was wont to tend his flocks. He never held <br> Converse with others, but devised apart <br> His wicked deeds. A frightful prodigy <br> Was he, and like no man who lives by bread, <br> But more like a huge mountain summit, rough <br> With woods, that towers alone above the rest.</p>
<p class="verse">“Then, bidding all the others stay and guard <br> The ship, I chose among my bravest men <br> Twelve whom I took with me. I had on board <br> A goatskin of dark wine⁠—a pleasant sort, <br> Which Maron late, Evanthes’ son, a priest <br> Of Phoebus, guardian god of Ismarus, <br> Gave me, when, moved with reverence, we saved <br> Him and his children and his wife from death. <br> For his abode was in the thick-grown grove <br> Of Phoebus. Costly were the gifts he gave⁠— <br> Seven talents of wrought gold; a chalice all <br> Of silver; and he drew for me, besides, <br> Into twelve jars, a choice rich wine, unspoiled <br> By mixtures, and a beverage for gods. <br> No one within his dwellings, maids or men, <br> Knew of it, save the master and his wife, <br> And matron of the household. Whensoe’er <br> They drank this rich red wine, he only filled <br> A single cup with wine, and tempered that <br> With twenty more of water. From the cup <br> Arose a fragrance that might please the gods, <br> And hard it was to put the draught aside. <br> Of this I took a skin well filled, besides <br> Food in a hamper⁠—for my thoughtful mind <br> Misgave me, lest I should encounter one <br> Of formidable strength and savage mood, <br> And with no sense of justice or of right.</p>
<p class="verse">“Soon were we at the cave, but found not him <br> Within it; he was in the fertile meads, <br> Tending his flocks. We entered, wondering much <br> At all we saw. Around were baskets heaped <br> With cheeses; pens were thronged with lambs and kids, <br> Each in a separate fold; the elder ones, <br> The younger, and the newly yeaned, had each <br> Their place apart. The vessels swam with whey⁠— <br> Pails smoothly wrought, and buckets into which <br> He milked the cattle. My companions then <br> Begged me with many pressing words to take <br> Part of the cheeses, and, returning, drive <br> With speed to our good galley lambs and kids <br> From where they stabled, and set sail again <br> On the salt sea. I granted not their wish; <br> Far better if I had. ’Twas my intent <br> To see the owner of the flocks and prove <br> His hospitality. No pleasant sight <br> Was that to be for those with whom I came.</p>
<p class="verse">“And then we lit a fire, and sacrificed, <br> And ate the cheeses, and within the cave <br> Sat waiting, till from pasturing his flocks <br> He came; a heavy load of well-dried wood <br> He bore, to make a blaze at suppertime. <br> Without the den he flung his burden down <br> With such a crash that we in terror slunk <br> Into a corner of the cave. He drove <br> His well-fed flock, all those whose milk he drew, <br> Under that spacious vault of rock, but left <br> The males, both goats and rams, without the court. <br> And then he lifted a huge barrier up, <br> A mighty weight; not two-and-twenty wains, <br> Four-wheeled and strong, could move it from the ground: <br> Such was the enormous rock he raised, and placed <br> Against the entrance. Then he sat and milked <br> The ewes and bleating goats each one in turn, <br> And gave to each its young. Next, half the milk <br> He caused to curdle, and disposed the curd <br> In woven baskets; and the other half <br> He kept in bowls to be his evening drink. <br> His tasks all ended thus, he lit a fire, <br> And saw us where we lurked, and questioned us:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“ ‘Who are ye, strangers? Tell me whence ye came <br> Across the ocean. Are ye men of trade, <br> Or wanderers at will, like those who roam <br> The sea for plunder, and, with their own lives <br> In peril, carry death to distant shores?’</p>
<p class="verse">“He spake, and we who heard with sinking hearts <br> Trembled at that deep voice and frightful form, <br> And thus I answered: ‘We are Greeks who come <br> From Ilium, driven across the mighty deep <br> By changing winds, and while we sought our home <br> Have made a different voyage, and been forced <br> Upon another course; such was the will <br> Of Jupiter. We boast ourselves to be <br> Soldiers of Agamemnon, Atreus’ son, <br> Whose fame is now the greatest under heaven, <br> So mighty was the city which he sacked, <br> So many were the warriors whom he slew; <br> And now we come as suppliants to thy knees, <br> And ask thee to receive us as thy guests, <br> Or else bestow the gifts which custom makes <br> The stranger’s due. Great as thou art, revere <br> The gods; for suitors to thy grace are we, <br> And hospitable Jove, whose presence goes <br> With every worthy stranger, will avenge <br> Suppliants and strangers when they suffer wrong.’</p>
<p class="verse">“I spake, and savagely he answered me:⁠— <br> ‘Thou art a fool, O stranger, or art come <br> From some far country⁠—thou who biddest me <br> Fear or regard the gods. We little care⁠— <br> We Cyclops⁠—for the Aegis-bearer, Jove, <br> Or any other of the blessed gods; <br> We are their betters. Think not I would spare <br> Thee or thy comrades to avoid the wrath <br> Of Jupiter, unless it were my choice; <br> But say⁠—for I would know⁠—where hast thou left <br> Thy gallant barque in landing? was it near, <br> Or in some distant corner of the isle?’</p>
<p class="verse">“He spake to tempt me, but I well perceived <br> His craft, and answered with dissembling words:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“ ‘Neptune, who shakes the shores, hath wrecked my barque <br> On rocks that edge thine island, hurling it <br> Against the headland. From the open sea <br> The tempest swept it hitherward, and I, <br> With these, escaped the bitter doom of death.’</p>
<p class="verse">“I spake; the savage answered not, but sprang, <br> And, laying hands on my companions, seized <br> Two, whom he dashed like whelps against the ground. <br> Their brains flowed out, and weltered where they fell. <br> He hewed them limb from limb for his repast, <br> And, like a lion of the mountain wilds, <br> Devoured them as they were, and left no part⁠— <br> Entrails nor flesh nor marrowy bones. We wept <br> To see his cruelties, and raised our hands <br> To Jove, and hopeless misery filled our hearts. <br> And when the Cyclops now had filled himself, <br> Devouring human flesh, and drinking milk <br> Unmingled, in his cave he laid him down, <br> Stretched out amid his flocks. The thought arose <br> In my courageous heart to go to him, <br> And draw the trenchant sword upon my thigh, <br> And where the midriff joins the liver deal <br> A stroke to pierce his breast. A second thought <br> Restrained me⁠—that a miserable death <br> Would overtake us, since we had no power <br> To move the mighty rock which he had laid <br> At the high opening. So all night we grieved, <br> Waiting the holy Morn; and when at length <br> That rosy-fingered daughter of the Dawn <br> Appeared, the Cyclops lit a fire, and milked <br> His fair flock one by one, and brought their young <br> Each to its mother’s side. When he had thus <br> Performed his household tasks, he seized again <br> Two of our number for his morning meal. <br> These he devoured, and then he moved away <br> With ease the massive rock that closed the cave, <br> And, driving forth his well-fed flock, he laid <br> The massive barrier back, as one would fit <br> The lid upon a quiver. With loud noise <br> The Cyclops drove that well-fed flock afield, <br> While I was left to think of many a plan <br> To do him mischief and avenge our wrongs, <br> If haply Pallas should confer on me <br> That glory. To my mind, as I revolved <br> The plans, this seemed the wisest of them all.</p>
<p class="verse">“Beside the stalls there lay a massive club <br> Of olive-wood, yet green, which from its stock <br> The Cyclops hewed, that he might carry it <br> When seasoned. As it lay it seemed to us <br> The mast of some black galley, broad of beam, <br> With twenty oarsmen, built to carry freight <br> Across the mighty deep⁠—such was its length <br> And thickness. Standing by it, I cut off <br> A fathom’s length, and gave it to my men, <br> And bade them smooth its sides, and they obeyed <br> While I made sharp the smaller end, and brought <br> The point to hardness in the glowing fire; <br> And then I hid the weapon in a heap <br> Of litter, which lay thick about the cave. <br> I bade my comrades now decide by lot <br> Which of them all should dare, along with me, <br> To lift the stake, and with its point bore out <br> Our enemy’s eye, when softly wrapped in sleep. <br> The lot was cast, and fell on those whom most <br> I wished with me⁠—four men, and I the fifth.</p>
<p class="verse">“At eve the keeper of these fair-woolled flocks <br> Returned, and brought his well-fed sheep and goats <br> Into the spacious cavern, leaving none <br> Without it, whether through some doubt of us <br> Or through the ordering of some god. He raised <br> The massive rock again, and laid it close <br> Against the opening. Then he sat and milked <br> The ewes and bleating goats, each one in turn, <br> And gave to each her young. When he had thus <br> Performed his household tasks, he seized again <br> Two of our number for his evening meal. <br> Then drew I near, and bearing in my hand <br> A wooden cup of dark red wine I said:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“ ‘Take this, O Cyclops, after thy repast <br> Of human flesh, and drink, that thou mayst know <br> What liquor was concealed within our ship. <br> I brought it as an offering to thee, <br> For I had hope that thou wouldst pity us, <br> And send us home. Yet are thy cruelties <br> Beyond all limit. Wicked as thou art, <br> Hereafter who, of all the human race, <br> Will dare approach thee, guilty of such wrong?’</p>
<p class="verse">“As thus I spake, he took the cup and drank. <br> The luscious wine delighted mightily <br> His palate, and he asked a second draught.</p>
<p class="verse">“ ‘Give me to drink again, and generously, <br> And tell thy name, that I may make a gift <br> Such as becomes a host. The fertile land <br> In which the Cyclops dwell yields wine, ’tis true, <br> And the large grapes are nursed by rains from Jove, <br> But nectar and ambrosia are in this.’</p>
<p class="verse">“He spake; I gave him of the generous juice <br> Again, and thrice I filled and brought the cup, <br> And thrice the Cyclops in his folly drank. <br> But when I saw the wine begin to cloud <br> His senses, I bespake him blandly thus:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“ ‘Thou hast inquired, O Cyclops, by what name <br> Men know me. I will tell thee, but do thou <br> Bestow in turn some hospitable gift, <br> As thou hast promised. Noman is my name, <br> My father and my mother gave it me, <br> And Noman am I called by all my friends.’</p>
<p class="verse">“I ended, and he answered savagely:⁠— <br> ‘Noman shall be the last of all his band <br> Whom I will eat, the rest will I devour <br> Before him. Let that respite be my gift.’</p>
<p class="verse">“He spake, and, sinking backward at full length, <br> Lay on the ground, with his huge neck aside; <br> All-powerful sleep had overtaken him. <br> Then from his mouth came bits of human flesh <br> Mingled with wine, and from his drunken throat <br> Rejected noisily. I put the stake <br> Among the glowing coals to gather heat, <br> And uttered cheerful words, encouraging <br> My men, that none might fail me through their fears. <br> And when the olive-wood began to blaze⁠— <br> For though yet green it freely took the lire⁠— <br> I drew it from the embers. Round me stood <br> My comrades, whom some deity inspired <br> With calm, high courage. In their hands they took <br> And thrust into his eye the pointed bar, <br> While perched upon a higher stand than they <br> I twirled it round. As when a workman bores <br> Some timber of a ship, the men who stand <br> Below him with a strap, on either side <br> Twirl it, and round it spins unceasingly, <br> So, thrusting in his eye that pointed bar, <br> We made it turn. The blood came streaming forth <br> On the hot wood; the eyelids and the brow <br> Were scalded by the vapor, and the roots <br> Of the scorched eyeball crackled with the fire. <br> As when a smith, in forging axe or adze, <br> Plunges, to temper it, the hissing blade <br> Into cold water, strengthening thus the steel, <br> So hissed the eyeball of the Cyclops round <br> That olive stake. He raised a fearful howl; <br> The rocks rang with it, and we fled from him <br> In terror. Plucking from his eye the stake <br> All foul and dripping with the abundant blood, <br> He flung it madly from him with both hands. <br> Then called he to the Cyclops who in grots <br> Dwelt on that breezy height. They heard his voice <br> And came by various ways, and stood beside <br> The cave, and asked the occasion of his grief.</p>
<p class="verse">“ ‘What hurts thee, Polyphemus, that thou thus <br> Dost break our slumbers in the ambrosial night <br> With cries? Hath any of the sons of men <br> Driven off thy flocks in spite of thee, or tried <br> By treachery or force to take thy life?’</p>
<p class="verse">“Huge Polyphemus answered from his den:⁠— <br> ‘O friends! ’tis Noman who is killing me; <br> By treachery Noman kills me; none by force’</p>
<p class="verse">“Then thus with winged words they spake again:⁠— <br> ‘If no man does thee violence, and thou <br> Art quite alone, reflect that none escape <br> Diseases; they are sent by Jove. But make <br> Thy prayer to Father Neptune, ocean’s king.’</p>
<p class="verse">“So spake they and departed. In my heart <br> I laughed to think that by the name I took, <br> And by my shrewd device, I had deceived <br> The Cyclops. Meantime, groaning and in pain, <br> And groping with his hands, he moved away <br> The rock that barred the entrance. There he sat, <br> With arms outstretched, to seize whoever sought <br> To issue from the cavern with the flock, <br> So dull of thought he deemed me. Then I planned <br> How best to save my comrades and myself <br> From death. I framed a thousand stratagems <br> And arts⁠—for here was life at stake, and great <br> The danger was. At last I fixed on this.</p>
<p class="verse">“The rams were plump and beautiful, and large <br> With thick dark fleeces. These I silently <br> Bound to each other, three and three, with twigs <br> Of which that prodigy of lawless guilt, <br> The Cyclops, made his bed. The middle ram <br> Of every three conveyed a man; the two, <br> One on each side, were there to make him safe. <br> Thus each of us was borne by three; but I <br> Chose for myself the finest one of all, <br> And seized him by the back, and, slipping down <br> Beneath his shaggy belly, stretched myself <br> At length, and clung with resolute heart, and hands <br> That firmly clenched the rich abundant fleece. <br> Then sighed we for the holy Morn to rise.</p>
<p class="verse">“And when again the daughter of the Dawn, <br> The rosy-fingered Morn, looked forth, the males <br> Went forth to pasture, while the ewes remained <br> Within the stables, bleating, yet unmilked, <br> For heavy were their udders. Carefully <br> The master handled, though in grievous pain, <br> The back of everyone that rose and passed, <br> Yet, slow of thought, perceived not that my men <br> Were clinging hid beneath their woolly breasts. <br> As the last ram of all the flock went out, <br> His thick fleece heavy with my weight, and I <br> In agitated thought, he felt his back, <br> And thus the giant Polyphemus spake:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“ ‘My favorite ram, how art thou now the last <br> To leave the cave? It hath not been thy wont <br> To let the sheep go first, but thou didst come <br> Earliest to feed among the flowery grass, <br> Walking with stately strides, and thou wert first <br> At the fresh stream, and first at eve to seek <br> The stable; now thou art the last of all. <br> Grievest thou for thy master, who has lost <br> His eye, put out by a deceitful wretch <br> And his vile crew, who stupefied me first <br> With wine⁠—this Noman⁠—who, if right I deem, <br> Has not escaped from death. O, didst thou think <br> As I do, and hadst but the power of speech <br> To tell me where he hides from my strong arm, <br> Then should his brains, dashed out against the ground, <br> Be scattered here and there; then should my heart <br> Be somewhat lighter, even amid the woes <br> Which Noman, worthless wretch, has brought on me!’</p>
<p class="verse">“He spake, and sent him forth among the rest; <br> And when we were a little way beyond <br> The cavern and the court, I loosed my hold <br> Upon the animal and unbound my men. <br> Then quickly we surrounded and drove off, <br> Fat sheep and stately paced, a numerous flock, <br> And brought them to our ship, where joyfully <br> Our friends received us, though with grief and tears <br> For those who perished. Yet I suffered not <br> That they should weep, but, frowning, gave command <br> By signs to lift with speed the fair-woolled sheep <br> On board, and launch our ship on the salt sea. <br> They went on board, where each one took his place <br> Upon the benches, and with diligent oars <br> Smote the gray deep; and when we were as far <br> As one upon the shore could hear a shout, <br> Thus to the Cyclops tauntingly I called:⁠— <br> “ ‘Ha! Cyclops! those whom in thy rocky cave <br> Thou, in thy brutal fury, hast devoured, <br> Were friends of one not unexpert in war; <br> Amply have thy own guilty deeds returned <br> Upon thee. Cruel one! who didst not fear <br> To eat the strangers sheltered by thy roof, <br> Jove and the other gods avenge them thus.’</p>
<p class="verse">“I spake; the anger in his bosom raged <br> More fiercely. From a mountain peak he wrenched <br> Its summit, hurling it to fall beside <br> Our galley, where it almost touched the helm. <br> The rock dashed high the water where it fell, <br> And the returning billow swept us back <br> And toward the shore. I seized a long-stemmed pike <br> And pushed it from the shore, encouraging <br> The men to bend with vigor to their oars <br> And so escape. With nods I gave the sign. <br> Forward to vigorous strokes the oarsmen leaned <br> Till we were out at sea as far from land <br> As when I spake before, and then again <br> I shouted to the Cyclops, though my crew <br> Strove to prevent it with beseeching words, <br> And one man first and then another said:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“ ‘O most unwise! why chafe that savage man <br> To fury⁠—him who just has cast his bolt <br> Into the sea, and forced us toward the land <br> Where we had well-nigh perished? Should he hear <br> A cry from us, or even a word of speech, <br> Then would he fling a rock to crush our heads <br> And wreck our ship, so fatal is his cast.’</p>
<p class="verse">“He spake, but moved not my courageous heart; <br> And then I spake again, and angrily:⁠— <br> “ ‘Cyclops, if any man of mortal birth <br> Note thine unseemly blindness, and inquire <br> The occasion, tell him that Laertes’ son, <br> Ulysses, the destroyer of walled towns, <br> Whose home is Ithaca, put out thine eye.’</p>
<p class="verse">“I spake; he answered with a wailing voice:⁠— <br> ‘Now, woe is me! the ancient oracles <br> Concerning me have come to pass. Here dwelt <br> A seer named Telemus Eurymides, <br> Great, good, and eminent in prophecy, <br> And prophesying he grew old among <br> The Cyclops. He foretold my coming fate⁠— <br> That I should lose my sight, and by the hand <br> And cunning of Ulysses. Yet I looked <br> For one of noble presence, mighty strength, <br> And giant stature landing on our coast. <br> Now a mere weakling, insignificant <br> And small of stature, has put out my eye, <br> First stupefying me with wine. Yet come <br> Hither, I pray, Ulysses, and receive <br> The hospitable gifts which are thy due; <br> And I will pray to Neptune, and entreat <br> The mighty god to guide thee safely home. <br> His son am I, and he declares himself <br> My father. He can heal me if he will, <br> And no one else of all the immortal gods <br> Or mortal men can give me back my sight.’</p>
<p class="verse">“He spake; I answered: ‘Rather would I take <br> Thy life and breath, and send thee to the abode <br> Of Hades, where thou wouldst be past the power <br> Of even Neptune to restore thine eye.’</p>
<p class="verse">“As thus I said, the Cyclops raised his hands, <br> And spread them toward the starry heaven, and thus <br> Prayed to the deity who rules the deep:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“ ‘Hear, dark-haired Neptune, who dost swathe the earth! <br> If I am thine, and thou dost own thyself <br> My father, grant that this Ulysses ne’er <br> May reach his native land! But if it be <br> The will of fate that he behold again <br> His friends, and enter his own palace-halls <br> In his own country, late and sorrowful <br> Be his return, with all his comrades lost, <br> And in a borrowed ship, and may he find <br> In his own home new griefs awaiting him.’</p>
<p class="verse">“He prayed, and Neptune hearkened to his prayer. <br> And then the Cyclops seized another stone, <br> Far larger than the last, and swung it round, <br> And cast it with vast strength. It fell behind <br> Our black-prowed galley, where it almost struck <br> The rudder’s end. The sea was dashed on high <br> Beneath the falling rock, and bore our ship <br> On toward the shore we sought. When we reached <br> The island where together in a fleet <br> Our other galleys lay, we found our friends <br> Sitting where they had waited long in grief. <br> We touched the shore and drew our galley up <br> On the smooth sand, and stepped upon the beach; <br> And taking from on board the sheep that formed <br> Part of the Cyclops’ flock, divided them, <br> That none might be without an equal share. <br> When all the rest were shared, my warrior friends <br> Decreed the ram to me. Of him I made <br> Upon the beach a sacrifice to Jove <br> The Cloud-compeller, Saturn’s son, whose rule <br> Is over all; to him I burned the thighs. <br> He heeded not the offering; even then <br> He planned the wreck of all my gallant ships, <br> And death of my dear comrades. All that day <br> Till set of sun we sat and feasted high <br> Upon the abundant meats and delicate wine. <br> But when the sun went down, and darkness crept <br> Over the earth, we slumbered on the shore; <br> And when again the daughter of the Dawn, <br> The rosy-fingered Morn, looked forth, I called <br> My men with cheerful words to climb the decks <br> And cast the hawsers loose. With speed they went <br> On board and manned the benches, took in hand <br> The oars and smote with them the hoary deep. <br> Onward in sadness, glad to have escaped, <br> We sailed, yet sorrowing for our comrades lost.”</p>
