<p class="lede">Arrival of Telemachus and his companion at Sparta⁠—A wedding; the marriage of the daughter of Menelaus⁠—Helen in Sparta⁠—Entertainment of the guests⁠—Helen’s account of her return to her husband⁠—The Trojan horse⁠—Narrative of the visit of Menelaus to Egypt, in order to consult the sea-god, Proteus⁠—Menelaus informed by him that Ulysses is detained by Calypso in her island⁠—Plot of the suitors to lie in wait for Telemachus on his voyage and destroy him⁠—Penelope visited and consoled by Pallas in a dream.</p>
<p class="verse">They came to Lacedaemon’s valley, seamed <br> With dells, and to the palace of its king, <br> The glorious Menelaus, whom they found <br> Within, and at a wedding banquet, made <br> Both for his blameless daughter and his son, <br> And many guests. Her he must send away, <br> Bride of the son of that invincible chief, <br> Achilles. He betrothed her while in Troy, <br> And gave his kingly word, and now the gods <br> Fulfilled it by the marriage. He was now <br> Sending her forth, with steeds and cars, to reach <br> The noble city of the Myrmidons, <br> Where ruled her consort. From the Spartan coast <br> He brought Alector’s daughter for his son, <br> The gallant Megapenthes, borne to him <br> By a handmaiden in his later years. <br> For not to Helen had the gods vouchsafed <br> Yet other offspring, after she had brought <br> A lovely daughter forth, Hermione, <br> Like golden Venus both in face and form.</p>
<p class="verse">So banqueting the neighbors and the friends <br> Of glorious Menelaus sat beneath <br> The lofty ceiling of those spacious halls, <br> Delighted with the feast. A sacred bard <br> Amidst them touched the harp and sang to them <br> While, as the song began, two dancers sprang <br> Into the midst and trod the measure there</p>
<p class="verse">But they⁠—the hero-youth Telemachus <br> And Nestor’s eminent son⁠—were at the gate, <br> And standing in the entrance with their steeds. <br> The worthy Eteoneus, coming forth⁠— <br> The trusty servant of the glorious son <br> Of Atreus⁠—saw, and hastening thence to tell <br> The shepherd of the people, through the hall <br> He came to him, and spake these winged words:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“O Menelaus, foster-child of Jove, <br> Two strangers have arrived, two men who seem <br> Descended from almighty Jupiter. <br> Shall we then loose the harness from their steeds, <br> Or bid them elsewhere seek a friendly host?”</p>
<p class="verse">The fair-haired king indignantly replied:⁠— <br> “Nay, Eteoneus, thou hast not been wont. <br> Son of Boëthus, thus to play the fool. <br> Thou pratest idly, like a child. Ourselves <br> Have sat, as guests, at generous banquets given <br> By other men, when journeying hitherward <br> In hope that Jove might grant a respite here <br> From our disasters. Hasten, then, to loose <br> The steeds, and bring the strangers to the feast.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake; the attendant hastened forth and called <br> The other trusty servitors, with charge <br> To follow. They unyoked the sweaty steeds, <br> And bound them to the stalls, and gave them oats, <br> With which they mingled the white barley-grains, <br> And close against the shining wall they placed <br> The car, and then they led the guests within <br> The sumptuous palace. Entering, these admired <br> The palace of the foster-child of Jove, <br> For like the splendor of the sun and moon <br> Its glory was. They with delighted eyes <br> Gazed, and, descending to the polished baths, <br> They bathed. The attendant maids who at the bath <br> Had ministered, anointing them with oil, <br> Arrayed the stranger guests in fleecy cloaks <br> And tunics. Each sat down upon a throne <br> Near to Atrides. Now a handmaid brought <br> A beautiful ewer of gold, and laver wrought <br> Of silver, and poured water for their hands, <br> And spread a polished table near their seat; <br> The reverend matron of the household came <br> With bread, and set before them many a dish <br> Gathered from all the feast. The carver next <br> Brought chargers lifted high, and in them meats <br> Of every flavor, and before them placed <br> Beakers of gold. The fair-haired monarch gave <br> His hand to each, and then bespake them thus:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Now taste our banquet and rejoice, and when <br> Ye are refreshed with food we will inquire <br> Who ye may be; for ye are not of those <br> Whose race degenerates, ye are surely born <br> Of sceptred kings, the favorites of Jove. <br> Ignoble men have never sons like you.”</p>
<p class="verse">Thus having said, and taking in his hands <br> A fatling bullock’s chine, which menials brought <br> Roasted, and placed beside the king in sign <br> Of honor, this he laid before his guests. <br> And they put forth their hands and banqueted; <br> And when the calls of hunger and of thirst <br> At length were stilled, Telemachus inclined <br> His head toward Nestor’s son, that no one else <br> Might listen to his words, and thus he said:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“See, son of Nestor, my beloved friend, <br> In all these echoing rooms the sheen of brass, <br> Of gold, of amber, and of ivory; <br> Such is the palace of Olympian Jove <br> Within its walls. How many things are here <br> Of priceless worth! I wonder as I gaze.”</p>
<p class="verse">The fair-haired Menelaus heard him speak, <br> And thus accosted both with winged words:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Dear sons, no mortal man may vie with Jove, <br> Whose palace and possessions never know <br> Decay, but other men may vie or not <br> In wealth with me. ’Twas after suffering <br> And wandering long that in my fleet I brought <br> My wealth with me, and landed on this coast <br> In the eighth year. For I had roamed afar <br> To Cyprus and to Phoenicè, and where <br> The Egyptians dwell, and Ethiopia’s sons, <br> And the Sidonians, and the Erembian race, <br> And to the coast of Lybia, where the lambs <br> Are yeaned with budding horns. There do the ewes <br> Thrice in the circle of the year bring forth <br> Their young. There both the master of the herd <br> And herdsman know no lack of cheese, or flesh, <br> Or of sweet milk; for there the herds yield milk <br> The whole year round. While I was roaming thus, <br> And gathering store of wealth, another slew <br> My brother, unforewarned, and through the fraud <br> Of his own guilty consort. Therefore small <br> Is the content I find in bearing rule <br> O’er these possessions. Ye have doubtless heard <br> This from your parents, be they who they may; <br> For much have I endured, and I have lost <br> A palace, a most noble dwelling-place, <br> Full of things rare and precious. Even now <br> Would I possessed within my palace here <br> But the third part of these; and would that they <br> Were yet alive who perished on the plain <br> Of Troy afar from Argos and its steeds! <br> Yet while I grieve and while I mourn them all, <br> Here, sitting in my palace, I by turns <br> Indulge my heart in weeping, and by turns <br> I pause, for with continual sorrow comes <br> A weariness of spirit. Yet, in truth, <br> For none of all those warriors, though their fate <br> Afflicts me sorely, do I so much grieve <br> As for one hero. When I think of him, <br> The feast and couch are joyless, since, of all <br> The Achaian chiefs, none brought so much to pass <br> As did Ulysses, both in what he wrought <br> And what he suffered. Great calamities <br> Fell to his lot in life, and to my own <br> Grief for his sake that cannot be consoled. <br> Long has he been divided from his friends, <br> And whether he be living now or dead <br> We know not. Old Laertes, the sage queen <br> Penelope, and young Telemachus, <br> Whom, when he went to war he left newborn <br> At home, are sorrowing somewhere for his sake.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake, and woke anew the young man’s grief <br> For his lost father. From his eyelids fell <br> Tears at the hearing of his father’s name, <br> And with both hands he held before his eyes <br> The purple mantle. Menelaus saw <br> His tears, and pondered, doubting which were best⁠— <br> To let the stranger of his own accord <br> Speak of his father, or to question him <br> At first, and then to tell him all he knew.</p>
<p class="verse">As thus he pondered, Helen, like in form <br> To Dian of the golden distaff, left <br> Her high-roofed chamber, where the air was sweet <br> With perfumes, and approached. Adrasta placed <br> A seat for her of costly workmanship; <br> Alcippè brought a mat of soft light wool, <br> And Phylo with a silver basket came, <br> Given by Alcandra, wife of Polybus, <br> Who dwelt at Thebes, in Egypt, and whose house <br> Was rich in things of price. Two silver baths <br> He gave to Menelaus, tripods two, <br> And talents ten of gold. His wife bestowed <br> Beautiful gifts on Helen⁠—one of gold, <br> A distaff; one a silver basket edged <br> With gold and round in form. This Phylo brought <br> Heaped with spun yarn and placed before the queen; <br> Upon it lay the distaff, wrapped in wool <br> Of color like the violet. Helen there <br> Sat down, a footstool at her feet, and straight <br> Questioned with earnest words her husband thus:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Say, Menelaus, foster-child of Jove, <br> Is it yet known what lineage these men claim⁠— <br> These visitants? And what I now shall say, <br> Will it be false or true? Yet must I speak. <br> Woman or man I think I never saw <br> So like another as this youth, on whom <br> I look with deep astonishment, is like <br> Telemachus, the son whom our great chief <br> Ulysses left at home a tender babe <br> When ye Achaians for my guilty sake <br> Went forth to wage the bloody war with Troy.”</p>
<p class="verse">And fair-haired Menelaus answered her:⁠— <br> “Yea, wife, so deem I as it seems to thee. <br> Such are his feet, his hands, the cast of the eye, <br> His head, the hair upon his brow. Just now, <br> In speaking of Ulysses, as I told <br> How he had toiled and suffered for my sake, <br> The stranger held the purple cloak before <br> His eyes, and from the lids dropped bitter tears.”</p>
<p class="verse">Peisistratus, the son of Nestor, spake <br> In answer: “Menelaus, foster-child <br> Of Jove and son of Atreus! sovereign king! <br> He is, as thou hast said, that hero’s son; <br> But he is modest, and he deems that ill <br> It would become him, on arriving here, <br> If he should venture in discourse while thou <br> Art present, in whose voice we take delight <br> As if it were the utterance of a god. <br> The knight Gerenian Nestor sent me forth <br> To guide him hither⁠—for he earnestly <br> Desired to see thee, that thou mightest give <br> Counsel in what he yet should say or do. <br> For bitterly a son, who finds at home <br> No others to befriend him, must lament <br> The absence of a father. So it is <br> With young Telemachus; for far away <br> His father is, and in the land are none <br> Who have the power to shelter him from wrong.”</p>
<p class="verse">The fair-haired Menelaus answered thus:⁠— <br> “O wonder! Then the son of one most dear, <br> Who for my sake so oft has braved and borne <br> The conflicts of the battlefield, hath come <br> Beneath my roof. I thought that I should greet <br> His father with a warmer welcome here <br> Than any other of the Argive race, <br> When Jove the Olympian Thunderer should grant <br> A safe return to us across the deep <br> In our good ships. I would have founded here <br> For him a city in Argos, and have built <br> Dwellings, and would have brought from Ithaca <br> Him and his son, and all his wealth and all <br> His people. To this end I would have caused <br> Some neighboring district where my sway is owned <br> To be dispeopled. Dwelling here we oft <br> Should then have met each other, and no cause <br> Would e’er have parted us, two faithful friends <br> Delighting in each other, till at last <br> Came Death’s black cloud to wrap us in its shade. <br> A god, no doubt, hath seen in this a good <br> Too great for us, and thus to him alone, <br> Unhappy man! denied a safe return.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake; his words awoke in every heart <br> Grief for the absent hero’s sake. Then wept <br> The Argive Helen, child of Jove; then wept <br> Telemachus; nor tearless were the eyes <br> Of Nestor’s son, for to his mind arose <br> The memory of the good Antilochus, <br> Slain by the bright Aurora’s eminent son; <br> Of him he thought, and spake these winged words:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“O son of Atreus! aged Nestor saith, <br> When in his palace we discourse of thee <br> And ask each other’s thought, that thou art wise <br> Beyond all other men. Now, if thou mayst, <br> Indulge me, for not willingly I weep <br> Thus at the evening feast, and soon will Morn, <br> Child of the Dawn, appear. I do not blame <br> This sorrow for whoever meets his fate <br> And dies; the only honors we can pay <br> To those unhappy mortals is to shred <br> Our locks away, and wet our cheeks with tears. <br> I lost a brother, not the least in worth <br> Among the Argives, whom thou must have seen. <br> I knew him not: I never saw his face; <br> Yet is it said Antilochus excelled <br> The others; swift of foot, and brave in war.”</p>
<p class="verse">The fair-haired Menelaus answered him:⁠— <br> “Since thou my friend hast spoken thus, as one <br> Discreet in word and deed, of riper years <br> Than thou, might speak and act⁠—for thou art born <br> Of such a father, and thy words are wise⁠— <br> And easy is it to discern the son <br> Of one on whom Saturnius has bestowed <br> Both at the birth-hour and in wedded life <br> His blessing; as he gives to Nestor now <br> A calm old age that lapses pleasantly, <br> Within his palace-halls, from day to day, <br> And sons wise-minded, mighty with the spear⁠— <br> Then let us lay aside this sudden grief <br> That has o’ertaken us, and only think <br> Of banqueting. Let water now be poured <br> Upon our hands; there will be time enough <br> Tomorrow for discourse; Telemachus <br> And I will then engage in mutual talk.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake, Asphalion, who with diligent heed <br> Served the great Menelaus, on their hands <br> Poured water, and they shared the meats that lay <br> Upon the board. But Helen, Jove-born dame, <br> Had other thoughts, and with the wine they drank <br> Mingled a drug, an antidote to grief <br> And anger, bringing quick forgetfulness <br> Of all life’s evils. Whoso drinks, when once <br> It is infused and in the cup, that day <br> Shall never wet his cheeks with tears, although <br> His father and his mother lie in death, <br> Nor though his brother or beloved son <br> Fall butchered by the sword before his eyes. <br> Such sovereign drugs she had, that child of Jove, <br> Given her by Polydamna, wife of Thon, <br> A dame of Egypt, where the bounteous soil <br> Brings forth abundantly its potent herbs, <br> Of healing some and some of bane, and where <br> Dwell the physicians who excel in skill <br> All other men, for they are of the race <br> Of Paeon. Now when Helen in the cups <br> Had placed the drug, and bidden them to pour <br> The wine upon it, thus she spake again:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Atrides Menelaus, reared by Jove, <br> And ye the sons of heroes!⁠—Jupiter <br> The sovereign, gives, at pleasure, good and ill <br> To one or to another, for his power <br> Is infinite⁠—now sitting in these halls, <br> Feast and enjoy free converse. I will speak <br> What suits the occasion. I could not relate, <br> I could not even name, the many toils <br> Borne by Ulysses, stout of heart. I speak <br> Only of what that valiant warrior did <br> And suffered once in Troy, where ye of Greece <br> Endured such hardships. He had given himself <br> Unseemly stripes, and o’er his shoulders flung <br> Vile garments like a slave’s, and entered thus <br> The enemy’s town, and walked its spacious streets. <br> Another man he seemed in that disguise⁠— <br> A beggar, though when at the Achaian fleet <br> So different was the semblance that he wore. <br> He entered Ilium thus transformed, and none <br> Knew who it was that passed, but I perceived, <br> And questioned him; he turned my quest aside <br> With crafty answers. After I had seen <br> The bath administered, anointed him <br> And clothed him, and had sworn a solemn oath <br> Not to reveal his visit to the men <br> Of Ilium till he reached again the tents <br> And galleys, then he opened to me all <br> The plans of the Achaians. Leaving me, <br> On his return he slew with his long spear <br> Full many a Trojan, and in safety reached <br> The Argive camp with tidings for the host. <br> Then wept aloud the Trojan dames, but I <br> Was glad at heart, for I already longed <br> For my old home, and deeply I deplored <br> The evil fate that Venus brought on me, <br> Who led me thither from my own dear land, <br> And from my daughter and my marriage-bower, <br> And from my lawful spouse, in whom I missed <br> No noble gift of person or of mind.”</p>
<p class="verse">Then fair-haired Menelaus said to her:⁠— <br> “All thou hast spoken, woman, is most true. <br> Of many a valiant warrior I have known <br> The counsels and the purposes, and far <br> Have roamed in many lands, but never yet <br> My eyes have looked on such another man <br> As was Ulysses, of a heart so bold <br> And such endurance. Witness what he did <br> And bore, the heroic man, what time we sat, <br> The bravest of the Argives, pent within <br> The wooden horse, about to bring to Troy <br> Slaughter and death. Thou earnest to the place, <br> Moved, as it seemed, by some divinity <br> Who thought to give the glory of the day <br> To Troy. Deiphobus, the godlike chief, <br> Was with thee. Thrice about the hollow frame <br> That held the ambush thou didst walk and touch <br> Its sides, and call the Achaian chiefs by name, <br> And imitate the voices of the wives <br> Of all the Argives. Diomed and I <br> Sat with the great Ulysses in the midst, <br> And with him heard thy call, and rose at once <br> To sally forth or answer from within; <br> But he forbade, impatient as we were, <br> And so restrained us. All the Achaian chiefs <br> Kept silence save Anticlus, who alone <br> Began to speak, when, with his powerful hands, <br> Ulysses pressed together instantly <br> The opening lips, and saved us all, and thus <br> Held them till Pallas lured thee from the spot.”</p>
<p class="verse">Then spake discreet Telemachus again:⁠— <br> “Atrides Menelaus, reared by Jove, <br> Ruler of tribes! the harder was his lot, <br> Since even thus he could not shun the stroke <br> Of death, not though a heart of steel were his. <br> But now dismiss us to our beds, that there, <br> Couched softly, we may welcome balmy sleep.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake, and Argive Helen called her maids <br> To make up couches in the portico, <br> And throw fair purple blankets over them, <br> And tapestry above, and cover all <br> With shaggy cloaks. Forth from the palace halls <br> They went with torches, and made ready soon <br> The couches; thither heralds led the guests. <br> There in the vestibule Telemachus, <br> The hero, and with him the eminent son <br> Of Nestor, took their rest. Meanwhile the son <br> Of Atreus lay within an inner room <br> Of that magnificent pile, and near to him <br> The glorious lady, long-robed Helen, slept. <br> But when at length the daughter of the Dawn, <br> The rosy-fingered Morning, brought her light, <br> Then Menelaus, great in battle, rose, <br> Put on his garments, took his trenchant sword, <br> And, having hung it on his shoulder, laced <br> The shapely sandals to his shining feet, <br> And issued from his chamber like a god <br> In aspect. Near Telemachus he took <br> His seat, and calling him by name he spake:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“What urgent cause, my brave Telemachus, <br> Brings thee to sacred Lacedaemon o’er <br> The breast of the great ocean? Frankly say, <br> Is it a private or a public need?”</p>
<p class="verse">And thus discreet Telemachus replied:⁠— <br> “Atrides Menelaus, reared by Jove, <br> Ruler of nations! I am come to ask <br> News of my father, if thou knowest aught. <br> My heritage is wasting; my rich fields <br> Are made a desolation. Enemies <br> Swarm in my palace, and from day to day <br> Slaughter my flocks and slow-paced horned herds; <br> My mother’s suitors they, and measureless <br> Their insolence. And therefore am I come <br> To clasp thy knees, and pray thee to relate <br> The manner of my father’s sorrowful death <br> As thou hast seen it with thine eyes, or heard <br> Its story from some wandering man⁠—for sure <br> His mother brought him forth to wretchedness <br> Beyond the common lot. I ask thee not <br> To soften aught in the sad history <br> Through tenderness to me, or kind regard, <br> But tell me plainly all that thou dost know; <br> And I beseech thee, if at any time <br> My father, good Ulysses, brought to pass <br> Aught that he undertook for thee in word <br> Or act while ye were in the realm of Troy, <br> Where the Greeks suffered sorely, bear it now <br> In mind, and let me have the naked truth.”</p>
<p class="verse">Then Menelaus of the amber locks <br> Drew a deep sigh, and thus in answer said:⁠— <br> “Heavens! they would climb into a brave man’s bed, <br> These craven weaklings. But as when a hart <br> Has hid her newborn suckling fawns within <br> The lair of some fierce lion, and gone forth <br> Herself to range the mountainsides and feed <br> Among the grassy lawns, the lion comes <br> Back to the place and brings them sudden death, <br> So will Ulysses bring a bloody fate <br> Upon the suitor crew. O father Jove, <br> And Pallas, and Apollo! I could wish <br> That now, with prowess such as once was his <br> When he, of yore, in Lesbos nobly built, <br> Rising to strive with Philomela’s son, <br> In wrestling threw him heavily, and all <br> The Greeks rejoiced, Ulysses might engage <br> The suitors. Short were then their term of life, <br> And bitter would the nuptial banquet be. <br> Now for the questions thou hast put, and craved <br> From me a true reply, I will not seek <br> To pass them by with talk of other things, <br> Nor yet deceive thee, but of all that once <br> Was told me by the Ancient of the Deep, <br> Whose words are truth, I shall keep nothing back.</p>
<p class="verse">“In Egypt still, though longing to come home, <br> The gods detained me; for I had not paid <br> The sacrifice of chosen hecatombs, <br> And ever do the gods require of us <br> Remembrance of their laws. There is an isle <br> Within the billowy sea before you reach <br> The coast of Egypt⁠—Pharos is its name⁠— <br> At such a distance as a ship could pass <br> In one whole day with a shrill breeze astern. <br> A sheltered haven lies within that isle, <br> Whence the good ships go forth with fresh supplies <br> Of water. There the gods constrained my stay <br> For twenty days, and never in that time <br> Blew favoring winds across the waters, such <br> As bear the galley over the great deep. <br> Now would our stores of food have been consumed, <br> Now would the courage of my men have died, <br> Had not a goddess pitied me, and come <br> To my relief, by name Eidothea, born <br> To the great Proteus, Ancient of the Deep. <br> For she was moved by my distress, and came <br> To me while I was wandering alone, <br> Apart from all the rest. They through the isle <br> Roamed everywhere from place to place, and, pinched <br> With hunger, threw the hook for fish. She came, <br> And, standing near, accosted me and said:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“ ‘Stranger, thou art an idiot, or at least <br> Of careless mood, or else art willingly <br> Neglectful, and art pleased with suffering, <br> That thou dost linger in this isle so long <br> And find no means to leave it, while the hearts <br> Of thy companions faint with the delay.’</p>
<p class="verse">“She spake, and I replied: ‘Whoe’er thou art, <br> goddess, let me say, not willingly <br> I linger here. I surely must have sinned <br> Against the immortal dwellers of high heaven; <br> But tell me⁠—for the gods know all things⁠—who <br> Of all the immortals holds me windbound here, <br> Hindering my voyage; tell me also how <br> To reach my home across the fishy deep.’</p>
<p class="verse">“I ended, and the glorious goddess said <br> In answer: ‘Stranger, I will truly speak; <br> The deathless Ancient of the Deep, whose words <br> Are ever true, Egyptian Proteus, oft <br> Here makes his haunt. To him are fully known⁠— <br> For he is Neptune’s subject⁠—all the depths <br> Of the great ocean. It is said I owe <br> To him my birth. If him thou canst insnare <br> And seize, he will disclose to thee thy way <br> And all its distances, and tell thee how <br> To reach thy home across the fishy deep; <br> And further will reveal, if so he choose, <br> O foster-child of Jove, whate’er of good <br> Or ill has in thy palace come to pass, <br> While thou wert wandering long and wearily.’</p>
<p class="verse">“So said the goddess, and I spake again:⁠— <br> ‘Explain by what device to snare and hold <br> The aged deity, lest he foreknow <br> Or else suspect our purpose and escape. <br> ’Twere hard for mortals to constrain a god.’</p>
<p class="verse">“I ended, and the glorious goddess thus <br> Made answer: ‘When the climbing sun has reached <br> The middle heaven, the Ancient of the Deep, <br> Who ne’er deceives, emerges from the waves, <br> And, covered with the dark scum of the sea, <br> Walks forth, and in a cavern vault lies down. <br> Thither fair Halosydna’s progeny, <br> The sea-calves from the hoary ocean, throng, <br> Rank with the bitter odor of the brine, <br> And slumber near him. With the break of day <br> I will conduct thee thither and appoint <br> Thy place, but thou shalt choose to go with thee <br> Three of the bravest men in thy good ships. <br> And let me now relate the stratagems <br> Of the old prophet. He at first will count <br> The sea-calves, going o’er them all by fives; <br> And when he has beheld and numbered all, <br> Amidst them all will he lie down, as lies <br> A shepherd midst his flock. And then, as soon <br> As ye behold him stretched at length, exert <br> Your utmost strength to hold him there, although <br> He strive and struggle to escape your hands; <br> For he will try all stratagems, and take <br> The form of every reptile on the earth, <br> And turn to water and to raging flame⁠— <br> Yet hold him firmly still, and all the more <br> Make fast the bands. When he again shall take <br> The form in which thou sawest him asleep, <br> Desist from force, and loose the bands that held <br> The ancient prophet. Ask of him what god <br> Afflicts thee thus, and by what means to cross <br> The fishy deep and find thy home again.’ <br> “Thus having said, the goddess straightway sprang <br> Into the billowy ocean, while I sought <br> The galleys, where they rested on the sand, <br> With an uneasy spirit. When I reached <br> The ship and shore we made our evening meal. <br> The hallowed night came down; we lay and slept <br> Upon the sea-beach. When the Morning came, <br> The rosy-fingered daughter of the Dawn, <br> Forth on the border of the mighty main <br> I went, and prayed the immortals fervently. <br> I led three comrades, whom I trusted most <br> In all adventures. Entering the depths <br> Of the great sea, the goddess brought us thence <br> Four skins of sea-calves newly flayed, that thus <br> We might deceive her father. Then she scooped <br> Beds for us in the sea-sand, and sat down <br> To wait his coming. We were near to her, <br> And there she laid us duly down, and threw <br> A skin o’er each. Now did our ambush seem <br> Beyond endurance, for the noisome smell <br> Of those sea-nourished creatures sickened us; <br> And who could bear to sleep beside a whale? <br> But she bethought her of an antidote, <br> A sovereign one, and so relieved us all. <br> To each she brought ambrosia, placing it <br> Beneath his nostrils, and the sweets it breathed <br> O’ercame the animal odor. All the morn <br> We waited patiently. The sea-calves came <br> From ocean in a throng, and laid themselves <br> In rows along the margin of the sea. <br> At noon emerged the aged seer, and found <br> His well-fed sea-calves. Going o’er them all <br> He counted them, ourselves among the rest, <br> With no misgiving of the fraud, and then <br> He laid him down to rest. We rushed with shouts <br> Upon him suddenly, and in our arms <br> Caught him; nor did the aged seer forget <br> His stratagems; and first he took the shape <br> Of a maned lion, of a serpent next, <br> Then of a panther, then of a huge boar, <br> Then turned to flowing water, then became <br> A tall tree full of leaves. With resolute hearts <br> We held him fast, until the aged seer <br> Was wearied out, in spite of all his wiles. <br> And questioned me in speech at last and said:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“ ‘O son of Atreus! who of all the gods <br> Hath taught thee how to take me in this snare, <br> Unwilling as I am? What wouldst thou have?’</p>
<p class="verse">“He spake; I answered: ‘Aged prophet, well <br> Thou knowest. Why deceitfully inquire? <br> It is that I am held a prisoner long <br> Within this isle, and vainly seek the means <br> Of my escape, and grief consumes my heart. <br> Now⁠—since the gods know all things⁠—tell me this, <br> What deity it is, that, hindering thus <br> My voyage, keeps me here, and tell me how <br> To cross the fishy deep and reach my home.’</p>
<p class="verse">“Such were my words, and he in answer said:⁠— <br> ‘But thou to Jove and to the other gods <br> Shouldst first have paid acceptable sacrifice, <br> And shouldst have then embarked to reach with speed <br> Thy native land across the dark-blue deep. <br> Now it is not thy fate to see again <br> Thy friends, thy stately palace, and the land <br> That saw thy birth, until thou stand once more <br> Beside the river that through Egypt flows <br> From Jove, and offer sacred hecatombs <br> To the ever-living gods inhabiting <br> The boundless heaven, and they will speed thee forth <br> Upon the voyage thou dost long to make.’</p>
<p class="verse">“He spake. My heart was broken as I heard <br> His bidding to recross the shadowy sea <br> To Egypt, for the way was difficult <br> And long; and yet I answered him and said:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“ ‘Duly will I perform, O aged seer, <br> What thou commandest. But I pray thee tell, <br> And truly, whether all the sons of Greece <br> Whom Nestor and myself, in setting sail, <br> Left on the Trojan coast, have since returned <br> Safe with their galleys, or have any died <br> Untimely in their ships or in the arms <br> Of their companions since the war was closed?’</p>
<p class="verse">“I spake; again he answered me and said:⁠— <br> ‘Why dost thou ask, Atrides, since to know <br> Thou needest not, nor is it well to explore <br> The secrets of my mind? Thou canst not, sure, <br> Refrain from tears when thou shalt know the whole. <br> Many are dead, and many left in Troy. <br> Two leaders only of the well-armed Greeks <br> Were slain returning; in that combat thou <br> Didst bear a part; one, living yet, is kept, <br> Far in the mighty main, from his return.</p>
<p class="verse">“ ‘Amid his well-oared galleys Ajax died. <br> For Neptune first had driven him on the rocks <br> Of Gyrae, yet had saved him from the sea; <br> And he, though Pallas hated him, had yet <br> Been rescued, but for uttering boastful words, <br> Which drew his fate upon him. He had said <br> That he, in spite of all the gods, would come <br> Safe from those mountain waves. When Neptune heard <br> The boaster’s challenge, instantly he laid <br> His strong hand on the trident, smote the rock <br> And cleft it to the base. Part stood erect, <br> Part fell into the deep. There Ajax sat, <br> And felt the shock, and with the falling mass <br> Was carried headlong to the billowy depths <br> Below, and drank the brine and perished there. <br> Thy brother in his roomy ships escaped <br> The danger, for imperial Juno’s aid <br> Preserved him. But when near Meleia’s heights <br> About to land, a tempest seized and swept <br> The hero thence across the fishy deep, <br> Lamenting his hard lot, to that far cape <br> Where once abode Thyestes, and where now <br> His son Aegisthus dwelt. But when the gods <br> Sent other winds, and safe at last appeared <br> The voyage, they returned, and reached their home. <br> With joy he stepped upon his native soil, <br> And kissed the earth that bore him, while his tears <br> At that most welcome sight flowed fast and warm. <br> Him from a lofty perch a spy beheld, <br> Whom treacherous Aegisthus planted there, <br> Bribed by two golden talents. He had watched <br> The whole year through, lest, coming unobserved, <br> The king might make his prowess felt. The spy <br> Flew to the royal palace with the news, <br> And instantly Aegisthus planned a snare. <br> He chose among the people twenty men, <br> The bravest, whom he stationed out of sight, <br> And gave command that others should prepare <br> A banquet. Then with chariots and with steeds, <br> And with a deadly purpose in his heart, <br> He went, and, meeting Agamemnon, bade <br> The shepherd of the people to the feast, <br> And slew him at the board as men might slay <br> A bullock at the crib. Of all who went <br> With Agamemnon thither, none survived, <br> And of the followers of Aegisthus none, <br> But all were slaughtered in the banquet-hall’</p>
<p class="verse">“He spake; my heart was breaking, and I wept, <br> While sitting on the sand, nor in my heart <br> Cared I to live, or longer to behold <br> The sweet light of the sun. But when there came <br> Respite from tears and writhing on the ground, <br> The Ancient of the Deep, who ne’er deceives, <br> Spake yet again: ‘Atrides, lose no time <br> In tears; they profit nothing. Rather seek <br> The means by which thou mayst the soonest reach <br> Thy native land. There thou perchance mayst find <br> Aegisthus yet alive, or haply first <br> Orestes may have slain him, and thyself <br> Arrive to see the funeral rites performed.’</p>
<p class="verse">“He spake, and though afflicted still, my heart <br> Was somewhat comforted; my spirit rose, <br> And thus I answered him with winged words:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“ ‘These men I know; name now the third, who still <br> Is kept from his return afar within <br> The mighty main⁠—alive, perchance, or dead; <br> For, though I dread to hear, I long to know.’</p>
<p class="verse">“I spake, and Proteus answered me again:⁠— <br> ‘It is Laertes’ son, whose dwelling stands <br> In Ithaca. I saw him in an isle, <br> And in the cavern-palace of the nymph <br> Calypso, weeping bitterly, for she <br> Constrains his stay. He cannot leave the isle <br> For his own country; ship arrayed with oars <br> And seamen has he none to bear him o’er <br> The breast of the great ocean. But for thee, <br> ’Tis not decreed that thou shalt meet thy fate <br> And die, most noble Menelaus, where <br> The steeds of Argos in her pastures graze. <br> The gods will send thee to the Elysian plain, <br> And to the end of earth, the dwelling-place <br> Of fair-haired Rhadamanthus. There do men <br> Lead easiest lives. No snow, no bitter cold, <br> No beating rains, are there; the ocean-deeps <br> With murmuring breezes from the West refresh <br> The dwellers. Thither shalt thou go; for thou <br> Art Helen’s spouse, and son-in-law of Jove.’</p>
<p class="verse">“He spake, and plunged into the billowy deep. <br> I to the fleet returned in company <br> With my brave men, revolving, as I went, <br> A thousand projects in my thought. I reached <br> My galley by the sea, and we prepared <br> Our evening meal. The hallowed night came down, <br> And there upon the ocean-beach we slept. <br> But when the rosy-fingered Morn appeared, <br> The daughter of the Dawn, we drew our ships <br> To the great deep, and raised the masts and spread <br> The sails; the crews, all entering, took their seats <br> Upon the benches, ranged in order due, <br> And beat the foaming water with their oars. <br> Again to Egypt’s coast I brought the fleet, <br> And to the river that descends from Jove, <br> And there I offered chosen hecatombs; <br> And having thus appeased the gods, I reared <br> A tomb to Agamemnon, that his fame <br> Might never die. When this was done I sailed <br> For home; the gods bestowed a favoring wind. <br> But now remain thou till the eleventh day, <br> Or till the twelfth, beneath my roof, and then <br> Will I dismiss thee with munificent gifts⁠— <br> Three steeds, a polished chariot, and a cup <br> Of price, with which to pour, from day to day, <br> Wine to the gods in memory of me.”</p>
<p class="verse">Then spake discreet Telemachus again:⁠— <br> “Atrides, seek not to detain me long, <br> Though I could sit contentedly a year <br> Beside thee, never longing for my home, <br> Nor for my parents, such delight I find <br> In listening to thy words; but even now, <br> In hallowed Pylos, my companions grow <br> Weary, while thou delayest my return. <br> The gifts⁠—whate’er thou choosest to bestow⁠— <br> Let them be such as I can treasure up. <br> The steeds to Ithaca I may not take, <br> I leave them to adorn thy retinue; <br> For thou art ruler o’er a realm of plains, <br> Where grows much lotus, and sweet grasses spring, <br> And wheat and rye, and the luxuriant stalks <br> Of the white barley. But in Ithaca <br> Are no broad grounds for coursing, meadows none. <br> Goats graze amid its fields, a fairer land <br> Than those where horses feed. No isle that lies <br> Within the deep has either roads for steeds <br> Or meadows, least of all has Ithaca.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake; the valiant Menelaus smiled, <br> And kindly touched him with his hand and said:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Dear son, thou comest of a generous stock; <br> Thy words declare it. I will change my gifts, <br> As well I may. Of all that in my house <br> Are treasured up, the choicest I will give, <br> And the most precious. I will give a cup <br> Wrought all of silver save its brim of gold. <br> It is the work of Vulcan. Phaedimus <br> The hero, King of Sidon, gave it me, <br> When I was coming home, and underneath <br> His roof was sheltered. Now it shall be thine.”</p>
<p class="verse">So talked they with each other. Meantime came <br> Those who prepared the banquet to the halls <br> Of the great monarch. Bringing sheep they came <br> And strengthening wine. Their wives, who on their brows <br> Wore showy fillets, brought the bread, and thus <br> Within the house of Menelaus all <br> Was bustle, setting forth the evening meal.</p>
<p class="verse">But in the well-paved court which lay before <br> The palace of Ulysses, where of late <br> Their insolence was shown, the suitor train <br> Amused themselves with casting quoits and spears, <br> While by themselves Antinoüs, and the youth <br> Of godlike mien, Eurymachus, who both <br> Were eminent above the others, sat. <br> To them Noëmon, son of Phronius, went, <br> Drew near, bespake Antinoüs and inquired:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Is it among us known, or is it not, <br> Antinoüs, when Telemachus returns <br> From sandy Pylos? Thither he is gone <br> And in my galley, which I need to cross <br> To spacious Elis. There I have twelve mares <br> And hardy mule-colts with them yet untamed, <br> And some I must subdue to take the yoke.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake, and they were both amazed; for they <br> Had never thought of him as visiting <br> Neleian Pylos, deeming that the youth <br> Was somewhere in his fields, among the flocks, <br> Or haply with the keeper of the swine.</p>
<p class="verse">Then did Antinoüs, Eupeithes’ son, <br> Make answer: “Tell me truly when he sailed, <br> And what young men of Ithaca he chose <br> To go with him. Were they his slaves, or hired <br> To be his followers? Tell, for I would know <br> The whole. Took he thy ship against thy will? <br> Or didst thou yield it at his first request?”</p>
<p class="verse">Noëmon, son of Phornius, thus replied:⁠— <br> “Most willingly I gave it, for what else <br> Would anyone have done when such a man <br> Desired it in his need? It would have been <br> Hard to deny it. For the band of youths <br> Who followed him, they are the bravest here <br> Of all our people; and I saw embark, <br> As their commander, Mentor, or some god <br> Like Mentor altogether. One thing moves <br> My wonder. Only yesterday, at dawn, <br> I met with Mentor here, whom I before <br> Had seen embarking for the Pylian coast.”</p>
<p class="verse">Noëmon spake, and to his father’s house <br> Departed. Both were troubled at his words, <br> And all the suitors took at once their seats, <br> And ceased their pastimes. Then Antinoüs spake, <br> Son of Eupeithes, greatly vexed; his heart <br> Was darkened with blind rage; his eyes shot fire.</p>
<p class="verse">“Strange doings these! a great and proud exploit <br> Performed⁠—this voyage of Telemachus, <br> Which we had called impossible! The boy, <br> In spite of us, has had his will and gone, <br> And carried off a ship, and for his crew <br> Chosen the bravest of the people here. <br> He yet will prove a pest. May Jupiter <br> Crush him ere he can work us further harm! <br> Now give me a swift barque and twenty men <br> That I may lie in ambush and keep watch <br> For his return within the straits between <br> This isle and rugged Samos; then, I deem, <br> He will have sought his father to his cost.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake; they praised his words and bade him act, <br> And rose and left their places, entering <br> The palace of Ulysses. Brief the time <br> That passed before Penelope was warned <br> Of what the suitors treacherously planned. <br> The herald Medon told her all. He heard <br> In the outer court their counsels while within <br> They plotted, and he hastened through the house <br> To bring the tidings to Penelope. <br> Penelope perceived him as he stepped <br> Across the threshold, and bespake him thus:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Why, herald, have the suitor princes sent <br> Thee hither? comest thou to bid the maids <br> Of great Ulysses leave their tasks and make <br> A banquet ready? Would their wooing here <br> And elsewhere were but ended, and this feast <br> Were their last feast on earth! Ye who in throngs <br> Come hither and so wastefully consume <br> The substance of the brave Telemachus, <br> Have ye not from your parents, while ye yet <br> Were children, heard how once Ulysses lived <br> Among them, never wronging any man <br> In all the realm by aught he did or said⁠— <br> As mighty princes often do, through hate <br> Of some and love of others? Never man <br> Endured injustice at his hands, but you⁠— <br> Your vile designs and acts are known; ye bear <br> No grateful memory of a good man’s deeds.”</p>
<p class="verse">And then, in turn, experienced Medon spake:⁠— <br> “O queen, I would this evil were the worst! <br> The suitors meditate a greater still, <br> And a more heinous far. May Jupiter <br> Never permit the crime! Their purpose is <br> To meet Telemachus, on his return, <br> And slay him with the sword; for thou must know <br> That on a voyage to the Pylian coast <br> And noble Lacedaemon he has sailed, <br> To gather tidings of his father’s fate.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake, and her knees failed her and her heart <br> Sank as she heard. Long time she could not speak; <br> Her eyes were filled with tears, and her clear voice <br> Was choked; yet, finding words at length, she said:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“O herald! wherefore should my son have gone? <br> There was no need that he should trust himself <br> To the swift ships, those horses of the sea, <br> With which men traverse its unmeasured waste. <br> Was it that he might leave no name on earth?”</p>
<p class="verse">And then again experienced Medon spake:⁠— <br> “I know not whether prompted by some god <br> Or moved by his own heart thy son has sailed <br> For Pylos, hoping there to hear some news <br> Of his returning father, or his fate.”</p>
<p class="verse">Thus having said, the herald, traversing <br> The palace of Ulysses, went his way, <br> While a keen anguish overpowered the queen, <br> Nor could she longer bear to keep her place <br> Upon her seat⁠—and many seats were there⁠— <br> But on the threshold of her gorgeous rooms <br> Lay piteously lamenting. Round her came <br> Her maidens wailing⁠—all, both old and young, <br> Who formed her household. These Penelope, <br> Sobbing in her great sorrow, thus bespake:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Hear me, my friends, the heavens have cast on me <br> Griefs heavier than on any others born <br> And reared with me⁠—me, who had lost by death <br> Already a most gracious husband, one <br> Who bore a lion heart and who was graced <br> With every virtue, greatly eminent <br> Among the Greeks, and widely famed abroad <br> Through Hellas and all Argos. Now my son, <br> He whom I loved, is driven before the storms <br> From home, inglorious, and I was not told <br> Of his departure. Ye too, worthless crew! <br> Ye took no thought, not one of you, to call <br> Me from my sleep, although ye must have known <br> Full well when he embarked in his black ship. <br> And if it had been told me that he planned <br> This voyage, then, impatient as he was <br> To sail, he would have certainly remained, <br> Or else have left me in these halls a corpse. <br> And now let one of my attendants call <br> The aged Dolius, whom, when first I came <br> To this abode, my father gave to me <br> To be my servant, and who has in charge <br> My orchards. Let him haste and take his place <br> Beside Laertes, and to him declare <br> All that has happened, that he may devise <br> Some fitting remedy, or go among <br> The people, to deplore the dark designs <br> Of those who now are plotting to destroy <br> The heir of great Ulysses and his own.”</p>
<p class="verse">Then Eurycleia, the beloved nurse, <br> Answered: “Dear lady, slay me with the sword, <br> Or leave me here alive; I will conceal <br> Nothing that has been done or said. I gave <br> All that he asked, both bread and delicate wine, <br> And took a solemn oath, which he required, <br> To tell thee naught of this till twelve days passed, <br> Or till thou shouldst thyself inquire and hear <br> Of his departure, that those lovely cheeks <br> Might not be stained with tears. Now bathe and put <br> Fresh garments on, and to the upper rooms <br> Ascending, with thy handmaids offer prayer <br> To Pallas, daughter of the god who bears <br> The aegis. She will then protect thy son, <br> Even from death. Grieve not the aged man, <br> Already much afflicted. Sure I am <br> The lineage of Arcesius has not lost <br> The favor of the gods, but someone yet <br> Surviving will possess its lofty halls <br> And its rich acres, stretching far away.”</p>
<p class="verse">She spake; the queen repressed her grief, and held <br> Her eyes from tears. She took the bath and put <br> Fresh garments on, and, to the upper rooms <br> Ascending with her maidens, heaped with cakes <br> A canister, and prayed to Pallas thus:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Daughter invincible of Jupiter <br> The Aegis-bearer, hear me. If within <br> Thy courts the wise Ulysses ever burned <br> Fat thighs of beeves or sheep, remember it, <br> And rescue my dear son, and bring to naught <br> The wicked plots of the proud suitor-crew.”</p>
<p class="verse">She spake, and wept aloud. The goddess heard <br> Her prayer. Meantime the suitors filled with noise <br> The shadowy palace-halls, and there were some <br> Among that throng of arrogant youths who said:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Truly the queen, whom we have wooed so long, <br> Prepares for marriage; little does she know <br> The bloody death we destine for her son.”</p>
<p class="verse">So spake they, unaware of what was done <br> Elsewhere. Antinoüs then stood forth and said:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Good friends, I warn you all that ye refrain <br> From boasts like these, lest someone should report <br> Your words within. Now let us silently <br> Rise up, and all conspire to put in act <br> The counsel all so heartily approve.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake, and chose a crew of twenty men, <br> The bravest. To the seaside and the ship <br> They went, and down to the deep water drew <br> The ship, and put the mast and sails on board, <br> And fitted duly to their leathern rings <br> The oars, and spread the white sail overhead. <br> Their nimble-handed servants brought them arms, <br> And there they moored the galley, went on board, <br> And supped and waited for the evening star.</p>
<p class="verse">Now in the upper chamber the chaste queen, <br> Penelope, lay fasting; food or wine <br> She had not tasted, and her thoughts were still <br> Fixed on her blameless son. Would he escape <br> The threatened death, or perish by the hands <br> Of the insolent suitors? As a lion’s thoughts, <br> When, midst a crowd of men, he sees with dread <br> The hostile circle slowly closing round, <br> Such were her thoughts, when balmy sleep at length <br> Came creeping over her as on her couch <br> She lay reclined, her limbs relaxed in rest.</p>
<p class="verse">Now Pallas framed a new device; she called <br> A phantom up, in aspect like the dame <br> Iphthime, whom Eumelus had espoused <br> In Pherae, daughter of the high-souled chief <br> Icarius. Her she sent into the halls <br> Of great Ulysses, that she might beguile <br> The sorrowful Penelope from tears <br> And lamentations. By the thong that held <br> The bolt she slid into the royal bower <br> And standing by her head bespake the queen:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Penelope, afflicted as thou art, <br> Art thou asleep? The ever-blessed gods <br> Permit thee not to grieve and weep; thy son, <br> Who has not sinned against them, shall return.”</p>
<p class="verse">And then discreet Penelope replied, <br> Still sweetly slumbering at the Gate of Dreams:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Why, sister, art thou here, who ne’er before <br> Hast come to me? The home is far away <br> In which thou dwellest. Thou exhortest me <br> To cease from grieving, and to lay aside <br> The painful thoughts that crowd into my mind, <br> And torture me who have already lost <br> A noble-minded, lionhearted spouse, <br> One eminent among Achaia’s sons <br> For every virtue, and whose fame was spread <br> Through Hellas and through Argos. Now my son, <br> My best beloved, goes to sea⁠—a boy, <br> Unused to hardships, and unskilled to deal <br> With strangers. More I sorrow for his sake <br> Than for his father’s. I am filled with fear, <br> And tremble lest he suffer wrong from those <br> Among whom he has gone, or on the deep, <br> Where he has enemies who lie in wait <br> To slay him ere he reach his home again.”</p>
<p class="verse">And then the shadowy image spake again:⁠— <br> “Be of good courage; let not fear o’ercome <br> Thy spirit, for there goes with him a guide <br> Such as all others would desire to have <br> Beside them ever, trusting in her power⁠— <br> Pallas Athene, and she looks on thee <br> With pity. From her presence I am sent, <br> Her messenger, declaring this to thee.”</p>
<p class="verse">Again discreet Penelope replied:⁠— <br> “If then thou be a goddess and hast heard <br> A goddess speak these words, declare, I pray, <br> Of that ill-fated one, if yet he live <br> And look upon the sun, or else have died <br> And passed to the abodes beneath the earth.”</p>
<p class="verse">Once more the shadowy image spake: “Of him <br> Will I say nothing, whether living yet <br> Or dead; no time is this for idle words.”</p>
<p class="verse">She said, and from the chamber glided forth <br> Beside the bolt, and mingled with the winds. <br> Then quickly from her couch of sleep arose <br> The daughter of Icarius, for her heart <br> Was glad, so plainly had the dream conveyed <br> Its message in the stillness of the night.</p>
<p class="verse">Meanwhile the suitors on their ocean-path <br> Went in their galley, plotting cruelly <br> To slay Telemachus. A rocky isle <br> Far in the middle sea, between the coast <br> Of Ithaca and craggy Samos, lies, <br> Named Asteris; of narrow bounds, yet there <br> A sheltered haven is to which two straits <br> Give entrance. There the Achaians lay in wait.</p>
