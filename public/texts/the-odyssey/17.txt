<p class="lede">Meeting of Telemachus and Penelope, Ulysses conducted by Eumaeus to the palace, where he is known by the dog Argus, who dies on seeing him⁠—Entrance of Ulysses among the suitors⁠—Assault made by Antinoüs upon Ulysses⁠—Ulysses sent for by Penelope.</p>
<p class="verse">Now when the rosy-fingered Morn looked forth⁠— <br> The daughter of the Dawn⁠—Telemachus, <br> The dear son of the great Ulysses, bound <br> The shapely sandals underneath his feet, <br> And took the massive spear that fitted well <br> His grasp, and, as he stood in act to go <br> Up to the town, bespake the swineherd thus:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Father, I hasten to the town, that there <br> My mother may behold me; for I think <br> She will not cease to grieve, and fear, and weep, <br> Till her eyes rest on me. I leave with thee <br> The charge of leading our unfortunate guest <br> Into the city, there to beg his bread. <br> Whoever will may give him food and drink. <br> All men I cannot feed, and I have cares <br> Enough already. If he chafe at this, <br> The worse for him. I like to speak my mind.”</p>
<p class="verse">And thus Ulysses, the sagacious, spake: <br> “Nor do I wish, my friend, to loiter here. <br> Better it is for one like me to beg <br> In town than in the country. In the town, <br> Whoever chooses will bestow his dole; <br> But here, if I remain about the stalls, <br> I am no longer of an age to do <br> All that a master may require. Go thou; <br> This man, at thy command, will lead me hence, <br> As soon as I have warmed me at the fire, <br> And the air grows milder. This keen morning-cold <br> May end me, and the way, ye say, is long.”</p>
<p class="verse">He ended; from the lodge Telemachus <br> Passed quickly, meditating to destroy <br> The suitors. Coming to his stately home, <br> He leaned his spear against a column’s shaft, <br> And, crossing the stone threshold, entered in. <br> First Eurycleia, who had been his nurse, <br> Beheld him, as she spread the beautiful thrones <br> With skins, and ran to him with weeping eyes; <br> And round him other handmaids of the house <br> Of resolute Ulysses thronged. They gave <br> Fond welcome, kissing him upon the brow <br> And shoulders. Issuing from her chamber next <br> The chaste Penelope, like Dian’s self <br> In beauty, or like golden Venus, came, <br> And, weeping, threw her arms about her son, <br> And kissed him on his forehead and on both <br> His glorious eyes, and said, amidst her tears:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Light of my eyes! O my Telemachus! <br> Art thou, then, come? I never thought again <br> To see thee, when I heard thou hadst embarked <br> For Pylos⁠—secretly, and knowing me <br> Unwilling⁠—in the hope to gather there <br> Some tidings of thy father. Tell me now <br> All that has happened, all that thou hast seen.”</p>
<p class="verse">And thus discreet Telemachus replied: <br> “Nay, mother, waken not my griefs again, <br> Nor move my heart to rage. I have just now <br> Escaped a cruel death. But go and bathe, <br> And put fresh garments on, and when thou com’st <br> Into thy chamber with thy maidens, make <br> A vow to all the gods that thou wilt burn <br> A sacrifice of chosen hecatombs <br> When Jupiter shall have avenged our wrongs. <br> Now must I hasten to the marketplace <br> In quest of one who came with me a guest <br> From Pylos. Him, with all my faithful crew, <br> I sent before me to this port, and bade <br> Piraeus lead him to his own abode, <br> There to be lodged and honored till I came.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake, nor flew his words unheeded by. <br> The princess bathed, and put fresh garments on, <br> And vowed to all the gods a sacrifice <br> Of chosen hecatombs when Jupiter <br> Should punish the wrongdoers. While she prayed, <br> Telemachus went forth, his spear in hand. <br> Two fleet dogs followed him. Minerva shed <br> A godlike beauty o’er his form and face, <br> And all the people wondered as he came. <br> The suitors thronged around him with smooth words, <br> Yet plotting mischief in their hearts. He turned <br> From their assembly hastily, and took <br> His place where Mentor sat with Antiphus, <br> And Halitherses⁠—all his father’s friends <br> And his from the beginning. While they asked <br> Of all that he had seen, Piraeus came, <br> The famous spearman, bringing through the town <br> The stranger with him to the marketplace. <br> Nor long Telemachus delayed, but came <br> To meet his guest, and then Piraeus said:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Telemachus, despatch to where I dwell <br> Thy serving-women; I would send to thee, <br> At once, the gifts which Menelaus gave.”</p>
<p class="verse">And then discreet Telemachus replied: <br> “We know not yet, Piraeus, what may be <br> The event; and if the suitors privily <br> Should slay me in the palace, and divide <br> The inheritance among them, I prefer <br> That thou, instead of them, shouldst have the gifts; <br> But should they meet the fate which I have planned, <br> And be cut off, then shalt thou gladly bring <br> The treasures, which I gladly will receive.”</p>
<p class="verse">So spake the prince, and to the palace led <br> The unhappy man, his guest. When now they reached <br> The stately pile, they both laid down their cloaks <br> Upon the benches, and betook themselves <br> To the well-polished baths. The attendant maids <br> There ministered and smoothed their limbs with oil, <br> And each received a tunic at their hands, <br> And fleecy mantle. Then they left the baths <br> And took their seats. A damsel came, and poured <br> Water from a fair ewer wrought of gold <br> Into a silver basin for their hands, <br> And spread a polished table near their seats; <br> And there the matron of the household placed <br> Bread, and the many dishes which her stores <br> Supplied. The queen was seated opposite, <br> Beside a column of the pile, and twirled <br> A slender spindle, while the son and guest <br> Put forth their hands and shared the meal prepared. <br> And when the calls of hunger and of thirst <br> Had ceased, thus spake the sage Penelope:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Telemachus, when I again go up <br> Into my chamber, I shall lay me down <br> Upon the couch which, since Ulysses sailed <br> For Troy with Atreus’ sons, has been to me <br> A couch of mourning, sprinkled with my tears. <br> And now thou hast not chosen to reveal, <br> Ere yet the haughty suitors throng again <br> Into these halls, what in thy voyage thou <br> Hast haply heard concerning his return.”</p>
<p class="verse">And thus discreet Telemachus replied: <br> “Then, mother, will I truly tell thee all. <br> We went to Pylos, and saw Nestor there, <br> The shepherd of the people. Kindly he <br> Received me in his stately home, as one <br> Might welcome back a wandering son returned <br> From foreign lands. Such welcome I received <br> Both from the king and his illustrious sons. <br> But he had heard, he said, from living man, <br> No tidings of the much-enduring chief <br> Ulysses, whether he were yet alive <br> Or dead. He therefore sent me with his steeds <br> And chariot to the court of Atreus’ son, <br> The warlike Menelaus. There I saw <br> The Argive Helen, for whose sake the Greeks <br> And Trojans, by the appointment of the gods, <br> Suffered so much. The valiant king inquired <br> What wish of mine had brought me to the town <br> Of hallowed Lacedaemon. I replied, <br> And truly told him all, and everything <br> In order. Then he answered me, and said:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“ ‘So then! these men, unwarlike as they are, <br> Aspire to occupy a brave man’s bed, <br> As when a hart hath left two suckling fawns, <br> Just born, asleep in a strong lion’s lair, <br> And roams for pasturage the mountain slopes <br> And grassy lawns, the lion suddenly <br> Comes back, and makes a cruel end of both, <br> So will Ulysses bring a sudden doom <br> Upon the suitors. Would to Father Jove, <br> And Pallas, and Apollo, that the chief, <br> Returning mighty, as he was when once <br> In well-built Lesbos, at a wrestling-match, <br> He rose to strive with Philomelides, <br> And threw him heavily, and all the Greeks <br> Rejoiced⁠—would he might come as then he was! <br> Short-lived would then the suitors be, and taste <br> A bitter marriage-feast. But now, to come <br> To what thou hast inquired, I will not seek <br> To turn from it, and talk of other things, <br> Nor will deceive. Of all that I was told <br> By the Ancient of the Deep, whose words are true, <br> I will not hide a single word from thee. <br> He saw thy father in an isle, he said, <br> A prey to wasting sorrows, and detained, <br> Unwilling, in the palace of the nymph <br> Calypso. To the country of his birth <br> He cannot come; no ships are there with oars <br> And crew to bear him o’er the great wide sea.’</p>
<p class="verse">“Thus Menelaus, mighty with the spear, <br> The son of Atreus, said. And having now <br> Fulfilled my errand, I returned. The gods <br> Gave favoring winds, and sent me swiftly home.”</p>
<p class="verse">He ended, and the queen was deeply moved. <br> Then Theoclymenus, the godlike, said:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“O gracious consort of Laertes’ son, <br> King Menelaus knew not all. Hear now <br> What I shall say⁠—for I will prophesy, <br> And truly, nor will keep back aught from thee. <br> Let Jupiter, the mightiest of the gods, <br> And this thy hospitable board, and this <br> The hearth of great Ulysses, where I find <br> A refuge, be my witnesses, that now <br> Ulysses is in his own land again, <br> And sits or walks observant of the deeds <br> Of wrong, and planning vengeance, yet to fall <br> On all the suitors; such the augury <br> Which I beheld when in the gallant barque <br> I sat and told it to Telemachus.”</p>
<p class="verse">And thus the sage Penelope replied: <br> “O stranger! may thy saying be fulfilled! <br> Then shalt thou have such thanks and such rewards <br> That all who greet thee shall rejoice with thee.”</p>
<p class="verse">So talked they with each other. In the space <br> Before the palace of Ulysses stood <br> The suitors, pleased with hurling quoits and spears <br> On the smooth pavement, where their insolence <br> So oft was seen. But when the supper-hour <br> Was near, and from the fields the cattle came, <br> Driven by the herdsmen, Medon⁠—he whom most <br> They liked of all the heralds, and who sat <br> Among them at the feast⁠—bespake them thus:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Youths! since ye now have had your pastime here, <br> Come in, and help prepare the evening meal; <br> At the due hour a banquet is not ill.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake; the suitors hearkened and obeyed, <br> And rose, and came into the halls, and laid <br> Their cloaks upon the benches and the thrones, <br> And slaughtered well-fed sheep and fading goats, <br> And made a victim of a pampered brawn, <br> And a stalled ox, preparing for the feast. <br> Meantime Ulysses and that noble hind <br> The swineherd hastened to begin their walk <br> To town, and thus the master swineherd spake:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Since, stranger, ’tis thy wish to pass today <br> Into the city, as my master bade⁠— <br> Though I by far prefer that thou remain <br> A guardian of the stalls, yet much I fear <br> My master, and am sure that he would chide, <br> And harsh the upbraidings of a master are⁠— <br> Let us depart; the day is now far spent, <br> And chill will be the air of eventide.”</p>
<p class="verse">Ulysses, the sagacious, answered thus: <br> “Enough; I know; thy words are heard by one <br> Who understands them. Let us then depart. <br> Lead thou the way; and if thou hast a staff, <br> Cut from the wood to lean on, give it me, <br> Since, as thou say’st, we have a slippery road.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake, and o’er his shoulders flung a scrip, <br> Old, cracked, and hanging by a twisted thong. <br> Eumaeus gave the staff he asked, and both <br> Went forth; the dogs and herdsmen stayed to guard <br> The lodge. The swineherd led his master on <br> Townward, a squalid beggar to the sight, <br> And aged, leaning on a staff, and wrapped <br> In sordid rags. There by the rugged way, <br> As they drew near the town, they passed a fount <br> Wrought by the hand of man, and pouring forth <br> Its pleasant streams, from which the citizens <br> Drew water. Ithacus and Neritus <br> Founded it with Polyctor, and a grove <br> Of alders feeding on the moistened earth <br> Grew round it on all sides. The ice-cold rill <br> Gushed from a lofty rock, upon whose brow <br> An altar stood, at which the passersby <br> Worshipped, and laid their offerings for the Nymphs. <br> There did Melanthius, son of Dolius, meet <br> The twain, as he was driving to the town <br> The finest goats of all the flocks, to make <br> A banquet for the suitors; with him went <br> Two shepherds, following the flock. As soon <br> As he beheld Eumaeus and his guest, <br> He railed at them with rude and violent words, <br> That made the anger of Ulysses rise.</p>
<p class="verse">“See that vile fellow lead the vile about! <br> Thus ever doth some god join like with like. <br> Thou worthless swineherd! whither wouldst thou take <br> This hungry, haunting beggar-man, this pest <br> Of feasts, who at the posts of many a door <br> Against them rubs his shoulders, asking crusts, <br> Tripods or cauldrons never. Shouldst thou leave <br> The wretch to me, to watch my stalls, and sweep <br> The folds, and bring fresh branches to the kids, <br> He might by drinking whey get stouter thighs. <br> But he has learned no good, and will refuse <br> To work; he better likes to stroll about <br> With that insatiable stomach, asking alms <br> To fill it. Let me tell thee what is sure <br> To happen to him, should he ever come <br> Into the palace of the glorious chief <br> Ulysses. Many a footstool will be flung <br> Around him by the hands of those who sit <br> As guests, and they will tear the fellow’s sides.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake, and in his folly thrust his heel <br> Against the hero’s thigh. The blow moved not <br> Ulysses from his path, nor swerved he aught, <br> But meditated whether with a blow <br> Of his good staff to take the fellow’s life, <br> Or lift him in the air and dash his head <br> Against the ground. Yet he endured the affront <br> And checked his wrath. The swineherd spake, and chid <br> The offender, and thus prayed with lifted hands:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Nymphs of the fountain, born to Jupiter! <br> If e’er in sacrifice Ulysses burned <br> To you the thighs of lambs and goats, o’erlaid <br> With fat, be pleased to grant the prayer I make, <br> That, guided by some deity, the chief <br> May yet return. Then thy rude boasts would cease. <br> Melanthius, which thou utterest in thy way <br> From place to place while wandering through the town. <br> Unfaithful shepherds make a perishing flock.”</p>
<p class="verse">Melanthius, keeper of the goats, rejoined: <br> “ ’Tis wonderful how flippant is the cur, <br> And shrewd! But I shall carry him on board <br> A good black ship, far off from Ithaca, <br> And there will sell him for a goodly price. <br> Would that Apollo of the silver bow <br> Might in the palace slay Telemachus <br> This very hour, or that the suitors might, <br> As certainly as that the day which brings <br> Ulysses to his home will never dawn!”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake, and left them there. They followed on <br> Slowly. Melanthius hastened, and was soon <br> At the king’s palace gate, and, entering, took <br> A seat right opposite Eurymachus, <br> Whose favorite he was. The attendants there <br> Brought meats, the matron of the household bread, <br> And both were set before them. Meantime stopped <br> Ulysses with the noble swineherd near <br> The palace, for around them in the air <br> Came the sweet murmurs of a lyre. Just then <br> Phemius, the minstrel, had begun his song, <br> Ulysses took the swineherd’s hand, and said:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Eumaeus, this must be the noble pile <br> In which Ulysses dwelt, for easily <br> ’Tis known among the others that are near. <br> Rooms over rooms are here; around its court <br> Are walls and battlements, and folding-doors <br> Shut fast the entrance; no man may contemn <br> Its strength. And I perceive that many guests <br> Banquet within; the smoke of fat goes up, <br> And the sweet lyre is heard; the gods have given <br> Its music to accompany the feast.”</p>
<p class="verse">And then, Eumaeus, thou didst make reply: <br> “Thou speakest rightly, and in other things <br> Thou art not slow of thought. Now let us think <br> What we shall do. First enter, if thou wilt, <br> The sumptuous rooms, while I remain without; <br> Or, if it please thee, I will enter first, <br> While thou remainest; yet delay not long, <br> Lest someone, seeing thee, should deal a blow, <br> Or drive thee hence. I pray thee, think of this.”</p>
<p class="verse">Ulysses, the great sufferer, answered thus: <br> “Enough; I know; thy words are heard by one <br> Who understands them. Go before me, then, <br> And leave me here. I am not quite unused <br> To blows and stripes, and patient is my mood, <br> For greatly have I suffered, both at sea <br> And in the wars; and I submit to bear <br> This also. But the stomach’s eagerness <br> Is desperate, and is not to be withstood, <br> And many are the mischiefs which it brings <br> Upon the race of men; it fits out fleets <br> That cross the barren deep arrayed for war, <br> And carry death and woe to hostile realms.”</p>
<p class="verse">So talked the twain. A dog was lying near, <br> And lifted up his head and pricked his ears. <br> ’Twas Argus, which the much-enduring man <br> Ulysses long before had reared, but left <br> Untried, when for the hallowed town of Troy <br> He sailed. The young men oft had led him forth <br> In eager chase of wild goats, stags, and hares; <br> But now, his master far away, he lay <br> Neglected, just before the stable doors, <br> Amid the droppings of the mules and beeves, <br> Heaped high till carried to the spacious fields <br> Of which Ulysses was the lord. There lay <br> Argus, devoured with vermin. As he saw <br> Ulysses drawing near, he wagged his tail <br> And dropped his ears, but found that he could come <br> No nearer to his master. Seeing this, <br> Ulysses wiped away a tear unmarked <br> By the good swineherd, whom he questioned thus:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Eumaeus, this I marvel at⁠—this dog, <br> That lies upon the dunghill, beautiful <br> In form, but whether in the chase as fleet <br> As he is fairly shaped I cannot tell. <br> Worthless, perchance, as house-dogs often are, <br> Whose masters keep them for the sake of show.”</p>
<p class="verse">And thus, Eumaeus, thou didst make reply: <br> “The dog belongs to one who died afar. <br> Had he the power of limb which once he had <br> For feats of hunting when Ulysses sailed <br> For Troy and left him, thou wouldst be amazed <br> Both at his swiftness and his strength. No beast <br> In the thick forest depths which once he saw, <br> Or even tracked by footprints, could escape. <br> And now he is a sufferer, since his lord <br> Has perished far from his own land. No more <br> The careless women heed the creature’s wants; <br> For, when the master is no longer near, <br> The servants cease from their appointed tasks, <br> And on the day that one becomes a slave <br> The Thunderer, Jove, takes half his worth away.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake, and, entering that fair dwelling-place, <br> Passed through to where the illustrious suitors sat, <br> While over Argus the black night of death <br> Came suddenly as soon as he had seen <br> Ulysses, absent now for twenty years. <br> Telemachus, the godlike, was the first <br> To mark the swineherd coming through the hall, <br> And, nodding, called to him. The swineherd looked <br> About him, and beheld a seat on which <br> The carver of the feast was wont to sit, <br> Distributing the meats. He bore it thence <br> And placed it opposite Telemachus, <br> And at his table. Then he sat him down, <br> And thither came the herald, bringing him <br> A portion of the feast, and gave him bread <br> From the full canister. Soon after him <br> Ulysses entered, seemingly an old <br> And wretched beggar, propped upon a staff, <br> And wrapped in sordid weeds. He sat him down <br> On the ashen threshold, just within the doors, <br> And leaned against a shaft of cypress-wood, <br> Which some artificer had skilfully <br> Wrought by a line, and smoothed. Telemachus <br> Called to the swineherd, bade him come, and took <br> A loaf that lay in the fair canister, <br> And all the flesh which his two hands could grasp.</p>
<p class="verse">“Bear this to yonder stranger; bid him go <br> And ask a dole from every suitor here. <br> No beggar should be bashful in his need.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake, the hind obeyed, and, drawing near <br> Ulysses, said to him in winged words:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“These from Telemachus, who bids thee ask <br> A dole from every suitor, for he says <br> No beggar should be bashful in his need.”</p>
<p class="verse">Ulysses, the sagacious, answered thus: <br> “May Jove, the sovereign, make Telemachus <br> A happy man among the sons of men, <br> And grant him all his heart desires in life!”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake, and took the gift in both his hands, <br> And laid it down upon his tattered scrip <br> Close to his feet. Then, while the poet sang, <br> He ate, and, just as he had supped, the bard <br> Closed his divine recital. Then ensued <br> Great clamor in the hall, but Pallas came <br> And moved Ulysses to arise, and ask <br> From every suitor there a dole of bread, <br> That he might know the better from the worse, <br> Though none were to be spared. From right to left <br> He took his way, and asked of every man, <br> With outstretched hand, as if he had been long <br> A beggar. And they pitied him, and gave, <br> And looked at him with wonder, and inquired <br> One of another who he was, and whence. <br> Then spake Melanthius, keeper of the goats:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Give ear, ye suitors of the illustrious queen. <br> As to this stranger, I have seen him once. <br> The swineherd brought him; but I know him not, <br> And of what race he is I cannot tell.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake; Antinoüs chid the swineherd thus: <br> “Why hast thou brought him, too well known thyself? <br> Have we not vagabonds enough? enough <br> Of sturdy beggars, pests of every feast. <br> Or is it a light matter that they throng <br> Hither to waste the substance of thy lord, <br> And therefore thou art with this fellow here?”</p>
<p class="verse">And thus, Eumaeus, thou didst make reply: <br> “Antinoüs, high as is thy station, thou <br> Hast spoken ill. What man goes ever forth <br> To bid a stranger to his house, unless <br> The stranger be of those whose office is <br> To serve the people, be he seer, or leech, <br> Or architect, or poet heaven-inspired, <br> Whose song is gladly heard? All these are called <br> To feasts wherever men are found; but none <br> Call in the poor, to live upon their means. <br> Antinoüs, thou, of all the suitor-train, <br> Dost ever with the greatest harshness treat <br> The servants of Ulysses, chiefly me. <br> I heed it not while sage Penelope <br> Dwells in the palace with her godlike son.”</p>
<p class="verse">Then interposed discreet Telemachus: <br> “Nay, have no strife of words with him, I pray. <br> Antinoüs takes delight in bitter words, <br> And rails, and stirs up railing in the rest.” <br> And then he turned, and thus with winged words <br> Bespake Antinoüs: “Truly thou dost care <br> For me as might a father for a son, <br> Bidding me drive a stranger from my door <br> With violent words⁠—which God forbid. Take now <br> Somewhat and give to him. I grudge it not, <br> Nay, I advise it. Fear not to offend <br> My mother, or displease a single one <br> Of all the household of the godlike chief, <br> Ulysses. But thou hast not thought of this. <br> It suits thee best to feast and never give.”</p>
<p class="verse">Antinoüs thus rejoined: “O utterer <br> Of big and braggart words! Telemachus, <br> If all the other suitors would bestow <br> As much as I will, he would not be seen <br> Within these halls for three months yet to come.”</p>
<p class="verse">So speaking, he brought forward to the sight, <br> From underneath the board, a stool, on which <br> Rested his dainty feet. The others all <br> Gave somewhat to Ulysses, till his scrip <br> Was filled with meat and bread. Then as he went <br> Back to the threshold, there to feast on what <br> The Greeks had given him in his rounds, he stopped <br> Beside Antinoüs, and bespake him thus:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Give somewhat also, friend. Thou dost not seem <br> One of the humbler rank among the Greeks, <br> But of the highest. Kingly is thy look; <br> It therefore will become thee to bestow <br> More freely than the rest, and I will sound <br> Thy praise through all the earth. Mine too was once <br> A happy lot, for I inhabited <br> A palace filled with goods, and often gave <br> To wanderers, whosoever they might be <br> That sought me out, and in whatever need. <br> And I had many servants, and large store <br> Of everything by which men live at ease <br> And are accounted rich. Saturnian Jove⁠— <br> Such was his pleasure⁠—brought me low; for, moved <br> By him, I joined me to a wandering band <br> Of pirates, and to my perdition sailed <br> Upon a distant voyage to the coast <br> Of Egypt. In the river of that land <br> I stationed my good ships, and bade my men <br> Remain with them and watch them well. I placed <br> Sentries upon the heights. Yet confident <br> In their own strength, and rashly giving way <br> To greed, my comrades ravaged the fair fields <br> Of the Egyptians, slew them, and bore off <br> Their wives and little ones. The rumor reached <br> The city soon; the people heard the alarm <br> And came together. With the dawn of day <br> All the great plain was thronged with horse and foot, <br> And gleamed with brass, while Jove, the Thunderer, sent <br> A deadly fear into our ranks, where none <br> Dared face the foe. On every side was death. <br> The Egyptians hewed down many with the sword, <br> And some they led away alive to toil <br> For them in slavery. Me my captors gave <br> Into a stranger’s hands, upon his way <br> To Cyprus, where he reigned, a mighty king, <br> Demetor, son of Jasus. Thence at last <br> I came through many hardships to this isle.”</p>
<p class="verse">Antinoüs lifted up his voice, and said: <br> “What god hath sent this nuisance to disturb <br> The banquet? Take thyself to the mid-hall, <br> Far from thy table, else expect to see <br> An Egypt and a Cyprus of a sort <br> That thou wilt little like. Thou art a bold <br> And shameless beggar. Thou dost take thy round <br> And ask from each, and foolishly they give, <br> And spare not nor consider; well supplied <br> Is each, and freely gives what is not his.”</p>
<p class="verse">Then sage Ulysses said as he withdrew: <br> “ ’Tis strange; thy mind agrees not with thy form. <br> Thou wouldst not give a suppliant even salt <br> In thine own house⁠—thou who, while sitting here <br> Fed at another’s table, canst not bear <br> To give me bread from thy well-loaded board.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake. Antinoüs grew more angry still, <br> And frowned and answered him with winged words:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Dealer in saucy words! I hardly think <br> That thou wilt leave this palace unchastised.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake, and raised the footstool in his hand, <br> And smote Ulysses on the lower part <br> Of the right shoulder. Like a rock he stood, <br> Unmoved beneath the blow Antinoüs gave, <br> But shook his head in silence as he thought <br> Of vengeance. Then, returning, he sat down <br> Upon the threshold, where he laid his scrip <br> Well filled, and thus bespake the suitor-train:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Hear me, ye suitors of the illustrious queen. <br> Grief or resentment no man feels for blows <br> Received by him while fighting for his own⁠— <br> His beeves or white-woolled sheep. But this man here, <br> Antinoüs, dealt that blow on me because <br> I have an empty stomach; hunger brings <br> Great mischiefs upon men. If there be gods <br> Or furies who avenge the poor, may death <br> O’ertake Antinoüs ere his marriage-day!”</p>
<p class="verse">He ended. Then again Eupeithes’ son, <br> Antinoüs, spake: “Eat, stranger, quietly; <br> Sit still, or get thee hence; our young men else <br> Who hear thy words will seize thee by the feet <br> Or hands, and drag thee forth and flay thee there.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake, and greatly were the rest incensed, <br> And one of those proud youths took up the word:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Antinoüs, it was ill of thee to smite <br> That hapless wanderer. Madman! what if he <br> Came down from heaven and were a god! The gods <br> Put on the form of strangers from afar, <br> And walk our towns in many different shapes, <br> To mark the good and evil deeds of men.”</p>
<p class="verse">Thus spake the suitors, but he heeded not <br> Their words. Telemachus, who saw the blow, <br> Felt his heart swell with anger and with grief, <br> Yet from his eyelids fell no tear; he shook <br> His head in silence, pondering to repay <br> The wrong. Meantime the sage Penelope <br> Heard of the stranger smitten in her halls, <br> And thus bespake the maidens of her train:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Would that Apollo, mighty with the bow, <br> Might smite thee also!” Then Eurynomè, <br> The matron of the household, said in turn:</p>
<p class="verse">“O, were our prayers but heard, not one of these <br> Should look upon the golden morn again!”</p>
<p class="verse">Then spake again the sage Penelope: <br> “Mother, they all are hateful; every one <br> Plots mischief, but Antinoüs most of all; <br> And he is like black death, to be abhorred. <br> A friendless stranger passes through these halls, <br> Compelled by need, and asks an alms of each, <br> And all the others give, and fill his scrip; <br> Antinoüs flings a footstool, and the blow <br> Bruises the shoulder of the suppliant man.”</p>
<p class="verse">So talked they with each other where they sat <br> In the queen’s chamber, mid the attendant train <br> Of women, while meantime Ulysses took <br> The evening meal. The queen then bade to call <br> The noble swineherd, and bespake him thus:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“My worthy friend Eumaeus, go and bring <br> The stranger hither. I would speak with him, <br> And ask if anywhere he saw or heard <br> Aught of Ulysses; for he seems like one <br> Whose wanderings have been in many lands.”</p>
<p class="verse">And thus, Eumaeus, thou didst make reply: <br> “Would that these Greeks, O queen, would hold their peace, <br> Then might this stranger in thy hearing speak <br> Words full of consolation. For three nights <br> I had him with me, for three days I made <br> My lodge his home⁠—for at the very first <br> He came to me, escaping from his ship⁠— <br> Nor when he left me had he told of all <br> That he had suffered. As a hearer looks <br> Upon a minstrel whom the gods have taught <br> To sing the poems that delight all hearts, <br> And, listening, longs to listen without end; <br> So, as the stranger sat beneath my roof, <br> He held me charmed. He was the ancestral friend, <br> He said, of thy Ulysses, and his home <br> Was Crete, where dwells the stock of Minos yet. <br> From Crete he came, and much had suffered since, <br> Driven on from place to place. And he had heard <br> Some tidings of Ulysses yet alive⁠— <br> So he affirmed⁠—in a rich region near <br> The realm of the Thesprotians, and prepared <br> To bring much riches to his native isle.”</p>
<p class="verse">Then spake the sage Penelope again: <br> “Go, call him hither, that he may relate <br> His story in my presence. Let these men, <br> As it may please them, sitting at our gates <br> Or in our halls, amuse themselves, for light <br> Are they of heart. Unwasted in their homes <br> Lie their possessions, and their bread and wine <br> Are only for their servants, while themselves <br> Frequent our palace, day by day, and slay <br> Our beeves and sheep and fatling goats, and feast, <br> And drink abundantly the dark red wine, <br> And all with lavish waste. No man is here, <br> Such as Ulysses was, to drive away <br> This pest from our abode. Should he return <br> To his own land, he and his son would take <br> Swift vengeance on the men who do him wrong.”</p>
<p class="verse">She ended. Suddenly Telemachus <br> Sneezed loudly, so that all the palace rang; <br> And, laughing as she heard, Penelope <br> Bespake Eumaeus thus with winged words:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Go, call the stranger. Dost thou not perceive <br> My son has sneezed as to confirm my words. <br> Not unfulfilled will now remain the doom <br> That waits the suitors; none will now escape <br> Death and the Fates. This further let me say, <br> And thou remember it; if what he tells <br> Be true, I will bestow on him a change <br> Of fair attire, a tunic and a cloak.”</p>
<p class="verse">She spake, the swineherd went, and, drawing near <br> Ulysses, said to him in winged words:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Stranger and father, sage Penelope, <br> The mother of the prince, hath sent for thee. <br> Though sorrowing, she is minded to inquire <br> What of her husband thou canst haply say; <br> And should she find that all thy words are true, <br> She will bestow a tunic and a cloak, <br> Garments which much thou needest. For thy food, <br> What will appease thy hunger thou wilt find <br> Among the people; ask, and each will give.”</p>
<p class="verse">Ulysses, much-enduring man, replied: <br> “Eumaeus, faithfully will I declare <br> All that I know to sage Penelope, <br> The daughter of Icarius. Well I knew <br> Her husband, and with like calamities <br> We both have suffered. But I greatly dread <br> This reckless suitor-crew, whose riotous acts <br> And violence reach to the iron heavens. <br> Even now, when that man dealt me, as I passed, <br> A painful blow, though I had done no harm, <br> None interposed, not even Telemachus, <br> In my defence. Now, therefore, ask, I pray, <br> Penelope that she will deign to wait <br> Till sunset in her rooms, though strong her wish <br> To hear my history. Of her husband then, <br> And his return, she may inquire, while I <br> Sit by the blazing hearth; for scant have been <br> My garments, as thou knowest, since the day <br> When first I came, a suppliant, to thy door.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake; the swineherd went, and as he crossed <br> The threshold of Penelope she said:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Thou bringst him not, Eumaeus? What may be <br> The wanderer’s scruple? Fear of someone here? <br> Or in a palace is he filled with awe? <br> To be a bashful beggar is most hard.”</p>
<p class="verse">And thus, Eumaeus, thou didst answer her: <br> “Rightly he speaks, and just as one would think <br> Who shuns the encounter of disorderly men. <br> He prays that thou wilt wait till set of sun; <br> And better were it for thyself, O queen, <br> To speak with him and hear his words alone.”</p>
<p class="verse">Then spake discreet Penelope again: <br> “Whoe’er may be the stranger, not unwise <br> He seems; for nowhere among men are done <br> Such deeds of wrong and outrage as by these.”</p>
<p class="verse">She spake, and the good swineherd, having told <br> The lady all, went forth among the crowd <br> Of suitors, drawing near Telemachus, <br> And bowed his head beside him that none else <br> Might hear, and said to him in winged words:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“I go, my friend, to tend the swine and guard <br> What there thou hast, thy sustenance and mine. <br> The charge of what is here belongs to thee. <br> Be thy first care to save thyself, and watch <br> To see that mischief overtake thee not⁠— <br> For many are the Achaians plotting it, <br> Whom Jove destroy ere we become their prey!”</p>
<p class="verse">Then spake discreet Telemachus in turn: <br> “So be it, father, and, when thou hast supped, <br> Depart, but with the morning come, and bring <br> Choice victims for the sacrifice. The care <br> Of all things here is with the gods and me.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake; the swineherd sat him down again <br> Upon his polished seat, and satisfied <br> His appetite and thirst with food and wine. <br> Then he departed to his herd, and left <br> The palace and the court before it thronged <br> With revellers, who gave the hour to song, <br> And joined the dance; for evening now was come.</p>
