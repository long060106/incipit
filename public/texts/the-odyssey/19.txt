<p class="lede">Removal of the weapons from the hall by Ulysses and his son⁠—Interview of Penelope and Ulysses, who tells her that he has seen her husband in Crete, describes his person and dress, and affirms that within a month he will be in Ithaca⁠—The bath administered to Ulysses by Eurycleia, who recognizes him by a scar on his leg⁠—Narrative of the manner in which the scar was caused.</p>
<p class="verse">Now was the godlike chief Ulysses left <br> In his own palace, planning, with the aid <br> Of Pallas, to destroy the suitor-train, <br> And thus bespake his son with winged words:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Now is the time, Telemachus, to take <br> The weapons that are here, and store them up <br> In the inner rooms. Then, if the suitors ask <br> The reason, answer them with specious words: <br> Say, ‘I have put them where there comes no smoke. <br> Since even now they do not seem the arms <br> Left by Ulysses when he sailed for Troy, <br> So tarnished are they by the breath of fire; <br> And yet another reason sways my mind, <br> The prompting of some god, that ye, when flushed <br> With wine and in the heat of a dispute, <br> May smite and wound each other, and disgrace <br> The banquet and your wooing; for the sight <br> Of steel doth draw men on to violence.’ ”</p>
<p class="verse">He ended, and Telemachus obeyed <br> His father’s words, and calling forth his nurse, <br> The aged Eurycleia, said to her:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Go, nurse, and see the women all shut up <br> In their own place, while in our inner room <br> I lay away my father’s beautiful arms, <br> Neglected long, and sullied by the smoke, <br> While he was absent. I was then a child, <br> But now would keep them from the breath of fire.”</p>
<p class="verse">And thus the nurse, Dame Eurycleia, said: <br> “Would that at length, my child, thou didst exert <br> Thy proper wisdom here, and take in charge <br> Thy house and thy possessions. But who goes <br> With thee to bear a torch, since none of these, <br> Thy handmaids, are allowed to light thy way?”</p>
<p class="verse">And thus discreet Telemachus replied: <br> “This stranger. No man may be idle here <br> Who eats my bread, though from a distant land.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake, nor flew his words in vain. The nurse <br> Closed all the portals of that noble pile. <br> Ulysses and his glorious son in haste <br> Bore off the helmets, and the bossy shields, <br> And the sharp spears, while Pallas held to them <br> A golden lamp, that shed a fair clear light. <br> Then to his father spake Telemachus:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Father! my eyes behold a marvel. All <br> The palace walls, each beautiful recess, <br> The fir-tree beams, the aspiring columns, shine, <br> Before my eyes, as with a blaze of fire. <br> Some god is surely here, someone of those <br> Who make their dwelling in the high broad heaven.”</p>
<p class="verse">Ulysses, the sagacious, answered thus: <br> “Keep silence; give thy thought no speech, nor ask <br> Concerning aught. Such is the wont of those <br> Who dwell upon Olympus. Now withdraw <br> To rest upon thy couch, while I remain, <br> For I would move thy mother and her maids <br> To ask of what concerns me. She, I deem, <br> Full sadly will inquire of many things.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake; Telemachus departed thence, <br> By torchlight, to his chamber, there to rest <br> Where he was wont to lie when gentle sleep <br> Came over him. There lay he down to wait <br> The hallowed morning, while Ulysses, left <br> Within the palace, meditated still <br> Death to the suitors with Minerva’s aid.</p>
<p class="verse">The sage Penelope now left her bower; <br> Like Dian or like golden Venus came <br> The queen. Beside the hearth they placed for her <br> The throne where she was wont to sit, inlaid <br> With ivory and silver, which of yore <br> The artisan Icmalius wrought. They laid <br> Close to the throne a footstool, over which <br> Was spread an ample fleece. On this sat down <br> The sage Penelope. Her white-armed train <br> Of handmaids came with her; they cleared away <br> The abundant feast, and bore the tables off, <br> And cups from which those insolent men had drunk; <br> They laid upon the ground the lighted brands, <br> And heaped fresh fuel round them, both for light <br> And warmth. And now Melantho once again <br> Bespake Ulysses with unmannerly words:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Stranger, wilt thou forever be a pest, <br> Ranging the house at night to play the spy <br> Upon the women? Leave the hall, thou wretch! <br> And gorge thyself without, else wilt thou go <br> Suddenly, driven by blows and flaming brands.”</p>
<p class="verse">The sage Ulysses frowned on her, and said: <br> “Pert creature! why so fiercely rail at me? <br> Is it that I am squalid and ill-clad, <br> And forced by want to beg from hand to hand? <br> Such is the fate of poor and wandering men. <br> I too was opulent once, inhabiting <br> A plenteous home among my fellow-men, <br> And often gave the wanderer alms, whoe’er <br> He might be and in whatsoever need; <br> And I had many servants, and large store <br> Of things by which men lead a life of ease <br> And are called rich. But Jupiter, the son <br> Of Saturn, put an end to this, for so <br> It pleased the god. Now, therefore, woman, think <br> That thou mayst lose the beauty which is now <br> Thy pride among the serving-women here; <br> Thy mistress may be wroth, and make thy life <br> A hard one; or Ulysses may come back⁠— <br> And there is hope of that. Or if it be <br> That he has perished, and returns no more, <br> There still remains his son Telemachus, <br> Who by Apollo’s grace is now a man, <br> And no one of the women in these halls <br> May think to misbehave, and yet escape <br> His eye, for he no longer is a boy.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake; Penelope, the prudent, heard, <br> And, calling to her maid, rebuked her thus:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“O bold and shameless! I have taken note <br> Of thy behavior; thou hast done a wrong <br> For which thy head should answer. Well thou know’st, <br> For thou hast heard me say, that I would ask <br> The stranger in these halls if aught he knows <br> Of my Ulysses, for whose sake I grieve.”</p>
<p class="verse">Then to the matron of the household turned <br> The queen, and thus bespake Eurynomè:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Bring now a seat, Eurynomè, and spread <br> A fleece upon it, where the stranger guest <br> May sit at ease, and hear what I shall say, <br> And answer me, for I have much to ask.”</p>
<p class="verse">She spake; the ancient handmaid brought with speed <br> A polished seat, and o’er it spread a fleece. <br> Ulysses, much-enduring chief, sat down, <br> And thus the sage Penelope began:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“First will I ask thee who thou art, and whence, <br> Where is thy birthplace, and thy parents who?”</p>
<p class="verse">Ulysses, the sagacious, answered thus: <br> “O lady, none in all the boundless earth <br> Can speak of thee with blame. Thy fame has reached <br> To the great heavens. It is like the renown <br> Of some most excellent king, of godlike sway <br> O’er many men and mighty, who upholds <br> Justice in all his realm. The dark-soiled earth <br> Brings wheat and barley forth; the trees are bowed <br> With fruit; the meadows swarm with noble herds, <br> The sea with fish, and under his wise reign <br> The people prosper. Therefore ask, I pray, <br> Of other things, while I am underneath <br> Thy palace-roof, but of my race and home <br> Inquire not, lest thou waken in my mind <br> Unhappy memories. I am a man <br> Of sorrow, and it would become me ill <br> To sit lamenting in another’s house <br> And shedding tears. Besides, a grief indulged <br> Doth grow in violence. Thy maids would blame, <br> And thou perhaps, and ye would call my tears <br> The maudlin tears of one o’ercome with wine.”</p>
<p class="verse">Then spake the sage Penelope again: <br> “Stranger, such grace of feature and of form <br> As once I had the immortals took away, <br> What time the Argive warriors sailed for Troy, <br> And my Ulysses with them. Could he now <br> Return to rule my household as of yore, <br> The wider and the brighter were my fame. <br> But now I lead a wretched life, so great <br> And many are the evils which some god <br> Heaps on me. For the chieftains who bear sway <br> Over the isles⁠—Dulichium, and the fields <br> Of Samos, and Zacynthus dark with woods, <br> And those who rule in sunny Ithaca⁠— <br> Woo me against my will, and waste away <br> My substance. Therefore have I small regard <br> For strangers and for suppliants, and the tribe <br> Of heralds, servants of the public weal, <br> But, pining for Ulysses, wear away <br> My life. The suitors urge the marriage rite, <br> And I with art delay it. Once some god <br> Prompted me to begin an ample web, <br> Wide and of subtle texture, in my rooms. <br> And then I said: ‘Youths, who are pressing me <br> To marriage, since Ulysses is no more, <br> Urge me no further till I shall complete⁠— <br> That so the threads may not be spun in vain⁠— <br> This shroud for old Laertes, when grim fate <br> And death’s long sleep at last shall overtake <br> The hero; else among the multitude <br> Of Grecian women I shall bear the blame, <br> If one whose ample wealth so well was known <br> Should lie in death without a funeral robe.’ <br> I spake, and easily their minds were swayed <br> By what I said, and I began to weave <br> The ample web, but ravelled it again <br> By torchlight every evening. For three years <br> I foiled them thus; but when the fourth year came, <br> And brought its train of hours and changing moons, <br> And many days had passed, they came on me, <br> And through my maidens’ fault, a careless crew, <br> They caught me at my fraud, and chid me sore. <br> So, though unwilling, I was forced to end <br> My task, and cannot longer now escape <br> The marriage, nor is any refuge left. <br> My parents both exhort me earnestly <br> To choose a husband, and my son with grief <br> Beholds the suitors wasting his estate, <br> And he already is a man and well <br> Can rule his household; Jupiter bestows so. <br> Such honor on him. Now, I pray, declare <br> Thy lineage, for thou surely art not sprung <br> From the old fabulous oak, nor from a rock.”</p>
<p class="verse">Ulysses, the sagacious, answered her: <br> “O royal consort of Laertes’ son! <br> Wilt thou still ask my lineage? I will then <br> Disclose it, but thou wakest in my heart <br> New sorrows. So it ever is with one <br> Who long, like me, is far away from home, <br> Wandering in many realms, and suffering much; <br> But since thou dost require it, thou shalt hear.</p>
<p class="verse">“Crete is a region lying in the midst <br> Of the black deep, a fair and fruitful land, <br> Girt by the waters. Many are the men, <br> Nay, numberless, who make it their abode, <br> And ninety are its cities. Different tongues <br> Are spoken by the dwellers of the isle. <br> In part they are Achaians, and in part <br> Are Cretans of the soil, a gallant stock; <br> There dwell Cydonians, Dorians of three tribes, <br> And proud Pelasgians. Their great capital <br> Is Knossos, where the monarch Minos dwelt, <br> He who at every nine years’ end conferred <br> With Jove almighty; and to him was born <br> Deucalion, my brave father, who begat <br> Me and Idomeneus, the King of Crete. <br> To Ilium in his beaked galleys sailed <br> Idomeneus with Atreus’ sons. My name⁠— <br> A name well known⁠—is Aethon. ’Twas at Crete <br> I saw Ulysses, who received from me <br> The welcome due a guest. A violent wind <br> Had driven him from Maleia and the course <br> That led to Ilium, and had carried him <br> To Crete, and lodged him in the dangerous port <br> Amnisus, close to Ilithyia’s cave, <br> Where scarce his fleet escaped the hurricane. <br> Thence came he to the city, and inquired <br> For King Idomeneus, who was, he said, <br> His dear and honored guest; but he had sailed <br> Ten days before, perhaps eleven, for Troy, <br> In his beaked galleys. To the palace there <br> I led Ulysses, and with liberal cheer <br> Welcomed the chief, for plentifully stored <br> The royal dwelling was. I also gave <br> Meal from the public magazines to him <br> And those who followed him, and dark red wine <br> Brought from the country round, and beeves to slay <br> In sacrifice, that so their hearts might feel <br> No lack of aught. Twelve days the noble Greeks <br> Remained with us. A violent north-wind, <br> Which scarcely suffered them to stand upright <br> On shore, withstood them. Some unfriendly power <br> Had bid it blow; but on the thirteenth day <br> Its fury ceased, and the fleet put to sea.”</p>
<p class="verse">Thus went he on, inventing tales that seemed <br> Like truth. She listened, melting into tears <br> That flowed as when on mountain height the snow, <br> Shed by the west-wind, feels the east-wind’s breath, <br> And flows in water, and the hurrying streams <br> Are filled; so did Penelope’s fair cheeks <br> Seem to dissolve in tears⁠—tears shed for him <br> Who sat beside her even then. He saw <br> His weeping wife, and pitied her at heart; <br> Yet were his eyes like iron or like horn, <br> And moved not in their lids; for artfully <br> He kept his tears from falling. When the queen <br> Had ceased to weep, she answered him and said:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Now, stranger, let me prove thee, if in truth <br> Thou didst receive, as thou hast just declared, <br> In thine abode, my husband and his train <br> Of noble friends. Describe the garb he wore; <br> How looked he, and the friends he brought with him?”</p>
<p class="verse">Ulysses, the sagacious, answered her: <br> “O lady, hard it is to answer thee, <br> So long have I been far away from home. <br> ’Tis now the twentieth year since he was there <br> And left the isle, but, as my memory bids, <br> So will I speak. A fleecy purple cloak <br> Ulysses wore, a double web; the clasp <br> Was golden, with two fastenings, and in front <br> It showed a work of rare design⁠—a hound <br> That held in his forepaws a spotted fawn, <br> Struggling before his open mouth. Although <br> The figures were of gold, we all admired <br> The hound intent to break his victim’s neck, <br> The fawn that, writhing, plied her nimble feet <br> To free herself. Around the hero’s chest <br> And waist I saw a lustrous tunic worn, <br> Soft, like the thin film of the onion dried, <br> And bright as sunshine; many ladies looked <br> With wonder on it. Yet consider this; <br> I know not whether thus attired he left <br> His home, or whether, in the voyage thence, <br> Some comrade gave the garments, or perhaps <br> Some friendly host, for he was very dear <br> To many; among the Greeks were few like him. <br> I gave him, from myself, a brazen sword, <br> And a fair purple cloak, a double web, <br> Besides a tunic reaching to his feet, <br> And with due honors sent him on his way <br> In his good ship. There came and went with him <br> A herald somewhat older than himself; <br> Let me portray him⁠—hunchbacked, swarthy skinned, <br> And curly haired, Eurybates his name. <br> Ulysses honored him above the rest <br> Of his companions, for they thought alike.”</p>
<p class="verse">He ceased; the queen was moved to deeper grief, <br> For she remembered all the tokens well <br> Of which he spake; and when that passionate gust <br> Of weeping ceased, she spake again and said:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Stranger, till now thy presence in these halls <br> Has only moved my pity; thou henceforth <br> Art dear and honored. It was I who gave <br> The garments thou hast told me of; these hands <br> Folded them in my chamber. I put on <br> The glittering clasp to be his ornament, <br> And now I never shall behold him more <br> Returning to his own dear land and home; <br> So cruel was the fate that took him hence <br> To Ilium, in his roomy ship, a town <br> Of evil omen never to be named.”</p>
<p class="verse">Ulysses, the sagacious, answered thus: <br> “O gracious consort of Laertes’ son! <br> Let not thy grief for him whom thou hast lost <br> Wither thy beauty longer, and consume <br> Thy heart. And yet I blame thee not at all; <br> For any wife in losing him to whom <br> She gave herself while yet a maid, and bore <br> Children, will mourn him, though he be in worth <br> Below Ulysses, who, as fame declares, <br> Is like the gods. But cease to grieve, and hear <br> What I shall say, and I shall speak the truth, <br> Nor will I hide from thee that I have heard, <br> But lately from Ulysses, yet alive, <br> And journeying homeward, in the opulent realm <br> Of the Thesprotians, whence he brings with him <br> Much and rare treasure, gathered there among <br> The people. His beloved friends he lost, <br> And his good ship; the black deep swallowed them <br> In coming from Trinacria, for his crew <br> Had slaughtered there the oxen of the Sun. <br> The Sun and Jove were angry; therefore all <br> His comrades perished in the billowy sea; <br> But him upon his galley’s keel the wind <br> Drove to the coast where the Phaeacians dwell, <br> The kinsmen of the gods. They welcomed him, <br> And honored him as if he were a god, <br> And gave him many things, and would have sent <br> The hero safely to his native isle; <br> And here Ulysses would have been long since, <br> But that he deemed it wise to travel far, <br> And gather wealth⁠—for well Ulysses knew, <br> Beyond all other men, the arts of gain, <br> And none in these could think to rival him; <br> So Pheidon, king of the Thesprotians said, <br> Who also, in his palace, swore to me⁠— <br> As to the gods of heaven he poured the wine⁠— <br> That even then a galley was drawn down <br> Into the water, and already manned <br> With rowers, who should take Ulysses home. <br> But me he first dismissed, for at the time <br> A barque of the Thesprotians left the port, <br> Bound for Dulichium’s cornfields. Ere I went <br> He showed the treasures of Ulysses stored <br> In the king’s palace⁠—treasures that might serve <br> To feed the household of another chief <br> To the tenth generation. He who owned <br> That wealth was at Dodona, so the king <br> Declared, inquiring, at the lofty oak <br> Of Jupiter, the counsel of the god <br> How to return to his dear native land, <br> So long a wanderer⁠—whether openly <br> Or else by stealth. So he is safe, and soon <br> Will he be nearer to us; for not long <br> Can he remain away from all his friends <br> And fatherland. To this I plight my oath; <br> Let Jove, the greatest and the best of gods, <br> Be witness, and this hearth of the good prince <br> Ulysses, where I sit, that every word <br> Which I have said to thee will be fulfilled. <br> Within the year Ulysses will return, <br> As this month passes and the next comes in.”</p>
<p class="verse">Then spake the sage Penelope again: <br> “Would that it might be thus, O stranger guest, <br> As thou hast said; then shouldst thou have such thanks <br> And bounty at my hands that everyone <br> Who meets thee should rejoice with thee. And yet <br> The thought abides with me, and so indeed <br> It must be, that Ulysses will no more <br> Return, nor wilt thou find an escort hence; <br> For now no master like Ulysses rules⁠— <br> And what a man was he!⁠—within these walls, <br> To welcome or dismiss the honored guest. <br> But now, ye maidens, let the stranger bathe, <br> And spread his couch with blankets, fleecy cloaks, <br> And showy tapestries, that he may lie <br> Warm till the Morning, in her golden car, <br> Draw near; then with the early morn again <br> Bathe and anoint him, that he may sit down <br> Beside Telemachus prepared to take <br> His morning meal. Ill shall he fare who dares <br> Molest the stranger; he shall have no place <br> Or office here, however he may rage. <br> And how, O stranger, wouldst thou learn that I <br> In mind and thoughtful wisdom am above <br> All other women, if I let thee sit <br> Squalid and meanly clad at banquets here? <br> Short is the life of man, and whoso bears <br> A cruel heart, devising cruel things, <br> On him men call down evil from the gods <br> While living, and pursue him, when he dies, <br> With scoffs. But whoso is of generous heart <br> And harbors generous aims, his guests proclaim <br> His praises far and wide to all mankind, <br> And numberless are they who call him good.”</p>
<p class="verse">Ulysses, the sagacious, answered thus: <br> “O gracious consort of Laertes’ son! <br> Such cloaks and splendid coverings please me not, <br> Since in my long-oared barque I left behind <br> The snowy peaks of Crete. I still will lie, <br> As I am wont through many a sleepless night, <br> On a mean couch to wait the holy Morn <br> Upon her car of gold. I do not like <br> This washing of the feet. No maiden here <br> That ministers to thee may touch my foot; <br> But if among them be some aged dame <br> And faithful, who has suffered in her life <br> As I have suffered, she may touch my feet.”</p>
<p class="verse">And thus the sage Penelope rejoined: <br> “Dear guest⁠—for never to these halls has come <br> A stranger so discreet or better liked <br> By me, so wisely thou dost speak, and well⁠— <br> I have an aged prudent dame, whose care <br> Reared my unfortunate husband. She received <br> The nursling when his mother brought him forth, <br> And she, though small her strength, will wash thy feet. <br> Rise, prudent Eurycleia, thou shalt wash <br> The feet of one whose years must be the same <br> As thy own master’s; such is doubtless now <br> Ulysses, with such wrinkled feet and hands. <br> For quickly doth misfortune make men old.”</p>
<p class="verse">She spake; the aged handmaid hid her face <br> With both her hands, and, shedding bitter tears, <br> Thus sorrowfully to the queen replied:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“My heart is sad for thee, my son; and yet <br> I can do nothing. Can it be that Jove <br> Hates thee beyond all other? though thyself <br> So reverent to the gods? No man on earth <br> Has burned so many thighs of fatling beasts <br> And chosen hecatombs as thou to Jove <br> The Thunderer, with prayer that thou mayst reach <br> A calm old age, and rear thy glorious son <br> To manhood; yet the god hath cut thee off <br> From thy return forever. Even now <br> Perchance the women of some princely house <br> Which he has entered in some distant land <br> Scoff at him as these wretched creatures scoff <br> At thee, O stranger, who, to shun their taunts <br> And insults, wilt not suffer them to wash <br> Thy feet. The sage Penelope commands, <br> And I am not unwilling. I will wash <br> Thy feet, both for her sake and for thy own; <br> For deeply am I moved at sight of thee. <br> Hear what I say: of strangers in distress <br> Come many hither, yet have I beheld <br> No one who bears, in shape and voice and feet, <br> Such likeness to our absent lord as thou.”</p>
<p class="verse">Ulysses, the sagacious, thus replied: <br> “O aged woman, so has it been said <br> By all who have beheld both him and me. <br> They all declare that we are very like <br> Each other; thou in this hast spoken well.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake; she took a shining vase designed <br> For washing feet, and poured cold water in <br> In large abundance, and warm water next. <br> Ulysses, who had sat before the hearth, <br> Moved to a darker spot, for in his mind <br> The thought arose that she might find a scar <br> Upon his limbs in handling them, and thus <br> His secret would be known. She came and bathed <br> His feet, and found the scar. ’Twas where a boar <br> With his white tooth had gashed the limb, as once <br> He journeyed to Parnassus, where he paid <br> A visit to Autolycus and his sons, <br> His mother’s noble father, who excelled <br> All men in craft and oaths, such was the gift <br> Conferred on him by Hermes; for to him <br> Autolycus made grateful offerings, <br> The thighs of lambs and kids, and evermore <br> The god was with him. Once Autolycus <br> Came to the opulent realm of Ithaca, <br> And found his daughter with a son new born; <br> There Eurycleia placed upon his knees <br> The infant, just as he had supped, and said:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Give this dear babe, Autolycus, a name⁠— <br> Thy daughter’s son, vouchsafed to many prayers.”</p>
<p class="verse">And thus Autolycus in answer spake: <br> “Daughter and son-in-law, be his the name <br> That I shall give. In coming to his isle <br> I bear the hate of many⁠—both of men <br> And women⁠—scattered o’er the nourishing earth; <br> Name him Ulysses therefore, and when, grown <br> To man’s estate, he visits the proud halls <br> Reared at Parnassus, where his mother dwelt <br> And my possessions lie, I will bestow <br> A share on him, and send him home rejoiced.”</p>
<p class="verse">And therefore went Ulysses to receive <br> The promised princely gifts. Autolycus <br> And all his sons received him with kind words, <br> And friendly grasp of hands. Amphithea there⁠— <br> His mother’s mother⁠—took him in her arms, <br> And kissed his brow and both his beautiful eyes. <br> Then to his noble sons Autolycus <br> Called to prepare a feast, and they obeyed. <br> They brought and slew a steer of five years old, <br> And flayed and dressed it, hewed the joints apart, <br> And sliced the flesh, and fixed it upon spits, <br> Roasted it carefully, and gave to each <br> His part. So all the day till set of sun <br> They feasted, to the full content of all. <br> And when the sun had set, and earth grew dark, <br> They laid them down, and took the gift of sleep. <br> But when the rosy-fingered Morn appeared, <br> Born of the Dawn, forth issued the young men, <br> The children of Autolycus, with hounds, <br> To hunt, attended by their noble guest, <br> Ulysses. Up the steeps of that high mount <br> Parnassus, clothed with woods, they climbed, and soon <br> Were on its airy heights. The sun, new risen <br> From the deep ocean’s gently flowing stream, <br> Now smote the fields. The hunters reached a dell; <br> The hounds before them tracked the game; behind <br> Followed the children of Autolycus. <br> The generous youth Ulysses, brandishing <br> A spear of mighty length, came pressing on <br> Close to the hounds. There lay a huge wild boar <br> Within a thicket, where moist-blowing winds <br> Came not, nor in his brightness could the sun <br> Pierce with his beams the covert, nor the rain <br> Pelt through, so closely grew the shrubs. The ground <br> Was heaped with sheddings of the withered leaves. <br> Around him came the noise of dogs and men <br> Approaching swiftly. From his lair he sprang <br> And faced them, with the bristles on his neck <br> Upright, and flashing eyes. Ulysses rushed <br> Before the others, with the ponderous spear <br> Raised high in his strong hand intent to smite. <br> The boar was first to strike; he dealt a blow <br> Sidelong, and gashed his foe above the knee, <br> And tore the flesh, but left untouched the bone. <br> Ulysses, striking with his burnished spear <br> The boar’s right shoulder, drove the weapon through. <br> He fell with piercing cries amid the dust, <br> And the life left him. Then around their guest <br> The kindly children of Autolycus <br> Came and bound up with care the wound, and stanched <br> With spells the dark blood of the blameless youth, <br> And hastened with him to their father’s home. <br> And when Autolycus and they his sons <br> Had seen him wholly healed, they loaded him <br> With presents, and, rejoicing for his sake, <br> Sent him rejoicing back to Ithaca. <br> His father and his gracious mother there <br> Rejoiced in turn, and asked him of the scar, <br> And how it came, and he related all⁠— <br> How by the white tusk of a savage boar <br> The wound was given on the Parnassian heights, <br> As he was hunting with her father’s sons.</p>
<p class="verse">The aged woman, as she took the foot <br> Into her hands, perceived by touch the scar, <br> And, letting fall the limb, it struck the vase. <br> Loud rang the brass, the vase was overturned, <br> And poured the water forth. At once a rush <br> Of gladness and of grief came o’er her heart. <br> Tears filled her eyes, and her clear voice was choked. <br> She touched Ulysses on the chin, and said:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Dear child! thou art Ulysses, of a truth. <br> I knew thee not till I had touched the scar.”</p>
<p class="verse">So speaking, toward Penelope she turned <br> Her eyes, about to tell her that her lord <br> Was in the palace; but the queen saw not, <br> And all that passed was unperceived by her, <br> For Pallas turned her thoughts another way. <br> Meantime, Ulysses on the nurse’s throat <br> Laid his right hand, and with the other drew <br> The aged woman nearer him, and said:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Nurse, wouldst thou ruin me, who drew long since <br> Milk from thy bosom, and who now return, <br> After much suffering borne for twenty years, <br> To mine own land? Now then, since thou hast learned <br> The truth⁠—by prompting of some god, no doubt⁠— <br> Keep silence, lest some others in the house <br> Should learn it also. Else⁠—I tell thee this, <br> And will perform my word⁠—if God permit <br> That I o’ercome the arrogant suitor-crew, <br> Nurse as thou art, I spare not even thee, <br> When in these halls the other maidens die.”</p>
<p class="verse">Then thus the prudent Eurycleia said: <br> “What words, my son, have passed thy lips? for well <br> Thou knowest my firm mind; it never yields. <br> Like solid rock or steel I keep my trust. <br> This let me tell thee, and, I pray thee, keep <br> My words in mind. If, by the aid of God, <br> Thou overcome the arrogant suitor-crew, <br> Then will I name the handmaids that disgrace <br> Thy household, and point out the innocent.”</p>
<p class="verse">Ulysses, the sagacious, thus rejoined: <br> “Why name them, nurse? It needs not. I myself <br> Shall watch them, and shall know them all. Hold thou <br> Thy peace, and leave the issue with the gods.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake; the aged woman left the place <br> To bring a second bath, for on the floor <br> The first was spilled. When she had bathed his feet <br> And made them smooth with oil, Ulysses drew <br> Close to the hearth his seat again, to take <br> The warmth, and with his tatters hid the scar. <br> And thus the sage Penelope began:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Stranger, but little longer will I yet <br> Inquire; the hour of grateful rest is near <br> For those who, though unhappy, can receive <br> The balm of slumber. Yet for me some god <br> Appoints immeasurable grief. All day <br> In sorrows and in sighs, my solace is <br> To oversee my maidens at their tasks <br> Here in the palace; but when evening comes, <br> And all betake themselves to rest, I lie <br> Upon my couch, and sorrows thick and sharp <br> Awake new misery in my heart. As when, <br> In the fresh spring, the swarthy Nightingale, <br> Daughter of Pandarus, among thick leaves <br> Sings sweetly to the woods, and, changing oft <br> The strain, pours forth her voice of many notes, <br> Lamenting the beloved Itylus, <br> Her son by royal Zethos, whom she smote <br> Unwittingly, and slew; with such quick change <br> My mind is tossed from thought to thought. I muse <br> Whether to keep my place beside my son, <br> And hold what here is mine, my dower, my maids <br> And high-roofed halls, as one who still reveres <br> Her husband’s bed, and heeds the public voice, <br> Or follow one of the Achaian chiefs, <br> The noblest of the wooers, and the one <br> Who offers marriage presents without stint. <br> My son’s green years, while he was yet a boy, <br> Unripe in mind, allowed me not to wed, <br> And leave his father’s home; but he is grown, <br> And on the verge of manhood. He desires <br> That I should leave the palace, for his wrath <br> Is great against the men who waste his wealth. <br> Hear, and interpret now a dream of mine: <br> Within these courts are twenty geese that eat <br> Corn from the water, and I look on them <br> Pleased and amused. From off a mountain came <br> A hook-beaked eagle, broke their necks, and left <br> Their bodies strewn about the palace dead, <br> And soared again into the air of heaven. <br> I wept and moaned, although it was a dream; <br> And round me came the fair-haired Grecian maids, <br> Lamenting wildly that the bird of prey <br> Had slain my geese. Then came the eagle back, <br> And took his perch upon the jutting roof, <br> And thus bespake me in a human voice:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“ ‘O daughter of Icarius, the renowned! <br> Let not thy heart be troubled; this is not <br> A dream, but a true vision, and will be <br> Fulfilled. The geese denote the suitor-train, <br> And I, who was an eagle once, am come, <br> Thy husband, now to end them utterly.’</p>
<p class="verse">“He spake; my slumbers left me, and I looked, <br> And saw the geese that in the palace still <br> Were at their trough, and feeding as before.”</p>
<p class="verse">And thus Ulysses, the sagacious, said: <br> “Lady, the dream that visited thy sleep <br> Cannot be wrested to another sense. <br> Ulysses has himself revealed to thee <br> The way of its fulfillment. Death is near <br> The suitors, and not one escapes his doom.”</p>
<p class="verse">Then spake the sage Penelope again: <br> “Of dreams, O stranger, some are meaningless <br> And idle, and can never be fulfilled. <br> Two portals are there for their shadowy shapes, <br> Of ivory one, and one of horn. The dreams <br> That come through the carved ivory deceive <br> With promises that never are made good; <br> But those which pass the doors of polished horn, <br> And are beheld of men, are ever true. <br> And yet I cannot hope that my strange dream <br> Came through them, though my son and I would both <br> Rejoice if it were so. This let me say, <br> And heed me well. Tomorrow brings to us <br> The hateful morn which takes me from my home, <br> The palace of Ulysses. I shall now <br> Propose a contest. In the palace court <br> Ulysses in a row set up twelve stakes, <br> Like props that hold a galley up; each stake <br> Had its own ring; he stood afar, and sent <br> An arrow through them all. I shall propose <br> This contest to the suitors. He who bends <br> The bow with easy mastery, and sends <br> Through the twelve rings an arrow, I will take <br> To follow from the palace where I passed <br> My youthful married life⁠—a beautiful home, <br> And stored with wealth; a home which I shall long <br> Remember, even in my nightly dreams.”</p>
<p class="verse">Ulysses, the sagacious, answered thus: <br> “O gracious consort of Laertes’ son! <br> Let not this contest be delayed; the man <br> Of ready wiles, Ulysses, will be here <br> Ere, tampering with the hero’s polished bow, <br> The suitors shall prevail to stretch the cord, <br> And send an arrow through the rings of steel.”</p>
<p class="verse">And thus the sage Penelope rejoined: <br> “Stranger, if, sitting in the palace here, <br> Thou still wouldst entertain me as thou dost, <br> Sleep would not fall upon my lids; and yet <br> Sleepless the race of mortals cannot be, <br> So have the gods ordained, who measure out <br> His lot to man upon the nourishing earth. <br> I to the upper rooms withdraw, to take <br> My place upon the couch which has become <br> To me a place of sorrow and of tears <br> Since my Ulysses went away to Troy, <br> That fatal town which should be named no more. <br> And I will lay me down; but thou remain <br> Within these walls, and make the floor thy bed, <br> Or let these maidens spread a couch for thee.”</p>
<p class="verse">Penelope, thus having spoken, went <br> Up to her royal bower, but not alone; <br> Her maids went with her. When they were within <br> She wept for her dear husband, till at length <br> The blue-eyed Pallas graciously distilled <br> Upon her closing lids the balm of sleep.</p>
