<p class="lede">The souls of the suitors conducted to Hades by Mercury⁠—Agamemnon and Achilles in Hades⁠—Their meeting with the souls of the suitors, and narrative of Amphimedon⁠—Meeting and mutual recognition of Ulysses and his father in the orchard at Ithaca⁠—Insurrection of the Ithacans, with Eupeithes, the father of Antinoüs, at their head⁠—The revolt quelled, Eupeithes slain by Laertes and a lasting peace made between Ulysses and his subject.</p>
<p class="verse">Cyllenian Hermes summoned forth the souls <br> Of the slain suitors. In his hand he bore <br> The beautiful golden wand, with which at will <br> He shuts the eyes of men, or opens them <br> From sleep. With this he guided on their way <br> The ghostly rout; they followed, uttering <br> A shrilly wail. As when a flock of bats, <br> Deep in a dismal cavern, fly about <br> And squeak, if one have fallen from the place <br> Where, clinging to each other and the rock, <br> They rested, so that crowd of ghosts went forth <br> With shrill and plaintive cries. Before them moved <br> Beneficent Hermes through those dreary ways, <br> And past the ocean stream they went, and past <br> Leucadia’s rock, the portals of the Sun, <br> And people of the land of dreams, until <br> They reached the fields of asphodel, where dwell <br> The souls, the bodiless forms of those who die.</p>
<p class="verse">And there they found the soul of Peleus’ son, <br> His friend Patroclus, and the blameless chief <br> Antilochus, and Ajax, who excelled <br> In stature and in form all other Greeks <br> Save the great son of Peleus. These were grouped <br> Around Achilles. Then approached the ghost <br> Of Agamemnon, Atreus’ son; he seemed <br> In sorrow, and around him others stood, <br> Who in the palace of Aegisthus met <br> Their fate and died. The son of Peleus took <br> The word, and spake to Agamemnon thus:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Atrides, we had thought that Jove, who wields <br> The thunder, favored thee, through all thy years, <br> Beyond all other men⁠—thou didst bear rule <br> Over so many and such valiant men <br> Upon the plain of Troy, where we of Greece <br> Endured such sufferings. Yet all too soon <br> The cruel doom of death, which no man born <br> Of woman can escape, has fallen on thee. <br> O, if amid the honors of thy sway <br> That doom had overtaken thee, while yet <br> In Troy’s far realm, then would the assembled Greeks <br> Have built a tomb to thee! Thou wouldst have left <br> A heritage of glory to thy son; <br> Now hast thou died a most unhappy death.”</p>
<p class="verse">And then the soul of Agamemnon said: <br> “Fortunate son of Peleus, godlike chief <br> Achilles, who didst die upon the field <br> Of Ilium, far from Argos, while there fell <br> Around thee many of the bravest sons <br> Of Troy and Greece, who fought for thee, and thou <br> Wert lying in thy mighty bulk, amid <br> Whirlwinds of dust, forgetful evermore <br> Of horsemanship. All that day long we fought, <br> Nor stayed our hands till Jove, to part us, sent <br> A hurricane. When we had borne thee thence <br> And brought thee to the fleet, upon a bier <br> We laid thee, pouring o’er thy shapely limbs <br> Warm water, and anointing them with oil. <br> Round thee the Achaians stood in tears, hot tears, <br> And cut their hair away. From ocean’s depth <br> Thy mother, when she heard the tidings, rose <br> With her immortal sea-nymphs. Mournfully <br> Came o’er the waves the sound of their lament. <br> Trembled the Greeks with fear, and, rushing forth, <br> Would have sought refuge in their roomy ships, <br> If Nestor, wise in ancient lore, and known <br> For counsels ever safe, had not restrained <br> Their haste, and thus declared his prudent thought:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“ ‘Stay, Argives, youths of Greece; think not of flight! <br> It is his mother; from the sea she comes <br> To her dead son, and brings her deathless nymphs.’</p>
<p class="verse">“He spake; his words withheld the valiant Greeks <br> From flight. And now around thee came and stood <br> The daughters of the Ancient of the Deep, <br> Lamenting bitterly. Upon thy corse <br> They put ambrosial robes. The Muses nine <br> Bewailed thee with sweet voices, answering <br> Each other. Then wouldst thou have seen no one <br> Of all the Argive host with eyes unwet, <br> The Muses’ song so moved them. Seventeen days <br> And nights we mourned thee⁠—both the immortal ones <br> And mortals. On the eighteenth day we gave <br> Thy body to the fire, and at the pile <br> Slew many fatling ewes, and many an ox <br> With crooked horns. In raiment of the gods <br> The fire consumed thee midst anointing oils <br> And honey. Many heroes of our host <br> In armor and in chariots, or on foot, <br> Contended round thy funeral pyre in games, <br> And mighty was the din. And when at length <br> The fires of Vulcan had consumed thy flesh, <br> We gathered up at morning thy white bones, <br> Achilles, pouring over them pure wine <br> And fragrant oils. Thy mother brought a vase <br> Of gold, which Bacchus gave, she said, the work <br> Of Vulcan the renowned, and in it now, <br> Illustrious son of Peleus, thy white bones <br> Are lying, and with thine are mingled those <br> Of dead Patroclus Menoetiades. <br> Apart we placed the ashes of thy friend <br> Antilochus, whom thou didst honor most <br> After the slain Patroclus. O’er all these <br> The sacred army of the warlike Greeks <br> Built up a tomb magnificently vast <br> Upon a cape of the broad Hellespont, <br> There to be seen, far off upon the deep, <br> By those who now are born, or shall be born <br> In future years. Thy mother, having first <br> Prayed to the gods, appointed noble games, <br> Within the circus, for the Achaian chiefs. <br> Full often have I seen the funeral rites <br> Of heroes, when the youth, their chieftain dead, <br> Were girded for the games, and strove to win <br> The prizes; but I most of all admired <br> Those which the silver-footed Thetis gave <br> To mark thy burial, who wert loved by all <br> The immortals. So thou hast not lost by death <br> Thy fame, Achilles, and among the tribes <br> Of men thy glory will be ever great; <br> But what hath it availed me to have brought <br> The war on Ilium to an end, since Jove <br> Doomed me to be destroyed on my return, <br> Slain by Aegisthus and my guilty wife?”</p>
<p class="verse">So talked they with each other. Now approached <br> The herald Argus-queller, bringing down <br> The souls of suitors by Ulysses slain. <br> Both chiefs moved toward them, wondering at the sight. <br> The soul of Agamemnon, Atreus’ son, <br> Knew well-renowned Amphimedon, whose birth <br> Was from Melanthius, and by whom he once <br> Was welcomed to his house in Ithaca; <br> And him the son of Atreus first bespake:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Amphimedon, what sad mischance has brought <br> You all, who seem like chosen men, and all <br> Of equal age, into these drear abodes <br> Beneath the earth? ’Twere hard indeed to find, <br> In a whole city, nobler forms of men. <br> Has Neptune wrecked you in your ships at sea <br> With fierce winds and huge waves, or armed men <br> Smitten you on the land, while carrying off <br> Their beeves and sheep, or fighting to defend <br> Your wives and city? Tell me, for I claim <br> To have been once your guest. Rememberest thou <br> I lodged in thy own palace when I came <br> With godlike Menelaus, and besought <br> Ulysses to unite his gallant fleet <br> To ours, and sail for Troy. A whole month long <br> Were we in crossing the wide sea, and hard <br> We found the task to gain as our ally <br> Ulysses, the destroyer of walled towns.”</p>
<p class="verse">The soul of dead Amphimedon replied: <br> “Atrides Agamemnon, far renowned, <br> And king of men, I well remember all <br> Of which thou speakest; I will now relate, <br> And truly, how we met our evil end. <br> We wooed the wife of the long-absent chief <br> Ulysses; she rejected not nor yet <br> Granted our suit, detested as it was, <br> But, meditating our destruction, planned <br> This shrewd device. She laid upon the loom <br> Within her rooms a web of delicate threads, <br> Ample in length and breadth, and thus she said <br> To all of us: ‘Young princes, who are come <br> To woo me⁠—since Ulysses is no more, <br> My noble husband⁠—urge me not, I pray, <br> To marriage, till I finish in the loom⁠— <br> That so my threads may not be spun in vain⁠— <br> A funeral vesture for the hero-chief <br> Laertes, when his fatal hour shall come, <br> With death’s long sleep; else some Achaian dame <br> Might blame me, should I leave without a shroud <br> Him who in life possessed such ample wealth.’ <br> Such were her words, and easily they won <br> Upon our generous minds. So went she on <br> Weaving that ample web, and every night <br> Unravelled it by torchlight. Three full years <br> She practised thus, and by the fraud deceived <br> The Grecian youths; but when the hours had brought <br> The fourth year round, a woman who knew all <br> Revealed the mystery, and we ourselves <br> Saw her unravelling the ample web. <br> Thenceforth constrained, and with unwilling hands, <br> She finished it. And when at length she showed <br> The vesture she had woven, the broad web <br> That she had bleached to brightness like the sun’s <br> Or like the moon’s, some hostile deity <br> Brought back Ulysses to a distant nook <br> Of his own fields, and to his swineherd’s lodge. <br> And thither also came in his black ship <br> His son, returning from the sandy coast <br> Of Pylos. Thence the twain, when they had planned <br> To slay the suitors, came within the walls <br> Of the great city; first Telemachus, <br> And after him Ulysses, with his guide <br> The swineherd. He was clad in sordid weeds, <br> And seemed a wretched beggar, very old, <br> Propped on a staff. In that disguise of rags <br> None knew him, as he suddenly appeared, <br> Not even the oldest of us all. Harsh words <br> And blows we gave him. He endured them all <br> Awhile with patience, smitten and reviled <br> In his own palace. Moved at length by Jove, <br> He and his son Telemachus bore off <br> The shining weapons from the hall, to lie <br> In a far chamber, and barred all the doors. <br> Then, prompted by her husband’s craft, the queen <br> Proposed a game of archery, with bow <br> And rings of hoary steel, to all of us <br> Ill-fated suitors. This drew on our death. <br> Not one of us could bend that sturdy bow, <br> None had the strength. But as it passed from us <br> Into Ulysses’ hands, we loudly chid <br> The bearer, and forbade him, but in vain. <br> Telemachus alone with stern command <br> Bade him deliver it. When in his hands <br> The much-enduring chief, Ulysses, took <br> The bow, he drew the string with ease, and sent <br> A shaft through all the rings. He sprang and stood <br> Upon the threshold; at his feet he poured <br> The winged arrows, cast a terrible glance <br> Around him, and laid King Antinoüs dead, <br> Then sent the fatal shafts at those who stood <br> Before him; side by side they fell and died. <br> Some god, we saw, was with them, as they rushed <br> Upon us mightily, and chased us through <br> The palace, slaying us on every side; <br> And fearful were the groans of dying men, <br> As skulls were cloven, and the pavement swam <br> With blood. Such, Agamemnon, was the fate <br> By which we perished. Now our bodies lie <br> Neglected at the palace; for not yet <br> Our kindred, dwelling in our homes, have heard <br> The tidings, nor have come to cleanse our wounds <br> From the dark blood, and lay us on the bier <br> With tears⁠—such honors as are due the dead.”</p>
<p class="verse">In turn the soul of Agamemnon spake: <br> “Son of Laertes, fortunate and wise, <br> Ulysses! thou by feats of eminent might <br> And valor dost possess thy wife again. <br> And nobly minded is thy blameless queen, <br> The daughter of Icarius, faithfully <br> Remembering him to whom she gave her troth <br> While yet a virgin. Never shall the fame <br> Of his great valor perish, and the gods <br> Themselves shall frame, for those who dwell on earth, <br> Sweet strains in praise of sage Penelope. <br> Not such was she who treacherously slew <br> The husband of her youth⁠—she of the house <br> Of Tyndarus. Her name among mankind <br> Shall be the hateful burden of a song; <br> And great is the dishonor it has brought <br> On women, even the faithful and the good.”</p>
<p class="verse">So talked they with each other, standing there <br> In Pluto’s realm beneath the vaulted earth. <br> Meantime Ulysses, hastening from the town, <br> Came to the fair fields of Laertes, tilled <br> With care. Laertes, after years of toil, <br> Acquired them. There his dwelling stood; a shed <br> Encircled it, where ate and sat and slept <br> The servants of the household, who fulfilled <br> His slightest wish. An old Sicilian dame <br> Was there, who waited, in that distant spot, <br> On her old master with assiduous care. <br> And then Ulysses to his followers said:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Go into that fair dwelling, and with speed <br> Slay for our feast the fattest of the swine. <br> I go to prove my father; I would learn <br> Whether he knows me when he sees my face, <br> Or haply knows me not, so long away.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake, and laid his weapons in their hands. <br> Straight toward the house they went. Ulysses passed <br> Into the fruitful orchard, there to prove <br> His father. Going down and far within <br> The garden-plot, he found not Dolius there, <br> Nor any of the servants, nor his sons. <br> All were abroad, old Dolius leading them. <br> They gathered thorns to fence the garden-grounds. <br> There, delving in that fertile spot, around <br> A newly planted tree, Ulysses saw <br> His father only, sordidly arrayed <br> In a coarse tunic, patched and soiled. He wore <br> Patched greaves of bullock’s hide upon his thighs, <br> A fence against the thorns; and on his hands <br> gloves, to protect them from the prickly stems <br> Of bramble; and upon his head a cap <br> Of goatskin. There he brooded o’er his grief. <br> Him when the much-enduring chief beheld, <br> Wasted with age and sorrow-worn, he stopped <br> Beside a lofty pear-tree’s stem and wept, <br> And pondered whether he should kiss and clasp <br> His father in his arms, and tell him all, <br> How he had reached his native land and home, <br> Or question first and prove him. Musing thus, <br> It pleased him to begin with sportive words; <br> And thus resolved, divine Ulysses drew <br> Near to his father stooping at his task, <br> And loosening the hard earth about a tree, <br> And thus the illustrious son accosted him:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“O aged man! there is no lack of skill <br> In tending this fair orchard, which thy care <br> Keeps flourishing; no growth is there of fig, <br> Vine, pear, or olive, or of plants that grow <br> In borders, that has missed thy friendly hand. <br> Yet let me say, and be thou not displeased, <br> Thou art ill cared for, burdened as thou art <br> With years, and squalid, and in mean attire. <br> It cannot be that for thy idleness <br> Thy master treats thee thus; nor is there seen <br> Aught servile in thy aspect⁠—in thy face <br> Or stature; thou art rather like a king; <br> Thou seemest one who should enjoy the bath <br> And banquet, and lie soft⁠—for this befits <br> Old men like thee. Now say, and tell me true, <br> Who may thy master be? whose orchard this <br> Which thou dost tend? And, more than this, declare, <br> For much I long to know, if I am come <br> To Ithaca, as I just now was told <br> By one who met me as I came⁠—a man <br> Not overwise, who would not stop to tell <br> What I desired to learn, nor bear to hear <br> My questions, when I asked him if a guest <br> Of mine were living yet in health, or dead <br> And in the realm of Pluto. Let me speak <br> Of him, and mark me well, I pray; I lodged <br> Once, in my native land, a man who came <br> Into my house, and never stranger yet <br> More welcome was than he. He was by birth <br> Of Ithaca, he said, Laertes’ son, <br> And grandson of Arcesias. Him I led <br> Beneath my roof, and hospitably lodged, <br> And feasted in the plenty of my home, <br> And gave such gifts as might become a host⁠— <br> Seven talents of wrought gold, a silver cup <br> All over rough with flowers, twelve single cloaks, <br> Twelve mats, twelve mantles passing beautiful, <br> And tunics twelve, and, chosen by himself, <br> Twelve graceful damsels, skilled in household arts.”</p>
<p class="verse">And then his father answered, shedding tears: <br> “Thou art indeed, O stranger, in the land <br> Of which thou dost inquire, but wicked men <br> And lawless now possess it. Thou hast given <br> Thy generous gifts in vain; yet hadst thou found <br> Ulysses living yet in Ithaca, <br> Then would he have dismissed thee recompensed <br> With gifts and liberal cheer, as is the due <br> Of him who once has been our host. Yet say, <br> And truly say, how many years have passed <br> Since thou didst lodge my son, if he it was, <br> Thy hapless guest, whom, far away from home <br> And all his friends, the creatures of the deep, <br> And the foul birds of air, and beasts of prey, <br> Already have devoured. No mother mourned <br> His death and wrapped him in his shroud, nor I, <br> His father; nor did chaste Penelope, <br> His consort nobly dowered, bewail the man <br> She loved upon his bier with eyes dissolved <br> In tears, as fitting was⁠—an honor due <br> To those who die. Now, further, truly tell, <br> For I would learn, what is thy name, and whence <br> Thou comest, from what tribe, thy city where, <br> And who thy parents. Where is the good ship <br> At anchor which has brought thee and thy friends? <br> Or hast thou landed from another’s barque, <br> Which put thee on the shore and left the isle?”</p>
<p class="verse">Ulysses, the sagacious, answered thus: <br> “I will tell all and truly. I am come <br> From Alybas; a stately dwelling there <br> Is mine, Apheidas is my father, son <br> Of royal Polypemon, and my name <br> Eperitus. Some deity has warped <br> My course astray from the Sicanian coast, <br> And brought me hitherward against my will. <br> My barque lies yonder, stationed by the field <br> Far from the city. This is the fifth year <br> Since parting with me thy Ulysses left <br> My native land for his, ill-fated man! <br> Yet there were flights of birds upon the right <br> Of happy presage as he sailed, and I <br> Dismissed him cheerfully, and cheerfully <br> He went. We hoped that we might yet become <br> Each other’s guests, exchanging princely gifts.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake, and a dark cloud of sorrow came <br> Over Laertes. With both hands he grasped <br> The yellow dust, and over his white head <br> Shed it with piteous groans. Ulysses felt <br> His heart within him melted; the hot breath <br> Rushed through his nostrils as he looked upon <br> His well-beloved father, and he sprang <br> And kissed and clasped him in his arms, and said:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Nay, I am he, my father; I myself <br> Am he of whom thou askest. I am come <br> To mine own country in the twentieth year. <br> But calm thyself, refrain from tears, and grieve <br> No more, and let me tell thee, in a word, <br> I have slain all the suitors in my halls, <br> And so avenged their insolence and crimes.”</p>
<p class="verse">And then Laertes spake again, and said: <br> “If now thou be Ulysses, my lost son, <br> Give some plain token, that I may believe.”</p>
<p class="verse">Ulysses, the sagacious, answered thus: <br> “First, then, behold with thine own eyes the scar <br> Which once the white tusk of a forest boar <br> Inflicted on Parnassus, when I made <br> The journey thither, by thy own command, <br> And by my gracious mother’s, to receive <br> Gifts which her father, King Autolycus, <br> Once promised, when he came to Ithaca. <br> And listen to me further; let me name <br> The trees which in thy well-tilled orchard grounds <br> Thou gavest me; I asked them all of thee, <br> When by thy side I trod the garden walks, <br> A little boy. We went among the trees, <br> And thou didst name them. Of the pear thirteen, <br> And of the apple ten thou gavest me, <br> And forty fig-trees; and thou didst engage <br> To give me fifty rows of vines, each row <br> Of growth to feed the winepress. Grapes are there <br> Of every flavor when the hours of Jove <br> Shall nurse them into ripeness from on high.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake; a trembling seized the old man’s heart <br> And knees, as he perceived how true were all <br> The tokens which Ulysses gave. He threw <br> Round his dear son his arms. The hardy chief, <br> Ulysses, drew him fainting to his heart. <br> But when the old man’s strength revived, and calm <br> Came o’er his spirit, thus he spake again:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“O father Jove, assuredly the gods <br> Dwell on the Olympian height, since we behold <br> The arrogant suitors punished for their crimes. <br> Yet much I fear lest all the Ithacans <br> Throng hither, and send messages to rouse <br> Against us all the Cephallenian states.”</p>
<p class="verse">Ulysses, the sagacious, answered thus: <br> “Take courage; let no thought like that disturb <br> Thy mind; but let us hasten to the house. <br> Telemachus is there, with whom I sent <br> The herdsman and the swineherd, bidding them <br> Make ready with all speed our evening meal.”</p>
<p class="verse">Thus talked the twain, and toward the dwelling took <br> Their way, and entering the commodious rooms <br> They found Telemachus, and by his side <br> The herdsman and the keeper of the swine, <br> Dividing for the feast the plenteous meats, <br> And mingling the dark wine. Then to the bath <br> Came the Sicilian dame, and ministered <br> To the large-souled Laertes, and with oil <br> Anointed him, and wrapped a sumptuous cloak <br> About him. Pallas gave the monarch’s limbs <br> An ampler roundness; taller to the sight <br> He stood, and statelier. As he left the bath, <br> His son beheld with wonder in his eyes, <br> So like a god Laertes seemed, and thus <br> Ulysses said to him in winged words:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Someone among the ever-living gods <br> Hath surely shed, O father, on thy form <br> And aspect all this grace and majesty.”</p>
<p class="verse">The sage Laertes answered: “Father Jove, <br> And Pallas and Apollo! would that I <br> Were now as when I took the citadel <br> Of Nericus, the strongly built, beside <br> The seashore of Epirus, leading on <br> My Cephallenians! With such strength as then, <br> Armed for the fray, I would have met and fought <br> The suitors in the palace yesterday, <br> And struck down many lifeless in the hall, <br> And greatly would thy spirit have rejoiced.”</p>
<p class="verse">So talked they with each other. When they all <br> Ceased from their task, and saw their meal prepared, <br> They sat them down in order on the thrones <br> And seats, and each put forth his hand and shared <br> The banquet. Now approached an aged man, <br> Dolius, attended by his sons, who came <br> Weary with toil, for the Sicilian dame, <br> The nurse who reared them, went and summoned them⁠— <br> She who in his late age with faithful care <br> Cherished the father. These, when at the board <br> They saw Ulysses, and knew who he was, <br> Stopped in the hall astonished. Instantly <br> Ulysses called to them with friendly words:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Sit at the board, old man; let none of you <br> Give way to blank amazement. Know that we, <br> Though keen our appetite for this repast, <br> Have waited long, expecting your return.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake, and Dolius sprang with outstretched arms <br> And seized Ulysses by the hand, and kissed <br> The wrist; and thus in winged words he spake:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Dear master! since thou art returned to us, <br> Who longed and yet expected not to see <br> Thy face again⁠—since some divinity <br> Has led thee hither⁠—hail! and great may be <br> Thy happiness, and may the gods bestow <br> All blessings on thee! But declare, for I <br> Would gladly know, if sage Penelope <br> Have heard the tidings yet of thy return, <br> Or must we send them by a messenger.”</p>
<p class="verse">Ulysses, the sagacious, answered thus: <br> “My aged friend, she knows already all. <br> Why wouldst thou take that care upon thyself?”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake, and Dolius on a polished seat <br> Sat down, but round the great Ulysses came <br> His sons, and welcomed him with loving words, <br> And hung upon his hand, and then they took <br> Their places by their father. So they sat <br> Beneath Laertes’ roof, and banqueted.</p>
<p class="verse">Now through the city meantime swiftly ran <br> The rumor that the suitors all had met <br> A bloody death. No sooner had men heard <br> The tidings than they came with cries and moans <br> Before the palace, moving to and fro. <br> Each carried forth his dead, and gave to each <br> His funeral rites, except to those who came <br> From distant cities; these they put on board <br> Swift-sailing galleys of the fishermen, <br> That they might bear them home. And then they came <br> Sorrowing together in the marketplace. <br> There, when the assembly now was full, arose <br> Eupeithes and addressed them. In his heart <br> Was sorrow, that could never be consoled, <br> For his slain son Antinoüs, who was first <br> To fall before Ulysses. Weeping rose <br> The father, and harangued the assembly thus:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Great things, indeed, my friends, hath this man done <br> For us Achaians. Many valiant men <br> He gathered in his ships and led abroad, <br> And lost his gallant ships, and lost his men; <br> And now, returning, he has put to death <br> The best of all the Cephallenian race. <br> Come, then, and ere he find a safe retreat <br> In Pylos, or in hallowed Elis, where <br> The Epeians rule, pursue him; endless shame <br> Will be our portion else, and they who live <br> In future years will hear of our disgrace. <br> If we avenge not on these men of blood <br> The murder of our sons and brothers, life <br> Will not be sweet to me, and I would go <br> At once, and gladly, down among the dead. <br> Rise, then, and fall upon them ere they flee.”</p>
<p class="verse">So spake he, weeping; and the Greeks were moved <br> With pity as they heard him. Now appeared <br> The herald Medon and the sacred bard, <br> As, rising from the sleep of night, they left <br> The palace of Ulysses. They stood forth <br> Amid the multitude, who all beheld <br> With wonder. Then sagacious Medon spake:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Give ear, ye men of Ithaca, and know <br> That not without the approval of the gods <br> Ulysses hath done this. I saw, myself, <br> One of the immortals taking part with him, <br> In all things like to Mentor. Now the god <br> Standing before Ulysses strengthened him <br> For combat, and now drove the routed band <br> Of suitors through the hall; in heaps they fell.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake, and all who heard were pale with fear. <br> The aged hero, Halitherses, son <br> Of Mastor, then came forward; he alone <br> Knew what was past and what was yet to come, <br> And, wisely judging, to the assembly said:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Hear now my words, ye men of Ithaca. <br> Through your own wrong all this has come to pass. <br> To me ye would not hearken, nor obey <br> When Mentor, shepherd of the people, spake. <br> On the mad doings of your sons ye put <br> No curb, nor checked the guilty insolence <br> That dared to waste the substance and insult <br> The consort of a man of eminent worth, <br> Who, so they thought, would nevermore return. <br> Now be it as I counsel; let us not <br> Go forth to draw down evil on our heads.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake; but more than half the assembly rushed <br> Abroad with shouts; the others kept their place <br> Together. Ill the augur’s speech had pleased <br> The most. Eupeithes had persuaded them. <br> They flew to arms, and when they had put on <br> The glittering brass, they mustered in close ranks <br> Before the spacious city. At their head <br> Eupeithes led them on, who madly deemed <br> Himself the avenger of his slaughtered son. <br> Yet he from that encounter nevermore <br> Was to return; his fate o’ertook him there.</p>
<p class="verse">Then Pallas thus addressed Saturnian Jove: <br> “Our Father, son of Saturn, king of kings, <br> Tell me, I pray, the purpose of thy heart <br> Yet unrevealed. Shall there be cruel war <br> And deadly combats, or wilt thou ordain <br> That these shall henceforth dwell in amity?”</p>
<p class="verse">And cloud-compelling Jove made answer thus: <br> “My child, why ask me? Was it not with thee <br> A cherished purpose, that, returning home, <br> Ulysses amply should avenge himself <br> Upon the suitors? Do, then, as thou wilt. <br> Yet this, as the most fitting, I advise. <br> Now that the great Ulysses has avenged <br> His wrongs, let there be made a faithful league <br> With oaths, and let Ulysses ever reign; <br> And we will cause the living to forget <br> Their sons and brothers slain, and all shall dwell <br> In friendship as they heretofore have dwelt, <br> And there shall be prosperity and peace.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake, and eager as she was before, <br> Encouraged by his words, the goddess plunged <br> Down from the summits of the Olympian mount. <br> Now when they all had feasted to the full, <br> The much-enduring chief, Ulysses, said: <br> “Go, one of you, and see if they are near.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake; a son of Dolius at his word <br> Went forth, and, coming to the threshold, stopped. <br> He saw them all at hand, and instantly <br> Bespake Ulysses thus, with winged words: <br> “They are upon us; we must arm at once.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake; they rose, and quickly were in arms. <br> Four were Ulysses and his friends, and six <br> The sons of Dolius. Old Laertes then, <br> And Dolius, put on armor with the rest, <br> Gray-headed as they were, for now their aid <br> Was needed. When they all had clad themselves <br> In shining brass, they threw the portals wide <br> And sallied forth, Ulysses at their head.</p>
<p class="verse">Now Pallas, daughter of almighty Jove, <br> Drew near them. She had taken Mentor’s form <br> And Mentor’s voice. The much-enduring chief, <br> Ulysses, saw her and rejoiced, and said <br> To his beloved son, Telemachus:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Now wilt thou, of thyself, Telemachus, <br> Bethink thee, when thou minglest in the fray <br> That tries men’s valor, not to cast disgrace <br> Upon thy forefathers⁠—a race renowned <br> For manly daring over all the earth.”</p>
<p class="verse">And thus discreet Telemachus replied: <br> “Nay, if thou wilt, my father, thou shalt see <br> That by no lack of valor shall I cast, <br> As thou hast said, dishonor on thy race.”</p>
<p class="verse">Laertes heard them, and rejoiced, and said: <br> “O what a day for me, ye blessed gods, <br> Is this! With what delight I see my son <br> And grandson rivals on the battlefield.”</p>
<p class="verse">And then the blue-eyed Pallas, drawing near <br> Laertes, said: “Son of Arcesias, loved <br> By me beyond all others of my friends, <br> Pray to Jove’s blue-eyed daughter, and to Jove, <br> And brandish thy long spear, and send it forth.”</p>
<p class="verse">So Pallas spake, and breathed into his frame <br> Strength irresistible. The aged chief <br> Prayed to the daughter of almighty Jove, <br> And brandished his long spear and sent it forth. <br> It smote Eupeithes on the helmet’s cheek. <br> The brass stayed not the spear, the blade passed through, <br> And heavily Eupeithes fell to earth, <br> His armor clashing round him as he fell. <br> Then rushed Ulysses and his valiant son <br> Forward, the foremost of their band, and smote <br> Their foes with swords and lancet double-edged, <br> And would have struck them down to rise no more, <br> If Pallas, daughter of the god who bears <br> The aegis, had not with a mighty voice <br> Commanded all the combatants to cease:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Stay, men of Ithaca; withhold your hands <br> From deadly combat. Part, and shed no blood.”</p>
<p class="verse">So Pallas spake, and they grew pale with awe, <br> And fear-struck; as they heard her words they dropped <br> Their weapons all upon the earth. They fled <br> Townward as if for life, while terribly <br> The much-enduring chief Ulysses raised <br> His voice, and shouted after them, and sprang <br> Upon them as an eagle darts through air. <br> Then Saturn’s son sent down a bolt of fire; <br> It fell before his blue-eyed daughter’s feet, <br> And thus the goddess to Ulysses called:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Son of Laertes, nobly born and wise, <br> Ulysses, hold thy hand; restrain the rage <br> Of deadly combat, lest the god who wields <br> The thunder, Saturn’s son, be wroth with thee.”</p>
<p class="verse">She spake, and gladly he obeyed; and then <br> Pallas, the child of aegis-bearing Jove, <br> Plighted, in Mentor’s form with Mentor’s voice, <br> A covenant of peace between the foes.</p>
