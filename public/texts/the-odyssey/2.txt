<p class="lede">The chief men of Ithaca assembled by Telemachus⁠—His complaint of the suitors⁠—Their attempt to justify themselves⁠—Prophecy of the return of Ulysses by the seer, Halitherses⁠—Request of Telemachus for a vessel to visit Pylos and Sparta, in quest of his father, granted by the assembly⁠—Preparations for his departure.</p>
<p class="verse">Now when the Morning, child of Dawn, appeared, <br> The dear son of Ulysses left his bed <br> And put his garments on. His trenchant sword <br> He hung upon his shoulders, and made fast <br> His shapely sandals to his shining feet, <br> And issued from his chamber like a god. <br> At once he bade the clear-voiced heralds call <br> The long-haired Greeks to council. They obeyed, <br> Quickly the chiefs assembled, and when all <br> Were at the appointed place, Telemachus <br> Went to the council, bearing in his hand <br> A brazen spear, yet went he not alone. <br> Two swift dogs followed him, while Pallas shed <br> A heavenly beauty over him, and all <br> Admired him as he came. He took the seat <br> Of his great father, and the aged men <br> Made way for him. And then Aegyptius spake⁠— <br> A hero bowed with age, who much had seen <br> And known. His son, the warlike Antiphus, <br> Went with the great Ulysses in his fleet <br> To courser-breeding Troy, and afterward <br> The cruel Cyclops, in the vaulted cave, <br> Slew him for his last meal. Three other sons <br> There were, and one of these, Eurynomus, <br> Was of the suitor train; the others took <br> Charge of their father’s acres. Never yet <br> Had he forgotten his lost son or ceased <br> To grieve for him, and as he spoke he wept</p>
<p class="verse">“Hear, men of Ithaca, what I shall say. <br> No council, no assembly, have we held <br> Since great Ulysses in his roomy ships <br> Departed from our isle. Who now is he <br> That summons us? On which of our young men <br> Or elders presses this necessity? <br> Is it belike that one of you has heard <br> Of an approaching foe, and can declare <br> The tidings clearly? Or would he propose <br> And urge some other matter which concerns <br> The public weal? A just and generous mind <br> I deem is his, and ’tis my hope that Jove <br> Will bring to pass the good at which he aims.”</p>
<p class="verse">As thus he spake Ulysses’ son rejoiced <br> In his auspicious words, nor longer kept <br> His seat, but, yielding to an inward force, <br> Rose midst them all to speak, while in his hand <br> Pisenor, the sagacious counsellor <br> And herald, placed the sceptre. Then he turned <br> To the old man, Aegyptius, speaking thus:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“O aged man, not far from thee is he <br> Who called this council, as thou soon shalt know <br> Mine chiefly is the trouble; I have brought <br> No news of an approaching foe, which I <br> Was first to hear, and would declare to all, <br> Nor urge I other matters which concern <br> The public weal; my own necessity⁠— <br> The evil that has fallen on my house⁠— <br> Constrains me; it is twofold. First, that I <br> Have lost an excellent father, who was king <br> Among you, and ruled o’er you with a sway <br> As gentle as a father’s. Greater yet <br> Is the next evil, and will soon o’erthrow <br> My house and waste my substance utterly. <br> Suitors, the sons of those who, in our isle, <br> Hold the chief rank, importunately press <br> Round my unwilling mother. They disdain <br> To ask her of Icarius, that the king <br> Her father may endow her, and bestow <br> His daughter on the man who best may gain <br> His favor, but with every day they come <br> Into our palace, sacrificing here <br> Oxen and sheep and fatling goats, and hold <br> High festival, and drink the purple wine <br> Unstinted, with unbounded waste; for here <br> Is no man like Ulysses to repel <br> The mischief from my house. Not such are we <br> As he was, to resist the wrong. We pass <br> For weaklings, immature in valor, yet <br> If I had but the power, assuredly <br> I would resist, for by these men are done <br> Insufferable things, nor does my house <br> Perish with honor. Ye yourselves should feel <br> Shame at these doings; ye should dread reproach <br> From those who dwell around us, and should fear <br> The offended gods, lest they repay these crimes <br> With vengeance. I beseech you, O my friends, <br> Both by Olympian Jove, and her by whom <br> Councils of men are summoned and dissolved⁠— <br> The goddess Themis⁠—that ye all refrain, <br> And leave me to my grief alone, unless <br> Ulysses, my great father, may have done <br> Wrong in his anger to the gallant Greeks, <br> Which ye, by prompting men to acts like these, <br> Seek to avenge on me. Far better ’twere, <br> Should ye yourselves destroy our goods and slay <br> Our herds, since, were it so, there might in time <br> Be some requital. We, from street to street, <br> Would plead continually for recompense, <br> Till all should be restored. But now ye heap <br> Upon me wrongs for which is no redress.”</p>
<p class="verse">Thus angrily he spake, and clashed to earth <br> The sceptre, shedding tears. The people felt <br> Compassion; all were silent for a space, <br> And there was none who dared with railing words <br> Answer Telemachus, save one alone, <br> Antinoüs, who arose and thus replied:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Telemachus, thou youth of braggart speech <br> And boundless in abuse, what hast thou said <br> To our dishonor? Thou wouldst fix on us <br> A brand of shame. The blame is not with us, <br> The Achaian suitors; ’tis thy mother’s fault, <br> Skilled as she is in crafty shifts. ’Tis now <br> Already the third year, and soon will be <br> The fourth, since she began to cozen us. <br> She gives us all to hope, and sends fair words <br> To each by message, yet in her own mind <br> Has other purposes. This shrewd device <br> She planned; she laid upon the loom a web, <br> Delicate, wide, and vast in length, and said <br> Thus to us all: ‘Young princes, who are come <br> To woo me, since Ulysses is no more⁠— <br> My noble husband⁠—urge me not, I pray, <br> To marriage, till I finish in the loom⁠— <br> That so my threads may not be spun in vain⁠— <br> A funeral vesture for the hero-chief <br> Laertes, when his fatal hour shall come <br> With death’s long sleep. Else some Achaian dame <br> Might blame me, should I leave without a shroud <br> Him who in life possessed such ample wealth!’ <br> Such were her words, and easily they wrought <br> Upon our generous minds. So went she on, <br> Weaving that ample web, and every night <br> Unravelled it by torchlight. Three full years <br> She practised thus, and by the fraud deceived <br> The Grecian youths; but when the hours had brought <br> The fourth year round, a woman who knew all <br> Revealed the mystery, and we ourselves <br> Saw her unravelling the ample web. <br> Thenceforth, constrained, and with unwilling hands, <br> She finished it. Now let the suitors make <br> Their answer to thy words, that thou mayst know <br> Our purpose fully, and the Achaians all <br> May know it likewise. Send thy mother hence, <br> Requiring that she wed the suitor whom <br> Her father chooses and herself prefers. <br> But if she still go on to treat the sons <br> Of Greece with such despite, too confident <br> In gifts which Pallas has bestowed on her <br> So richly, noble arts, and faculties <br> Of mind, and crafty shifts, beyond all those <br> Of whom we ever heard that lived of yore, <br> The bright-haired ladies of the Achaian race, <br> Tyro, Alcmena, and Mycenè, famed <br> For glossy tresses, none of them endowed <br> As is Penelope, though this last shift <br> Be ill devised⁠—so long will we consume <br> Thy substance and estate as she shall hold <br> Her present mood, the purpose which the gods <br> Have planted in her breast. She to herself <br> Gains great renown, but surely brings on thee <br> Loss of much goods. And now we go not hence <br> To our affairs nor elsewhere, till she wed <br> Whichever of the Greeks may please her most.”</p>
<p class="verse">And then rejoined discreet Telemachus:⁠— <br> “Antinoüs, grievous wrong it were to send <br> Unwilling from this palace her who bore <br> And nursed me. Whether he be living yet <br> Or dead, my father is in distant lands; <br> And should I, of my own accord and will, <br> Dismiss my mother, I must make perforce <br> Icarius large amends, and that were hard. <br> And he would do me mischief, and the gods <br> Would send yet other evils on my head. <br> For then my mother, going forth, would call <br> On the grim Furies, and the general curse <br> Of all men would be on me. Think not I <br> Will ever speak that word. But if ye bear <br> A sense of injury for what is past, <br> Go from these halls; provide for other feasts, <br> Consuming what is yours, and visiting <br> Each other’s homes in turn. But if it seem <br> To you the wiser and the better way <br> To plunder one man’s goods, go on to waste <br> My substance. I shall call the eternal gods <br> To aid me, and, if Jupiter allow <br> Fit retribution for your crimes, ye die <br> Within this very palace unavenged.”</p>
<p class="verse">So spake Telemachus. The Thunderer, Jove, <br> Sent flying from a lofty mountaintop <br> Two eagles. First they floated on the wind <br> Close to each other, and with wings outspread; <br> But as they came to where the murmuring crowd <br> Was gathered just beneath their flight, they turned <br> And clapped their heavy pinions, looking down <br> With deadly omen on the heads below, <br> And with their talons tore each other’s cheeks <br> And necks, and then they darted to the right <br> Away through Ithaca among its roofs. <br> All who beheld the eagles were amazed, <br> And wondered what event was near at hand. <br> Among the rest an aged hero spake, <br> Named Halitherses, Mastor’s son. He knew <br> More truly than the others of his age, <br> To augur from the flight of birds, and read <br> The will of fate⁠—and wisely thus he spake:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Hear, men of Ithaca, what I shall say. <br> I speak of what most narrowly concerns <br> The suitors, over whom already hangs <br> Great peril, for Ulysses will not be <br> Long at a distance from his home and friends. <br> Even now he is not far, and meditates <br> Slaughter and death to all the suitor train; <br> And evil will ensue to many more <br> Of us, who dwell in sunny Ithaca. <br> Now let us think what measures may restrain <br> These men⁠—or let them of their own accord <br> Desist⁠—the soonest were for them the best. <br> For not as one untaught do I foretell <br> Events to come, but speak of what I know. <br> All things that I predicted to our chief, <br> What time the Argive troops embarked for Troy, <br> And sage Ulysses with them, are fulfilled; <br> I said that after many hardships borne, <br> And all his comrades lost, the twentieth year <br> Would bring him back, a stranger to us all⁠— <br> And all that then I spake of comes to pass.”</p>
<p class="verse">Eurymachus, the son of Polybus, <br> Answered the seer: “Go to thy house, old man, <br> And to thy boys, and prophesy to them, <br> Lest evil come upon them. I can act, <br> In matters such as these, a prophet’s part <br> Better than thou. True, there are many birds <br> That fly about in sunshine, but not all <br> Are ominous. Ulysses far away <br> Has perished; well it would have been if thou <br> Hadst perished with him; then thou wouldst not prate <br> Idly of things to come, nor wouldst thou stir <br> Telemachus to anger, in the hope <br> Of bearing to thy house some gift from him. <br> Now let me say, and be assured my words <br> Will be fulfilled: experienced as thou art, <br> If thou by treacherous speeches shalt inflame <br> A younger man than thou to violent deeds, <br> The sharper punishment shall first be his, <br> But we will lay on thee a penalty, <br> Old man, which thou shalt find it hard to bear, <br> And bitterly wilt thou repent. And now <br> Let me persuade Telemachus to send <br> His mother to her father. They will make <br> A marriage for her there, and give with her <br> A liberal dowry, such as may become <br> A favorite daughter on her wedding-day, <br> Else never will the sons of Greece renounce, <br> I think, the difficult suit. We do not fear <br> Telemachus himself, though glib of speech, <br> Nor care we for the empty oracle <br> Which thou, old man, dost utter, making thee <br> Only more hated. Still will his estate <br> Be wasted, nor will order e’er return <br> While she defers her marriage with some prince <br> Of the Achaians. We shall urge our suit <br> For that most excellent of womankind <br> As rivals, nor withdraw to seek the hand <br> Of others, whom we fitly might espouse.”</p>
<p class="verse">To this discreet Telemachus replied:⁠— <br> “Eurymachus, and ye, the illustrious train <br> Of suitors, I have nothing more to ask⁠— <br> No more to say⁠—for now the gods and all <br> The Achaians know the truth. But let me have <br> A gallant barque, and twenty men to make <br> From coast to coast a voyage, visiting <br> Sparta and sandy Pylos, to inquire <br> For my long-absent father, and the chance <br> Of his return, if any of mankind <br> Can tell me aught, or if some rumor come <br> From Jove, since thus are tidings often brought <br> To human knowledge. Should I learn that yet <br> He lives and may return, I then would wait <br> A twelvemonth, though impatient. Should I hear <br> That he no longer lives, I shall return <br> Homeward, and pile his monument on high <br> With funeral honors that become the dead, <br> And give my mother to a second spouse.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake and took his seat, and then arose <br> Mentor, once comrade of the excellent chief <br> Ulysses, who, departing with his fleet, <br> Consigned his household to the aged man, <br> That they should all obey him, and that he <br> Should safely keep his charge. He rose amid <br> The assembly, and addressed them wisely thus:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Hear and attend, ye men of Ithaca, <br> To what I say. Let never sceptred king <br> Henceforth be gracious, mild, and merciful, <br> And righteous; rather be he deaf to prayer <br> And prone to deeds of wrong, since no one now <br> Remembers the divine Ulysses more, <br> Among the people over whom he ruled <br> Benignly like a father. Yet I bear <br> No envy to the haughty suitors here, <br> Moved as they are to deeds of violence <br> By evil counsels, since, in pillaging <br> The substance of Ulysses, who they say <br> Will nevermore return, they risk their lives. <br> But I am angry with the rest, with all <br> Of you who sit here mute, nor even with words <br> Of stern reproof restrain their violence, <br> Though ye so many are and they so few.”</p>
<p class="verse">Leiocritus, Evenor’s son, rejoined:⁠— <br> “Malicious Mentor, foolish man! what talk <br> Is this of holding us in check? ’Twere hard <br> For numbers even greater than our own <br> To drive us from a feast. And should the prince <br> Of Ithaca, Ulysses, come himself, <br> Thinking to thrust the illustrious suitors forth <br> That banquet in these palace halls, his queen <br> Would have no cause for joy at his return, <br> Greatly as she desired it. He would draw <br> Sure death upon himself in strife with us <br> Who are so many. Thou hast spoken ill. <br> Now let the people who are gathered here <br> Disperse to their employments. We will leave <br> Mentor and Halitherses, who were both <br> His father’s early comrades, to provide <br> For the youth’s voyage. He will yet remain <br> A long time here, I think, to ask for news <br> In Ithaca, and never will set sail.”</p>
<p class="verse">Thus having said, he instantly dismissed <br> The people; they departed to their homes; <br> The suitors sought the palace of the prince.</p>
<p class="verse">Then to the ocean-side, apart from all, <br> Went forth Telemachus, and washed his hands <br> In the gray surf, and prayed to Pallas thus:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Hear me, thou deity who yesterday, <br> In visiting our palace, didst command <br> That I should traverse the black deep to learn <br> News of my absent father, and the chance <br> Of his return! The Greeks themselves withstand, <br> My purpose; the proud suitors most of all.”</p>
<p class="verse">Such was his prayer, and straightway Pallas stood, <br> In form and voice like Mentor, by his side, <br> And thus accosted him with winged words:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Telemachus, thou henceforth shalt not lack <br> Valor or wisdom. If with thee abides <br> Thy father’s gallant spirit, as he was <br> In deed and word, thou wilt not vainly make <br> This voyage. But if thou be not in truth <br> The son of him and of Penelope, <br> Then I rely not on thee to perform <br> What thou dost meditate. Few sons are like <br> Their fathers: most are worse, a very few <br> Excel their parents. Since thou wilt not lack <br> Valor and wisdom in the coming time, <br> Nor is thy father’s shrewdness wanting quite <br> In thee, great hope there is that happily <br> This plan will be fulfilled. Regard not then <br> The suitor train, their purposes and plots. <br> Senseless are they, as little wise as just, <br> And have no thought of the black doom of death <br> Now drawing near to sweep them in a day <br> To their destruction. But thy enterprise <br> Must suffer no delay. So much am I <br> Thy father’s friend and thine, that I will cause <br> A swift barque to be fitted out for sea, <br> And will myself attend thee. Go now hence <br> Among the suitors, and make ready there <br> The needful stores, and let them all be put <br> In vessels⁠—wine in jars, and meal, the strength <br> Of man, in close thick skins⁠—while I engage, <br> Among the people here, a willing crew. <br> Ships are there in our seagirt Ithaca <br> Full many, new and old, and I will choose <br> The best of these, and see it well equipped. <br> Then will we drag it down to the broad sea.”</p>
<p class="verse">Thus Pallas spake, the child of Jupiter. <br> Telemachus obeyed the heavenly voice, <br> And stayed not; home he hastened, where he saw <br> Sadly the arrogant suitors in the hall, <br> Busily flaying goats and roasting swine. <br> Antinoüs, laughing, came to meet the youth. <br> And fastened on his hand, and thus he spake:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Telemachus, thou youth of lofty speech <br> And boundless in abuse, let neither word <br> Nor deed that may displease thee vex thy heart, <br> But gayly eat and drink as thou wert wont. <br> The Achaians generously will provide <br> Whatever thou requirest, ship and men⁠— <br> All chosen rowers⁠—that thou mayst arrive <br> Sooner at sacred Pylos, there to learn <br> Tidings of thy illustrious father’s fate.”</p>
<p class="verse">Then spake discreet Telemachus in turn:⁠— <br> “Antinoüs, never could I sit with you, <br> Arrogant ones! in silence nor enjoy <br> The feast in quiet. Is it not enough, <br> O suitors, that while I was yet a child <br> Ye wasted on your revelries my large <br> And rich possessions? Now that I am grown, <br> And, when I hear the words of other men, <br> Discern their meaning, now that every day <br> Strengthens my spirit, I will make the attempt <br> To bring the evil fates upon your heads, <br> Whether I go to Pylos or remain <br> Among this people. I shall surely make <br> This voyage, and it will not be in vain. <br> Although I go a passenger on board <br> Another’s ship⁠—since neither ship have I <br> Nor rowers⁠—ye have judged that so were best.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake, and quickly from the suitor’s hand <br> Withdrew his own. The others who prepared <br> Their banquet in the palace scoffed at him, <br> And flung at him their bitter taunts, and one <br> Among the insolent youths reviled him thus:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Telemachus is certainly resolved <br> To butcher us. He goes to bring allies <br> From sandy Pylos or the Spartan coast, <br> He is so bent on slaughter. Or perhaps <br> He visits the rich land of Ephyrè <br> In search of deadly poisons to be thrown <br> Into a cup and end us all at once.”</p>
<p class="verse">Then said another of the haughty youths:⁠— <br> “Who knows but, wandering in his hollow barque, <br> He too may perish, far from all his friends, <br> Just as Ulysses perished? This would bring <br> Increase of labor; it would cast on us <br> The trouble to divide his goods, and give <br> His palace to his mother, and to him <br> Who takes the woman as his wedded wife.”</p>
<p class="verse">So spake they, but Telemachus went down <br> To that high-vaulted room, his father’s, where <br> Lay heaps of gold and brass, and garments store <br> In chests, and fragrant oils. And there stood casks <br> Of delicate old wine and pure, a drink <br> For gods, in rows against the wall, to wait <br> If ever, after many hardships borne, <br> Ulysses should return. Upon that room <br> Close-fitting double doors were shut, and there <br> Was one who night and day kept diligent watch, <br> A woman, Eurycleia, child of Ops, <br> Peisenor’s son. Telemachus went in <br> And called her to him, and bespake her thus:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Nurse, let sweet wine be drawn into my jars, <br> The finest next to that which thou dost keep <br> Expecting our unhappy lord, if yet <br> The nobly born Ulysses shall escape <br> The doom of death and come to us again. <br> Fill twelve, and fit the covers close, and pour <br> Meal into well-sewn skins, and let the tale <br> Be twenty measures of the flour of wheat. <br> This none but thou must know. Let all these things <br> Be brought together; then, as night shuts in, <br> When to her upper chamber, seeking rest, <br> My mother shall withdraw, I come and take <br> What thou providest for me. I am bound <br> For Sparta and for Pylos in the sands, <br> To gather news concerning the return <br> Of my dear father, if I haply may.”</p>
<p class="verse">So spake the youth, and his beloved nurse <br> Sobbed, wept aloud, and spake these winged words:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Why should there come, dear child, a thought like this <br> Into thy heart. Why wouldst thou wander forth <br> To distant regions⁠—thou an only son <br> And dearly loved? Ulysses, nobly born, <br> Has perished, from his native land afar, <br> ’Mid a strange race. These men, when thou art gone, <br> At once will lay their plots to take thy life, <br> And share thy wealth among them. Stay thou here <br> Among thy people; need is none that thou <br> Shouldst suffer, roaming o’er the barren deep.”</p>
<p class="verse">Then spake discreet Telemachus again:⁠— <br> “Be of good cheer, O nurse, for my design <br> Is not without the sanction of a god; <br> But swear thou not to let my mother know <br> Of my intent until the eleventh day <br> Or twelfth shall pass, or till, in missing me, <br> She learn of my departure, lest she weep <br> And stain with tears the beauty of her face.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake; the ancient woman solemnly <br> Swore by the gods, and when the rite was o’er <br> Drew wine into the jars, and poured the meal <br> Into the well-sewn skins. Telemachus <br> Entered the hall and joined the suitor train.</p>
<p class="verse">Then did the blue-eyed goddess turn her thoughts <br> To other plans, and taking on herself <br> The semblance of Telemachus, she ranged <br> The city, speaking to each man in turn, <br> And bidding him at nightfall to repair <br> To where the good ship lay. That gallant ship <br> She begged of the renowned Noëmon, son <br> Of Phronius, who with cheerful grace complied.</p>
<p class="verse">The sun went down, the city streets lay all <br> In shadow. Then she drew the good ship down <br> Into the sea, and brought and put on board <br> The appointments every well-built galley needs, <br> And moored her at the bottom of the port, <br> Where, in a throng, obedient to the word <br> Of Pallas, round her came her gallant crew.</p>
<p class="verse">With yet a new device the blue-eyed maid <br> Went to the palace of the godlike chief <br> Ulysses, where she poured a gentle sleep <br> Over the suitors. As they drank she made <br> Their senses wander, and their hands let fall <br> The goblets. Now no longer at the board <br> They sat, but sallied forth, and through the town <br> Went to their slumbers, for the power of sleep <br> Had fallen heavily upon their lids. <br> Then blue-eyed Pallas from those sumptuous halls <br> Summoned Telemachus. She took the form <br> And voice of Mentor, and bespake him thus:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Telemachus, already at their oars <br> Sit thy well-armed companions and await <br> Thy coming; let us go without delay.”</p>
<p class="verse">Thus having spoken, Pallas led the way <br> With rapid footsteps which he followed fast; <br> Till having reached the galley and the sea <br> They found their long-haired comrades at the beach, <br> And thus the gallant prince Telemachus <br> Bespake them: “Hither, comrades, let us bring <br> The sea-stores from the dwelling where they lie; <br> My mother knows not of it, nor her maids; <br> The secret has been told to one alone.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake, and went before them. In his steps <br> They followed. To the gallant barque they brought <br> The stores, and, as the well-beloved son <br> Of King Ulysses bade, they laid them down <br> Within the hull. Telemachus went up <br> The vessel’s side, but Pallas first embarked, <br> And at the stern sat down, while next to her <br> Telemachus was seated. Then the crew <br> Cast loose the fastenings and went all on board, <br> And took their places on the rowers’ seats, <br> While blue-eyed Pallas sent a favoring breeze, <br> A fresh wind from the west, that murmuring swept <br> The dark-blue main. Telemachus gave forth <br> The word to wield the tackle; they obeyed, <br> And raised the fir-tree mast, and, fitting it <br> Into its socket, bound it fast with cords, <br> And drew and spread with firmly twisted ropes <br> The shining sails on high. The steady wind <br> Swelled out the canvas in the midst; the ship <br> Moved on, the dark sea roaring round her keel, <br> As swiftly through the waves she cleft her way. <br> And when the rigging of that swift black ship <br> Was firmly in its place, they filled their cups <br> With wine, and to the ever-living gods <br> Poured out libations, most of all to one, <br> Jove’s blue-eyed daughter. Thus through all that night <br> And all the ensuing morn they held their way.</p>
