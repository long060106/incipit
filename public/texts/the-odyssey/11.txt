<p class="lede">Voyage to the land of the dead⁠—Interview with Tiresias, the seer⁠—The heroes and heroines whom Ulysses saw there⁠—Interview with his mother, and with Agamemnon, Achilles, and others⁠—Occupations of the dead⁠—Punishments of the guilty.</p>
<p class="verse">“Now, when we reached our galley by the shore, <br> We drew it first into the mighty deep, <br> And set the mast and sails, and led on board <br> The sheep, and sorrowfully and in tears <br> Embarked ourselves. The fair-haired and august <br> Circè, expert in music, sent with us <br> A kindly fellow-voyager⁠—a wind <br> That breathed behind the dark-prowed barque, and swelled <br> The sails; and now, with all things in their place <br> Throughout the ship, we sat us down⁠—the breeze <br> And helmsman guiding us upon our way. <br> All day our sails were stretched, as o’er the deep <br> Our vessel ran; the sun went down; the paths <br> Of the great sea were darkened, and our barque <br> Reached the far confines of Océanus.</p>
<p class="verse">“There lies the land, and there the people dwell <br> Of the Cimmerians, in eternal cloud <br> And darkness. Never does the glorious sun <br> Look on them with his rays, when he goes up <br> Into the starry sky, nor when again <br> He sinks from heaven to earth. Unwholesome night <br> O’erhangs the wretched race. We touched the land, <br> And, drawing up our galley on the beach, <br> Took from on board the sheep, and followed on <br> Beside the ocean-stream until we reached <br> The place of which the goddess Circè spake.</p>
<p class="verse">“Here Perimedes and Eurylochus <br> Held in their grasp the victims, while I drew <br> The trusty sword upon my thigh, and scooped <br> A trench in earth, a cubit long and wide, <br> Round which we stood, and poured to all the dead <br> Libations⁠—milk and honey first, and next <br> Rich wine, and lastly water, scattering <br> White meal upon them. Then I offered prayer <br> Fervently to that troop of airy forms, <br> And made a vow that I would sacrifice, <br> When I at last should come to Ithaca, <br> A heifer without blemish, barren yet, <br> In my own courts, and heap the altar-pyre <br> With things of price, and to the seer alone, <br> Tiresias, by himself, a ram whose fleece <br> Was wholly black, the best of all my flocks.</p>
<p class="verse">“When I had worshipped thus with prayer and vows <br> The nations of the dead, I took the sheep <br> And pierced their throats above the hollow trench. <br> The blood flowed dark; and thronging round me came <br> Souls of the dead from Erebus⁠—young wives <br> And maids unwedded, men worn out with years <br> And toil, and virgins of a tender age <br> In their new grief, and many a warrior slain <br> In battle, mangled by the spear, and clad <br> In bloody armor, who about the trench <br> Flitted on every side, now here, now there, <br> With gibbering cries, and I grew pale with fear. <br> Then calling to my friends, I bade them flay <br> The victims lying slaughtered by the knife, <br> And, burning them with fire, invoke the gods⁠— <br> The mighty Pluto and dread Proserpine. <br> Then from my thigh I drew the trusty sword, <br> And sat me down, and suffered none of all <br> Those airy phantoms to approach the blood <br> Until I should bespeak the Theban seer.</p>
<p class="verse">“And first the soul of my companion came, <br> Elpenor, for he was not buried yet <br> In earth’s broad bosom. We had left him dead <br> In Circè’s halls, unwept and unentombed. <br> We had another task. But when I now <br> Beheld I pitied him, and, shedding tears, <br> I said these winged words: ‘How earnest thou, <br> Elpenor, hither into these abodes <br> Of night and darkness? Thou hast made more speed, <br> Although on foot, than I in my good ship.’</p>
<p class="verse">“I spake; the phantom sobbed and answered me:⁠— <br> ‘Son of Laertes, nobly born and wise, <br> Ulysses! ’twas the evil doom decreed <br> By some divinity, and too much wine, <br> That wrought my death. I laid myself to sleep <br> In Circè’s palace, and, remembering not <br> The way to the long stairs that led below, <br> Fell from the roof, and by the fall my neck <br> Was broken at the spine; my soul went down <br> To Hades. I conjure thee now, by those <br> Whom thou hast left behind and far away, <br> Thy consort and thy father⁠—him by whom <br> Thou when a boy wert reared⁠—and by thy son <br> Telemachus, who in thy palace-halls <br> Is left alone⁠—for well I know that thou, <br> In going hence from Pluto’s realm, wilt moor <br> Thy gallant vessel in the Aeaean isle⁠— <br> That there, O king, thou wilt remember me, <br> And leave me not when thou departest thence <br> Unwept, unburied, lest I bring on thee <br> The anger of the gods. But burn me there <br> With all the armor that I wore, and pile, <br> Close to the hoary deep, a mound for me⁠— <br> A hapless man of whom posterity <br> Shall hear. Do this for me, and plant upright <br> Upon my tomb the oar with which I rowed, <br> While yet a living man, among thy friends.’</p>
<p class="verse">“He spake and I replied: ‘Unhappy youth, <br> All this I duly will perform for thee.’</p>
<p class="verse">“And then the soul of Anticleia came⁠— <br> My own dead mother, daughter of the king <br> Autolycus, large-minded. Her I left <br> Alive, what time I sailed for Troy, and now <br> I wept to see her there, and pitied her, <br> And yet forbade her, though with grief, to come <br> Near to the blood till I should first accost <br> Tiresias. He too came, the Theban seer, <br> Tiresias, bearing in his hand a wand <br> Of gold; he knew me and bespake me thus:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“ ‘Why, O unhappy mortal, hast thou left <br> The light of day to come among the dead <br> And to this joyless land? Go from the trench <br> And turn thy sword away, that I may drink <br> The blood, and speak the word of prophecy.’</p>
<p class="verse">“He spake; withdrawing from the trench, I thrust <br> Into its sheath my silver-studded sword, <br> And after drinking of the dark red blood <br> The blameless prophet turned to me and said:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“ ‘Illustrious chief Ulysses, thy desire <br> Is for a happy passage to thy home, <br> Yet will a god withstand thee. Not unmarked <br> By Neptune shalt thou, as I deem, proceed <br> Upon thy voyage. He hath laid up wrath <br> Against thee in his heart, for that thy hand <br> Deprived his son of sight. Yet may ye still <br> Return, though after many hardships borne, <br> If thou but hold thy appetite in check, <br> And that of thy companions, when thou bring <br> Thy gallant barque to the Trinacrian isle, <br> Safe from the gloomy deep. There will ye find <br> The beeves and fading wethers of the Sun⁠— <br> The all-beholding and all-hearing Sun. <br> If these ye leave unharmed, and keep in mind <br> The thought of your return, ye may go back, <br> Though sufferers, to your home in Ithaca; <br> But if thou do them harm, the event will be <br> Destruction to thy ship and to its crew; <br> And thou, if thou escape it, wilt return <br> Late to thy country, all thy comrades lost, <br> And in a foreign barque, and thou shalt find <br> Wrong in thy household⁠—arrogant men who waste <br> Thy substance, wooers of thy noble wife, <br> And offering bridal gifts. On thy return <br> Thou shalt avenge thee of their violent deeds; <br> And when thou shalt have slain them in thy halls, <br> Whether by stratagem or by the sword <br> In open fight, then take a shapely oar <br> And journey on, until thou meet with men <br> Who have not known the sea nor eaten food <br> Seasoned with salt, nor ever have beheld <br> Galleys with crimson prows, nor shapely oars, <br> Which are the wings of ships. I will declare <br> A sign by which to know them, nor canst thou <br> Mistake it. When a traveller, meeting thee, <br> Shalt say that thou dost bear a winnowing-fan <br> Upon thy sturdy shoulder, stop and plant <br> Thy shapely oar upright in earth, and there <br> Pay to King Neptune solemn sacrifice⁠— <br> A ram, a bull, and from his herd of swine <br> A boar. And then returning to thy home, <br> See that thou offer hallowed hecatombs <br> To all the ever-living ones who dwell <br> In the broad heaven, to each in order due. <br> So at the last thy death shall come to thee <br> Far from the sea, and gently take thee off <br> In a serene old age that ends among <br> A happy people. I have told thee true.’</p>
<p class="verse">“He spake, and thus I answered him: ‘The gods, <br> Tiresias, have decreed as thou hast said. <br> But tell, and tell me truly⁠—I behold <br> The soul of my dead mother; there she sits <br> In silence by the blood, and will not deign <br> To look upon her son nor speak to him. <br> Instruct me, mighty prophet, by what means <br> To make my mother know me for her son.’</p>
<p class="verse">“I spake, and instantly the seer replied:⁠— <br> ‘Easily that is told; I give it thee <br> To bear in mind. Whoever of the dead <br> Thou sufferest to approach and drink the blood <br> Will speak the truth; those whom thou dost forbid <br> To taste the blood will silently withdraw.’</p>
<p class="verse">“The soul of King Tiresias, saying this, <br> Passed to the abode of Pluto; he had given <br> The oracle I asked. I waited still <br> Until my mother, drawing near again, <br> Drank the dark blood; she knew me suddenly, <br> And said in piteous tones these winged words:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“ ‘How didst thou come, my child, a living man, <br> Into this place of darkness? Difficult <br> It is for those who breathe the breath of life <br> To visit these abodes, through which are rolled <br> Great rivers, fearful floods⁠—the first of these <br> Océanus, whose waters none can cross <br> On foot, or save on board a trusty barque. <br> Hast thou come hither on thy way from Troy, <br> A weary wanderer with thy ship and friends? <br> And hast thou not been yet at Ithaca, <br> Nor in thine island palace seen thy wife?’</p>
<p class="verse">“She spake, I answered: ‘ ’Tis necessity, <br> Dear mother, that has brought me to the abode <br> Of Pluto, to consult the Theban seer, <br> Tiresias. Not to the Achaian coast <br> Have I returned, nor reached our country, yet <br> Continually I wander; everywhere <br> I meet misfortune⁠—even from the time <br> When, in the noble Agamemnon’s train, <br> I came to Ilium, famed for steeds, and made <br> War on its dwellers. Tell me now, I pray, <br> And truly, how it was that fate on thee <br> Brought the long sleep of death? by slow disease? <br> Or, stealing on thee, did the archer-queen, <br> Diana, slay thee with her silent shafts? <br> And tell me of my father, and the son <br> Left in my palace. Rests the sway I bore <br> On them, or has another taken it, <br> Since men believe I shall return no more? <br> And tell me of my wedded wife, her thoughts <br> And purposes, and whether she remains <br> Yet with my son. Is she the guardian still <br> Of my estates, or has the noblest chief <br> Of those Achaians led her thence a bride?’</p>
<p class="verse">“I spake; my reverend mother answered thus:⁠— <br> ‘Most certain is it that she sadly dwells <br> Still in thy palace. Weary days and nights <br> And tears are hers. No man has taken yet <br> Thy place as ruler, but Telemachus <br> Still has the charge of thy domain, and gives <br> The liberal feasts which it befits a prince <br> To give, for all invite him. In the fields <br> Thy father dwells, and never in the town <br> Is seen; nor beds nor cloaks has he, nor mats <br> Of rich device, but, all the winter through, <br> He sleeps where sleep the laborers, on the hearth, <br> Amid the dust, and wears a wretched garb; <br> And when the summer comes, or autumn days <br> Ripen the fruit, his bed is on the ground, <br> And made of leaves, that everywhere are shed <br> In the rich vineyards. There he lies and grieves, <br> And, cherishing his sorrow, mourns thy fate, <br> And keenly feels the miseries of age. <br> And thus I underwent my fate and died; <br> For not the goddess of the unerring bow <br> Stealing upon me smote me in thy halls <br> With silent arrows, nor did slow disease <br> Come o’er me, such as, wasting cruelly <br> The members, takes at last the life away; <br> But constant longing for thee, anxious thoughts <br> Of thee, and memory of thy gentleness, <br> Ulysses, made an end of my sweet life.’</p>
<p class="verse">“She spake; I longed to take into my arms <br> The soul of my dead mother. Thrice I tried, <br> Moved by a strong desire, and thrice the form <br> Passed through them like a shadow or a dream. <br> And then did the great sorrow in my heart <br> Grow sharper, and in winged words I said:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“ ‘Beloved mother, why wilt thou not keep <br> Thy place, that I may clasp thee, so that here, <br> In Pluto’s realm and in each other’s arms, <br> We each might in the other soothe the sense <br> Of misery? Hath mighty Proserpine <br> Sent but an empty shade to meet me here, <br> That I might only grieve and sigh the more?’</p>
<p class="verse">“I spake, and then my reverend mother said:⁠— <br> ‘Believe not that Jove’s daughter Proserpine <br> Deceives thee. ’Tis the lot of all our race <br> When they are dead. No more the sinews bind <br> The bones and flesh, when once from the white bones <br> The life departs. Then like a dream the soul <br> Flies off, and flits about from place to place. <br> But haste thou to the light again, and mark <br> What I have said, that thou in after days <br> Mayst tell it to thy wife on thy return.’</p>
<p class="verse">“Thus we conferred. Meantime the women came <br> Around me, moved by mighty Proserpine; <br> In throngs they gathered to the dark red blood. <br> Then, as I pondered how to question each, <br> This seemed the wisest⁠—from my sturdy thigh <br> I plucked the trenchant sword, and suffered not <br> All that were there to taste the blood at once; <br> So one by one they came, and each in turn <br> Declared her lineage. Thus I questioned all.</p>
<p class="verse">“Then saw I highborn Tyro first, who claimed <br> To be the daughter of that blameless man <br> Salmoneus, and who called herself the wife <br> Of Cretheus, son of Aeolus. She loved <br> Enipeus, hallowed river, fairest stream <br> Of all that flow on earth, and often walked <br> Beside its pleasant waters. He whose arms <br> Surround the islands, Neptune, once put on <br> The river’s form, and at its gulfy mouth <br> Met her; the purple waters stood upright <br> Around them like a wall, and formed an arch, <br> And hid the god and woman. There he loosed <br> The virgin zone of Tyro, shedding sleep <br> Upon her. Afterward he took her hand <br> And said: ‘Rejoice, O maiden, in our love, <br> For with the year’s return shalt thou bring forth <br> Illustrious sons; the embraces of the gods <br> Are not unfruitful. Rear them carefully. <br> And now return to thy abode, and watch <br> Thy words, and keep thy secret. Thou must know <br> That I am Neptune, he who shakes the earth.’</p>
<p class="verse">“He spake, and plunged into the billowy deep. <br> And she became a mother, and brought forth <br> Pelias and Neleus, valiant ministers <br> Of mighty Jupiter. On the broad lands <br> Of Iäolchos Pelias dwelt, and reared <br> Vast flocks of sheep, while Neleus made his home <br> In Pylos midst the sands. The queenly dame, <br> His mother, meanwhile brought forth other sons <br> To Cretheus⁠—Aeson first, and Pheres next, <br> And Amythaon, great in horsemanship.</p>
<p class="verse">“And after her I saw Antiopè, <br> The daughter of Asopus⁠—her who made <br> A boast that she had slumbered in the arms <br> Of Jove. Two sons she bore⁠—Amphion one, <br> The other Zethus⁠—and they founded Thebes <br> With its seven gates, and girt it round with towers; <br> For, valiant as they were, they could not dwell <br> Safely in that great town unfenced by towers.</p>
<p class="verse">“And after her I saw Amphitryon’s wife, <br> Alcmena, her who brought forth Hercules, <br> The dauntless hero of the lion-heart⁠— <br> For she had given herself into the arms <br> Of mighty Jupiter. I also saw <br> Megara there, a daughter of the house <br> Of haughty Creion. Her Amphitryon’s son, <br> Unamable in strength, had made his wife.</p>
<p class="verse">“The mother, too, of Oedipus I saw, <br> Beautiful Epicastè, who in life <br> Had done unwittingly a heinous deed⁠— <br> Had married her own son, who, having slain <br> Her father first, espoused her; but the gods <br> Published abroad the rumor of the crime. <br> He in the pleasant town of Thebes bore sway <br> O’er the Cadmeians; yet in misery <br> He lived, for so the offended gods ordained. <br> And she went down to Hades and the gates <br> That stand forever barred; for, wild with grief, <br> She slung a cord upon a lofty beam <br> And perished by it, leaving him to bear <br> Woes without measure, such as on a son <br> The furies of a mother might inflict.</p>
<p class="verse">“And there I saw the dame supremely fair, <br> Chloris, whom Neleus with large marriage-gifts <br> Wooed, and brought home a bride; the youngest she <br> Among the daughters of Iäsus’ son, <br> Amphion, ruler o’er Orchomenus, <br> The Minyeian town, and o’er the realm <br> Of Pylos. Three illustrious sons she bore <br> To Neleus⁠—Nestor, Chromius, and a chief <br> Of lofty bearing, Periclymenus. <br> She brought forth Pero also, marvellous <br> In beauty, wooed by all the region round; <br> but Neleus would bestow the maid on none <br> Save him who should drive off from Phylacè <br> The beeves, broad-fronted and with crooked horns, <br> Of valiant Iphicles⁠—a difficult task. <br> One man alone, a blameless prophet, dared <br> Attempt it; but he found himself withstood <br> By fate, and rigid fetters, and a force <br> Of rustic herdsmen. Months and days went by, <br> And the full year, led by the hours, came round. <br> The valiant Iphicles, who from the seer <br> Had heard the oracles explained, took off <br> The shackles, and the will of Jove was done.</p>
<p class="verse">“Then saw I Leda, wife of Tyndarus, <br> Who bore to Tyndarus two noble sons, <br> Castor the horseman, Pollux skilled to wield <br> The cestus. Both of them have still a place <br> Upon the fruitful earth; for Jupiter <br> Gave them such honor that they live by turns <br> Each one a day, and then are with the dead <br> Each one by turns; they rank among the gods.</p>
<p class="verse">“The wife of Aloëus next appeared, <br> Iphidameia, who, as she declared, <br> Had won the love of Neptune. She brought forth <br> Two short-lived sons⁠—one like a god in form, <br> Named Otus; and the other, far renowned, <br> Named Ephialtes. These the bounteous earth <br> Nourished to be the tallest of mankind, <br> And goodliest, save Orion. When the twain <br> Had seen but nine years of their life, they stood <br> In breadth of frame nine cubits, and in height <br> Nine fathoms. They against the living gods <br> Threatened to wage, upon the Olympian height, <br> Fierce and tumultuous battle, and to fling <br> Ossa upon Olympus, and to pile <br> Pelion, with all its growth of leafy woods, <br> On Ossa, that the heavens might thus be scaled. <br> And they, if they had reached their prime of youth, <br> Had made their menace good. The son of Jove <br> And amber-haired Latona took their lives <br> Ere yet beneath their temples sprang the down <br> And covered with its sprouting tufts the chin.</p>
<p class="verse">“Phaedra I saw, and Procris, and the child <br> Of the wise Minos, Ariadne, famed <br> For beauty, whom the hero Theseus once <br> From Crete to hallowed Athens’ fertile coast <br> Led, but possessed her not. Diana gave <br> Ear to the tale which Bacchus brought to her, <br> And in the isle of Dia slew the maid.</p>
<p class="verse">“And Maera I beheld, and Clymenè, <br> And Eriphylè, hateful in her guilt, <br> Who sold her husband for a price in gold. <br> But vainly might I think to name them all⁠— <br> The wives and daughters of heroic men <br> Whom I beheld⁠—for first the ambrosial night <br> Would wear away. And now for me the hour <br> Of sleep is come, at my good ship among <br> My friends, or haply here. Meantime the care <br> For my return is with the gods and you.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake, and all were silent: all within <br> The shadows of those palace-halls were held <br> Motionless by the charm of what he said. <br> And thus the white-armed Queen Aretè spake:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Phaeacians, how appears this man to you <br> In form, in stature, and well-judging mind? <br> My guest he is, but each among you shares <br> The honor of the occasion. Now, I pray, <br> Dismiss him not in haste, nor sparingly <br> Bestow your gifts on one in so much need; <br> For in your dwellings is much wealth, bestowed <br> Upon you by the bounty of the gods.”</p>
<p class="verse">Then also Echeneüs, aged chief, <br> The oldest man of the Phaeacians, spake:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“My friends, the word of our sagacious queen <br> Errs not, nor is ill-timed, and yours it is <br> To hearken and obey: but all depends <br> Upon Alcinoüs⁠—both the word and deed.”</p>
<p class="verse">And then in turn Alcinoüs spake: “That word <br> Shall be fulfilled, if I am ruler here <br> O’er the Phaeacians, skilled in seamanship. <br> But let the stranger, though he long for home, <br> Bear to remain till morning, that his store <br> Of gifts may be complete. To send him home <br> Shall be the charge of all, but mostly mine, <br> Since mine it is to hold the sovereign power.”</p>
<p class="verse">And then the wise Ulysses said: “O King <br> Alcinoüs, eminent o’er all thy race! <br> Shouldst thou command me to remain with thee <br> Even for a twelvemonth, and at length provide <br> For my return, and give me princely gifts, <br> Even that would please me; for with fuller hands, <br> The happier were my lot on my return <br> To my own land. I should be honored then, <br> And meet a kinder welcome there from all <br> Who see me in my Ithaca once more.”</p>
<p class="verse">And then again in turn Alcinoüs spake:⁠— <br> “Ulysses, when we look on thee, we feel <br> No fear that thou art false, or one of those, <br> The many, whom the dark earth nourishes, <br> Wandering at large, and forging lies, that we <br> May not suspect them. Thou hast grace of speech <br> And noble thoughts, and fitly hast thou told, <br> Even as a minstrel might, the history <br> Of all thy Argive brethren and thy own. <br> Now say, and frankly, didst thou also see <br> Any of those heroic men who went <br> With thee to Troy, and in that region met <br> Their fate? A night immeasurably long <br> Is yet before us. Let us have thy tale <br> Of wonders. I could listen till the break <br> Of hallowed morning, if thou canst endure <br> So long to speak of hardships thou hast borne.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake, and wise Ulysses answered thus:⁠— <br> “O King Alcinoüs, eminent beyond <br> All others of thy people. For discourse <br> There is a time; there is a time for sleep. <br> If more thou yet wouldst hear, I will not spare <br> To give the story of the greater woes <br> Of my companions, who were afterward <br> Cut off from life; and though they had escaped <br> The cruel Trojan war, on their return <br> They perished by a woman’s fraud and guilt.</p>
<p class="verse">“When chaste Proserpina had made the ghosts <br> Of women scatter right and left, there came <br> The soul of Agamemnon, Atreus’ son. <br> He came attended by a throng of those <br> Who in the palace of Aegisthus met <br> A fate like his and died. When he had drunk <br> The dark red blood, he knew me at a look, <br> And wailed aloud, and, bursting into tears, <br> Stretched out his hands to touch me; but no power <br> Was there of grasp or pressure, such as once <br> Dwelt in those active limbs. I could not help <br> But weep at sight of him, for from my heart <br> I pitied him, and spake these winged words:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“ ‘Most glorious son of Atreus, king of men! <br> How, Agamemnon, has the fate that brings <br> To man the everlasting sleep of death <br> O’ertaken thee? Did Neptune, calling up <br> The winds in all their fury, make thy fleet <br> A wreck, or did thine enemies on land <br> Smite thee, as thou wert driving off their beeves <br> And their fair flocks, or fighting to defend <br> Some city, and the helpless women there?’</p>
<p class="verse">“I spake, and Agamemnon thus replied:⁠— <br> ‘Son of Laertes, nobly born and wise, <br> ’Twas not that Neptune calling up the winds <br> In all their fury wrecked me in my fleet, <br> Nor hostile warriors smote me on the land, <br> But that Aegisthus, bent upon my death, <br> Plotted against me with my guilty wife, <br> And bade me to his house and slew me there, <br> Even at the banquet, as a hind might slay <br> A bullock at the stall. With me they slew <br> My comrades, as a herd of white-toothed swine <br> Are slaughtered for some man of large estates, <br> Who makes a wedding or a solemn feast. <br> Thou hast seen many perish by the sword <br> In the hard battle, one by one, and yet <br> Thou wouldst have pitied us, hadst thou beheld <br> The slain beside the wine-jar, and beneath <br> The loaded tables, while the pavement swam <br> With blood. I heard Cassandra’s piteous cry, <br> The cry of Priam’s daughter, stricken down <br> By treacherous Clytemnestra at my side. <br> And there I lay, and, dying, raised my hands <br> To grasp my sword. The shameless woman went <br> Her way, nor stayed to close my eyes, nor press <br> My mouth into its place, although my soul <br> Was on its way to Hades. There is naught <br> That lives more horrible, more lost to shame, <br> Than is the woman who has brought her mind <br> To compass deeds like these⁠—the wretch who plans <br> So foul a crime⁠—the murder of the man <br> Whom she a virgin wedded. I had looked <br> For a warm welcome from my children here, <br> And all my household in my ancient home. <br> This woman, deep in wickedness, hath brought <br> Disgrace upon herself and all her sex, <br> Even those who give their thoughts to doing good.’</p>
<p class="verse">“He spake, and I replied: ‘O, how the God <br> Who wields the thunder, Jupiter, must hate <br> The house of Atreus for the women’s sake! <br> At first we fell by myriads in the cause <br> Of Helen; Clytemnestra now hath planned <br> This guile against thee while thou wert afar.’</p>
<p class="verse">“I spake, and instantly his answer came:⁠— <br> ‘Therefore be not compliant to thy wife, <br> Nor let her hear from thee whatever lies <br> Within thy knowledge. Tell her but a part, <br> And keep the rest concealed. Yet is thy life, <br> Ulysses, in no danger from thy spouse; <br> For wise and well instructed in the rules <br> Of virtuous conduct is Penelope, <br> The daughter of Icarius. When we went <br> To war, we left her a young bride; a babe <br> Was at her breast, a boy, who now must sit <br> Among grown men; and fortunate is he, <br> For certainly his father will behold <br> The youth on his return, and he embrace <br> His father, as is meet. But as for me, <br> My consort suffered not my eyes to feed <br> Upon the sight of my own son; for first <br> She slew me. This, then, I admonish thee⁠— <br> Heed thou my words. Bring not thy ship to land <br> Openly in thy country, but by stealth, <br> Since now no longer can we put our trust <br> In woman. Meantime, tell me of my son, <br> And faithfully, if thou hast heard of him <br> As living, whether in Orchomenus, <br> Or sandy Pylos, or in the broad realm <br> Of Menelaus, Sparta; for not yet <br> Has my Orestes passed from earth and life.’</p>
<p class="verse">“He spake, and I replied: ‘Why ask of me <br> That question, O Atrides? I know not <br> Whether thy son be living or be dead, <br> And this is not a time for idle words.’</p>
<p class="verse">“Thus in sad talk we stood, and freely flowed <br> Our tears. Meanwhile the ghosts of Peleus’ son <br> Achilles, and Patroclus, excellent <br> Antilochus, and Ajax, all drew near⁠— <br> Ajax for form and stature eminent <br> O’er all the Greeks save Peleus’ faultless son. <br> Then did the soul of fleet Aeacides <br> Know me, and thus in winged words he said:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“ ‘Ulysses! what hath moved thee to attempt <br> This greatest of thy labors? How is it <br> That thou hast found the courage to descend <br> To Hades, where the dead, the bodiless forms <br> Of those whose work is done on earth, abide?’</p>
<p class="verse">“He spake; I answered: ‘Greatest of the Greeks! <br> Achilles, son of Peleus! ’Twas to hear <br> The counsel of Tiresias that I came, <br> If haply he might tell me by what means <br> To reach my rugged Ithaca again; <br> For yet have I not trod my native coast, <br> Nor even have drawn nigh to Greece. I meet <br> Misfortunes everywhere. But as for thee, <br> Achilles, no man lived before thy time, <br> Nor will hereafter live, more fortunate <br> Than thou⁠—for while alive we honored thee <br> As if thou wert a god, and now again <br> In these abodes thou rulest o’er the dead; <br> Therefore, Achilles, shouldst thou not be sad.’</p>
<p class="verse">“I spake; Achilles quickly answered me:⁠— <br> ‘Noble Ulysses, speak not thus of death, <br> As if thou couldst console me. I would be <br> A laborer on earth, and serve for hire <br> Some man of mean estate, who makes scant cheer, <br> Rather than reign o’er all who have gone down <br> To death. Speak rather of my noble son, <br> Whether or not he yet has joined the wars <br> To fight among the foremost of the host. <br> And tell me also if thou aught hast heard <br> Of blameless Peleus⁠—whether he be yet <br> Honored among his many Myrmidons, <br> Or do they hold him now in small esteem <br> In Hellas and in Phthia, since old age <br> Unnerves his hands and feet, and I no more <br> Am there, beneath the sun, to give him aid, <br> Strong as I was on the wide plain of Troy, <br> When warring for the Achaian cause I smote <br> That valiant people. Could I come again, <br> But for a moment, with my former strength, <br> Into my father’s palace, I would make <br> That strength and these unconquerable hands <br> A terror to the men who do him wrong, <br> And rob him of the honor due a king.’</p>
<p class="verse">“He spake; I answered: ‘Nothing have I heard <br> Of blameless Peleus, but I will relate <br> The truth concerning Neoptolemus, <br> Thy son, as thou requirest. Him I took <br> From Scyros in a gallant barque to join <br> The well-armed Greeks. Know, then, that when we sat <br> In council, planning to conduct the war <br> Against the city of Troy, he always rose <br> The first to speak, nor were his words unwise. <br> The godlike Nestor and myself alone <br> Rivalled him in debate. And when we fought <br> About the city walls, he loitered not <br> Among the others in the numerous host, <br> But hastened on before them, giving place <br> To no man there in valor. Many men <br> He slew in desperate combat, whom to name <br> Were past my power, so many were they all <br> Whom in the cause of Greece he struck to earth. <br> Yet one I name, Eurypylus, the son <br> Of Telephus, who perished by his sword <br> With many of his band, Citeians, led <br> To war because of liberal gifts bestowed <br> Upon their chieftain’s wife; the noblest he <br> Of men, in form, whom I have ever seen, <br> Save Memnon. When into the wooden steed, <br> Framed by Epeius, we the chiefs of Greece <br> Ascended, and to me was given the charge <br> Of all things there, to open and to shut <br> The close-built fraud, while others of high rank <br> Among the Greeks were wiping off their tears, <br> And their limbs shook, I never saw thy son <br> Turn pale in his fine face, or brush away <br> A tear, but he besought me earnestly <br> That he might leave our hiding-place, and grasped <br> His falchion’s hilt, and lifted up his spear <br> Heavy with brass, for in his mind he smote <br> The Trojan crowd already. When at last <br> We had o’erthrown and sacked the lofty town <br> Of Priam, he embarked upon a ship, <br> With all his share of spoil⁠—a large reward⁠— <br> Unhurt, not touched in combat hand to hand, <br> Nor wounded from afar, as oftentimes <br> Must be the fortune of a fight, for Mars <br> Is wont to rage without regard to men.’</p>
<p class="verse">“I spake. The soul of swift Aeacides <br> Over the meadows thick with asphodel <br> Departed with long strides, well pleased to hear <br> From me the story of his son’s renown.</p>
<p class="verse">“The other ghosts of those who lay in death <br> Stood sorrowing by, and each one told his griefs; <br> But that of Ajax, son of Telamon, <br> Kept far aloof, displeased that I had won <br> The victory contending at the fleet <br> Which should possess the arms of Peleus’ son. <br> His goddess-mother laid them as a prize <br> Before us, and the captive sons of Troy <br> And Pallas were the umpires to award <br> The victory. And now how much I wish <br> I had not conquered in a strife like that, <br> Since for that cause the dark earth hath received <br> The hero Ajax, who in nobleness <br> Of form and greatness of exploits excelled <br> All other Greeks, except the blameless son <br> Of Peleus. Then I spake in soothing words:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“ ‘O Ajax, son of blameless Telamon! <br> Wilt thou not even in death forget the wrath <br> Caused by the strife for those accursed arms? <br> The gods have made them fatal to the Greeks, <br> For thou, the bulwark of our host, didst fall, <br> And we lamented thee as bitterly <br> When thou wert dead as we had mourned the son <br> Of Peleus. Nor was any man to blame; <br> ’Twas Jupiter who held in vehement hate <br> The army of the warlike Greeks, and laid <br> This doom upon thee. Now, O king, draw near, <br> And hear our voice and words, and check, I pray, <br> The anger rising in thy generous breast.’</p>
<p class="verse">“I spake; he answered not, but moved away <br> To Erebus, among the other souls <br> Of the departed. Yet would I have had <br> Speech of him, angry as he was, or else <br> Have spoken to him further, but my wish <br> Was strong to see yet others of the dead.</p>
<p class="verse">“Then I beheld the illustrious son of Jove, <br> Minos, a golden sceptre in his hand, <br> Sitting to judge the dead, who round the king <br> Pleaded their causes. There they stood or sat <br> In Pluto’s halls⁠—a pile with ample gates.</p>
<p class="verse">“And next I saw the huge Orion drive, <br> Across the meadows green with asphodel, <br> The savage beast whom he had slain; he bore <br> The brazen mace, which no man’s power could break.</p>
<p class="verse">“And Tityus there I saw⁠—the mighty earth <br> His mother⁠—overspreading, as he lay, <br> Nine acres, with two vultures at his side, <br> That, plucking at his liver, plunged their beaks <br> Into the flesh; nor did his hands avail <br> To drive them off, for he had offered force <br> To Jove’s proud wife Latona, as she went <br> To Pytho, through the pleasant Panopeus.</p>
<p class="verse">“And next I looked on Tantalus, a prey <br> To grievous torments, standing in a lake <br> That reached his chin. Though painfully athirst, <br> He could not drink; as often as he bowed <br> His aged head to take into his lips <br> The water, it was drawn away, and sank <br> Into the earth, and the dark soil appeared <br> Around his feet; a god had dried it up. <br> And lofty trees drooped o’er him, hung with fruit⁠— <br> Pears and pomegranates, apples fair to sight, <br> And luscious figs, and olives green of hue. <br> And when that ancient man put forth his hands <br> To pluck them from their stems, the wind arose <br> And whirled them far among the shadowy clouds.</p>
<p class="verse">“There I beheld the shade of Sisyphus <br> Amid his sufferings. With both hands he rolled <br> A huge stone up a hill. To force it up, <br> He leaned against the mass with hands and feet; <br> But, ere it crossed the summit of the hill <br> A power was felt that sent it rolling back, <br> And downward plunged the unmanageable rock <br> Before him to the plain. Again he toiled <br> To heave it upward, while the sweat in streams <br> Ran down his limbs, and dust begrimed his brow.</p>
<p class="verse">“Then I beheld the mighty Hercules⁠— <br> The hero’s image⁠—for he sits himself <br> Among the deathless gods, well pleased to share <br> Their feasts, and Hebe of the dainty feet⁠— <br> A daughter of the mighty Jupiter <br> And golden-sandalled Juno⁠—is his wife. <br> Around his image flitted to and fro <br> The ghosts with noise, like fear-bewildered birds. <br> His look was dark as night. He held in hand <br> A naked bow, a shaft upon the string, <br> And fiercely gazed, like one about to send <br> The arrow forth. Upon his breast he wore <br> The formidable baldric, on whose band <br> Of gold were sculptured marvels⁠—forms of bears, <br> Wild boars, grim lions, battles, skirmishings, <br> And death by wounds, and slaughter. He who wrought <br> That band had never done the like before, <br> Nor could thereafter. As I met his eye, <br> The hero knew me, and, beholding me <br> With pity, said to me in winged words:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“ ‘Son of Laertes, nobly born and wise, <br> And yet unhappy; surely thou dost bear <br> A cruel fate, like that which I endured <br> While yet I saw the brightness of the sun. <br> The offspring of Saturnian Jupiter <br> Am I, and yet was I compelled to serve <br> One of a meaner race than I, who set <br> Difficult tasks. He sent me hither once <br> To bring away the guardian hound; he deemed <br> No harder task might be. I brought him hence, <br> I led him up from Hades, with such aid <br> As Hermes and the blue-eyed Pallas gave.’</p>
<p class="verse">“Thus having spoken, he withdrew again <br> Into the abode of Pluto. I remained <br> And kept my place, in hope there yet might come <br> Heroes who perished in the early time, <br> And haply I might look on some of those⁠— <br> The ancients, whom I greatly longed to see⁠— <br> On Theseus and Pirithoüs, glorious men, <br> The children of the gods. But now there flocked <br> Already round me, with a mighty noise, <br> The innumerable nations of the dead; <br> And I grew pale with fear, lest from the halls <br> Of Pluto the stern Proserpine should send <br> The frightful visage of the monster-maid, <br> The Gorgon. Hastening to my ship, I bade <br> The crew embark, and cast the hawsers loose. <br> Quickly they went on board, and took their seats <br> Upon the benches. Through Océanus <br> The current bore my galley, aided first <br> By oars and then by favorable gales.”</p>
