<p class="lede">Descent of Penelope to the hall⁠—Her doubts of the identity of Ulysses removed by evident tokens given by Ulysses⁠—Her transport at their removal⁠—His narrative of his adventures⁠—Departure of Ulysses with his son, the herdsman, and the swineherd, to the country.</p>
<p class="verse">Up to the royal bower the matron went <br> With an exulting heart, to tell the queen <br> That her beloved husband was within. <br> With knees that faltered not, and quick light step <br> She went, and, standing by her mistress, said:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Awake, Penelope, dear child, and see <br> With thine own eyes what thou hast pined for long. <br> Ulysses has returned; thy lord is here, <br> Though late, and he has slain the arrogant crew <br> Of suitors, who disgraced his house, and made <br> His wealth a spoil, and dared insult his son.”</p>
<p class="verse">And thus discreet Penelope replied: <br> “The gods, dear nurse, have made thee mad; for they <br> Have power to change the wisest men to fools, <br> And make the foolish wise, and they have warped <br> Thy mind once sound. How canst thou mock me thus, <br> Amidst my sorrows, with such idle tales? <br> Why wake me from the pleasant sleep that closed <br> My lids so softly? Never have I slept <br> So sweetly since Ulysses went from me <br> To that bad city, which no tongue should name. <br> Go, then; return into the lower rooms. <br> Had any of my women save thyself <br> Brought such a message to disturb my sleep, <br> I would have sent her back into the hall <br> With angry words; thy years are thy excuse.”</p>
<p class="verse">But Eurycleia, the dear nurse, rejoined: <br> “Nay, my dear child, I mock thee not. Most true <br> It is that thy Ulysses has returned, <br> And here he is at home, as I have said. <br> The stranger whom they scoffed at in the hall <br> Is he; and long Telemachus has known <br> That he was here, but wisely kept from all <br> His father’s secret, till he should avenge <br> Upon those violent men their guilty deeds.”</p>
<p class="verse">She ended, and her mistress, overjoyed, <br> Sprang from her couch, embraced the aged dame, <br> And wept, and said to her in winged words:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Tell me, dear nurse, and truly, if indeed <br> Ulysses have returned as thou hast said. <br> How smote he those proud suitors?⁠—he alone, <br> And they so many, gathered in the hall.”</p>
<p class="verse">And thus the well-beloved nurse replied: <br> “I saw it not, nor knew of it. I heard <br> Only the moanings of the slain, while we <br> The maids, affrighted, sat in a recess <br> Of that well-vaulted chamber; the firm doors <br> Closed us all in, until at length thy son, <br> Sent by his father, called me forth. I found <br> Ulysses standing midst the dead that lay <br> Heaped on each other, everywhere along <br> The solid pavement. Thou wouldst have rejoiced <br> To see him like a lion with the stains <br> Of slaughter on him. Now the suitors lie <br> Before the portals of the palace-court, <br> And he has kindled a great fire, and steeps <br> In smoke the noble hall. He bade me come <br> To call thee. Follow me, that ye may give <br> Your hearts to gladness⁠—for ye have endured <br> Great sorrows both, and your long-cherished hope <br> Is now fulfilled. He hath returned alive <br> To his dear home, and finds thee and his son <br> Yet in his palace, and hath terribly <br> Avenged himself upon the guilty men <br> Who under his own roof have done him wrong.”</p>
<p class="verse">Then spake the sage Penelope again: <br> “Beloved nurse, exult not overmuch, <br> Nor rashly boast. Well is it known to thee, <br> Were he to come beneath this roof again, <br> How welcome he would be to all, but most <br> To me and to the son to whom we gave <br> His being. Yet thy tidings are not true. <br> Someone of the immortals must have slain <br> The arrogant suitors, angry to behold <br> Their foul injustice and their many crimes; <br> For no respect had they to mortal man, <br> Good he might be, or bad, whome’er they met; <br> And therefore have they made an evil end. <br> But my Ulysses must have perished far <br> From Ithaca, cut off from his return.”</p>
<p class="verse">Then Eurycleia, the dear nurse, rejoined: <br> “What words are these, my child, that pass thy lips? <br> Sayst thou, then, that thy husband, who now stands <br> Upon thy hearthstone, never will return? <br> O slow of faith! but thou wert ever thus. <br> Come, then, I give a certain proof. I saw <br> Myself, when he was at the bath, the scar <br> Left on him by the white tusk of a boar, <br> And would have told thee, but he laid his hands <br> Upon my mouth, and would not suffer me <br> To bear the tidings, such his forecast was. <br> Now follow me; I give my life in pledge. <br> If I deceive thee, slay me ruthlessly.”</p>
<p class="verse">Then spake discreet Penelope again: <br> “Dear nurse, though thou in many things art wise, <br> Think not to scan the counsels of the gods, <br> Who live forever. Yet will we descend, <br> And meet my son, and look upon the slain, <br> And see the avenger by whose hand they fell.”</p>
<p class="verse">She spake, and from the royal bower went down, <br> Uncertain whether she should stand aloof <br> And question there her lord, or haste to him <br> And clasp his hands in hers and kiss his brow. <br> But having passed the threshold of hewn stone, <br> Entering she took her seat right opposite <br> Ulysses, in the full glow of the fire, <br> Against the other wall. Ulysses sat <br> Beside a lofty column with his eyes <br> Cast down, and waiting for his highborn wife <br> To speak when she had seen him. Long she sat <br> In silence, for amazement overpowered <br> Her senses. Sometimes, looking in his eyes, <br> She saw her husband there, and then again, <br> Clad in those sordid weeds, she knew him not. <br> Then spake Telemachus, and chid her thus:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Mother, unfeeling mother! hard of heart <br> Art thou; how else couldst thou remain aloof? <br> How keep from taking, at my father’s side, <br> Thy place, to talk with him, and question him? <br> No other wife could bring herself to bear <br> Such distance from a husband, just returned <br> After long hardships, in the twentieth year <br> Of absence, to his native land and her. <br> Mother! thy heart is harder than a stone.”</p>
<p class="verse">And thus the sage Penelope replied: <br> “Dear child, my faculties are overpowered <br> With wonder, and I cannot question him, <br> Nor even speak to him, nor fix my looks <br> Upon his face. But if it be indeed <br> Ulysses, and he have returned, we soon <br> Shall know each other; there are tokens known <br> To both of us, to none but him and me.”</p>
<p class="verse">She ended, and the much-enduring chief <br> Ulysses, smiling at her words, bespake <br> Telemachus at once, in winged words:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Suffer thy mother, O Telemachus, <br> To prove me; she will know me better soon. <br> My looks are sordid, and my limbs are wrapped <br> In tattered raiment, therefore does she think <br> Meanly of me, and cannot willingly <br> Believe that I am he. But let us now <br> Consider what most wisely may be done. <br> He who hath slain, among a tribe of men, <br> A single one with few to avenge his death, <br> Flees from his kindred and his native land; <br> But we have slain the champions of the realm, <br> The flower of all the youth of Ithaca. <br> Therefore, I pray thee, think what shall be done.”</p>
<p class="verse">And then discreet Telemachus replied: <br> “Look thou to that, dear father; for they say <br> That thou of all mankind wert wont to give <br> The wisest counsels. None of mortal birth <br> In this was deemed thy peer. We follow thee <br> With cheerful hearts; nor will our courage fail, <br> I think, in aught that lies within our power.”</p>
<p class="verse">Ulysses, the sagacious, answered thus: <br> “Then will I tell thee what I deem most wise. <br> First take the bath, and then array yourselves <br> In tunics, bid the palace-maidens choose <br> Fresh garments; let the godlike bard, who bears <br> The clear-toned harp, be leader, and strike up <br> A melody to prompt the festive dance, <br> That all may say who hear it from without⁠— <br> Whether the passers by or dwellers near⁠— <br> ‘It is a wedding.’ Else throughout the land <br> The rumor of the slaughter we have wrought <br> Among the suitors may have spread before <br> We reach our wooded farm, and there consult <br> Beneath the guidance of Olympian Jove.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake; they hearkened and obeyed. They took <br> The bath, and then they put their garments on. <br> The maids arrayed themselves; the godlike bard <br> Took the curved harp, and woke in all the love <br> Of melody, and of the graceful dance. <br> The spacious pile resounded to the steps <br> Of men and shapely women in their mirth, <br> And one who stood without was heard to say:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Someone, no doubt, has made the long-wooed queen <br> His bride at last; a worthless woman she, <br> Who could not, for the husband of her youth, <br> Keep his fair palace till he came again.”</p>
<p class="verse">Such words were said, but they who uttered them <br> Knew little what had passed. Eurynomè, <br> The matron of the palace, meantime took <br> Magnanimous Ulysses to the bath <br> In his own dwelling, smoothed his limbs with oil, <br> And threw a gorgeous mantle over him <br> And tunic. Pallas on the hero’s head <br> Shed grace and majesty; she made him seem <br> Taller and statelier, made his locks flow down <br> In curls like blossoms of the hyacinth, <br> As when a workman skilled in many arts, <br> And taught by Pallas and Minerva, twines <br> A golden border round the silver mass, <br> A glorious work; so did the goddess shed <br> Grace o’er his face and form. So from the bath <br> He stepped, like one of the immortals, took <br> The seat from which he rose, right opposite <br> Penelope, and thus addressed the queen:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Lady, the dwellers of the Olympian heights <br> Have given thee an impenetrable heart <br> Beyond all other women. Sure I am <br> No other wife could bring herself to bear <br> Such distance from a husband just returned <br> After long hardships, in the twentieth year <br> Of absence, to his native land and her. <br> Come, nurse, prepare a bed, where by myself <br> I may lie down; an iron heart is hers.”</p>
<p class="verse">To this the sage Penelope replied: <br> “Nay, sir, ’tis not through pride or disregard, <br> Or through excess of wonder, that I act <br> Thus toward thee. Well do I remember thee <br> As thou wert in the day when thy good ship <br> Bore thee from Ithaca. Bestir thyself, <br> Dame Eurycleia, and make up with care <br> A bed without the chamber, which he framed <br> With his own hands; bear out the massive bed, <br> And lay upon it seemly coverings, <br> Fleeces and mantles for his nightly rest.”</p>
<p class="verse">She spake to try her husband; but, displeased, <br> Ulysses answered thus his virtuous queen:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“O woman, thou hast said unwelcome words. <br> Who hath displaced my bed? That task were hard <br> For long-experienced hands, unless some god <br> Had come to shift its place. No living man, <br> Even in his prime of years, could easily <br> Have moved it, for in that elaborate work <br> There was a mystery; it was I myself <br> Who shaped it, no one else. Within my court <br> There grew an olive-tree with full-leaved boughs, <br> A tall and flourishing tree; its massive stem <br> Was like a column. Round it I built up <br> A chamber with cemented stones until <br> The walls were finished; then I framed a roof <br> Above it, and put on the well-glued doors <br> Close fitting. Next I lopped the full-leaved boughs, <br> And, cutting off the trunk above the root, <br> Smoothed well the stump with tools, and made of it <br> A post to bear the couch. I bored the wood <br> With wimbles, placed on it the frame, and carved <br> The work till it was done, inlaying it <br> With silver, gold, and ivory. I stretched <br> Upon it thongs of oxhide brightly dyed <br> In purple. Now, O wife, I cannot know <br> Whether my bed remains as then it was, <br> Or whether someone from the root has hewn <br> The olive trunk, and moved it from its place.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake, and her knees faltered and her heart <br> Was melted as she heard her lord recount <br> The tokens all so truly; and she wept, <br> And rose, and ran to him, and flung her arms <br> About his neck, and kissed his brow, and said:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Ulysses, look not on me angrily, <br> Thou who in other things art wise above <br> All other men. The gods have made our lot <br> A hard one, jealous lest we should have passed <br> Our youth together happily, and thus <br> Have reached old age. I pray, be not incensed, <br> Nor take it ill that I embraced thee not <br> As soon as I beheld thee, for my heart <br> Has ever trembled lest someone who comes <br> Into this isle should cozen me with words; <br> And they who practise fraud are numberless. <br> The Argive Helen, child of Jupiter, <br> Would ne’er have listened to a stranger’s suit <br> And loved him, had she known that in the years <br> To come the warlike Greeks would bring her back <br> To her own land. It was a deity <br> Who prompted her to that foul wrong. Her thought <br> Was never of the great calamity <br> Which followed, and which brought such woe on us. <br> But now, since thou, by tokens clear and true, <br> Hast spoken of our bed, which human eye <br> Has never seen save mine and thine, and those <br> Of one handmaiden only, Actoris⁠— <br> Her whom my father gave me when I came <br> To this thy palace, and who kept the door <br> Of our close chamber⁠—thou hast won my mind <br> To full belief, though hard it was to win.”</p>
<p class="verse">She spake, and he was moved to tears; he wept <br> As in his arms he held his dearly loved <br> And faithful wife. As welcome as the land <br> To those who swim the deep, of whose stout barque <br> Neptune has made a wreck amidst the waves, <br> Tossed by the billow and the blast, and few <br> Are those who from the hoary ocean reach <br> The shore, their limbs all crested with the brine, <br> These gladly climb the sea-beach, and are safe⁠— <br> So welcome was her husband to her eyes. <br> Nor would her fair white arms release his neck, <br> And there would rosy-fingered Morn have found <br> Both weeping, but the blue-eyed Pallas planned <br> That thus it should not be; she stayed the night <br> When near its close, and held the golden Morn <br> Long in the ocean deeps, nor suffered her <br> To yoke her steeds that bring the light to men⁠— <br> Lampas and Phaëthon, swift steeds that bear <br> The Morning on her way. Ulysses then, <br> The man of forecast, thus bespake his queen:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Not yet, O wife, have we attained the close <br> Of all our labors. One remains which yet <br> I must achieve, toilsome, and measureless <br> In difficulty; for so prophesied <br> The spirit of Tiresias, on the day <br> When to the abode of Pluto I went down <br> To ask the seer concerning the return <br> Of my companions, and my own. But now <br> Seek we our couch, dear wife, that, softly laid, <br> We may refresh ourselves with welcome sleep.”</p>
<p class="verse">Then spake in turn the sage Penelope: <br> “Whenever thou desirest it thy couch <br> Shall be made ready, since the gods vouchsafe <br> To bring thee back into thy pleasant home <br> And to thy native land. But now that thou <br> Hast spoken of it, and some deity <br> Is prompting thee, declare what this new task <br> May be. Hereafter I shall hear of it, <br> No doubt, nor were it worse to know it now.”</p>
<p class="verse">Ulysses, the sagacious, answered thus: <br> “Dear wife, why wilt thou ask? why press me thus? <br> Yet will I tell thee truly, nor will keep <br> Aught from thee, though thou wilt not gladly hear, <br> Nor I relate. Tiresias bade me pass <br> Through city after city, till I found <br> A people who know not the sea, nor eat <br> Their food with salt, who never yet beheld <br> The red-prowed galley, nor the shapely oars, <br> Which are the wings of ships. And this plain sign <br> He gave, nor will I keep it back from thee, <br> That when another traveller whom I meet <br> Shall say it is a winnowing-fan I bear <br> On my stout shoulder, there he bade me plant <br> The oar upright in earth, and offer up <br> To monarch Neptune there a ram, a bull, <br> And sturdy boar, and then, returning home, <br> Burn hallowed hecatombs to all the gods <br> Who dwell in the broad heaven, each one in turn. <br> At last will death come over me, afar <br> From ocean, such a death as peacefully <br> Shall take me off in a serene old age, <br> Amid a people prosperous and content. <br> All this, the prophet said, will come to pass.”</p>
<p class="verse">And then the sage Penelope rejoined: <br> “If thus the immortals make thy later age <br> The happier, there is hope that thou wilt find <br> Escape from evil in the years to come.”</p>
<p class="verse">So talked they with each other. Meantime went <br> Eurynomè, attended by the nurse, <br> And in the light of blazing torches dressed <br> With soft fresh drapery a bed; and when <br> Their busy hands had made it full and high, <br> The aged dame withdrew to take her rest <br> In her own chamber, while Eurynomè, <br> Who kept the royal bower, upheld a torch <br> And thither led the pair, and, when they both <br> Were in the chamber, went her way. They took <br> Their place delighted in the ancient bed. <br> The prince, the herdsman, and the swineherd ceased <br> Meantime to tread the dance, and bade the maids <br> Cease also, and within the palace-rooms <br> Dark with night’s shadow, sought their place of rest. <br> Then came the time of pleasant mutual talk, <br> In which that noblest among women spake <br> Of wrongs endured beneath her roof from those <br> Who came to woo her⁠—an insatiate crew⁠— <br> Who made of beeves and fatlings of the flock <br> Large slaughter, and drained many a wine-cask dry. <br> Then nobly born Ulysses told what woes <br> His valor brought on other men; what toils <br> And suffering he had borne; he told her all, <br> And she, delighted, heard him, nor did sleep <br> Light on her eyelids till his tale was done.</p>
<p class="verse">And first he told her how he overcame <br> The people of Ciconia; how he passed <br> Thence to the rich fields of the race who feed <br> Upon the lotus; what the Cyclops did, <br> And how upon the Cyclops he avenged <br> The death of his brave comrades, whom the wretch <br> Had piteously slaughtered and devoured. <br> And how he came to Aeolus, and found <br> A friendly welcome, and was sent by him <br> Upon his voyage; yet ’twas not his fate <br> To reach his native land; a tempest caught <br> His fleet, and far across the fishy deep <br> Bore him away, lamenting bitterly. <br> And how he landed at Telepylus, <br> Among the Laestrigonians, who destroyed <br> His ships and warlike comrades, he alone <br> In his black ship escaping. Then he told <br> Of Circè, her deceit and many arts, <br> And how he went to Pluto’s dismal realm <br> In his good galley, to consult the soul <br> Of him of Thebes, Tiresias, and beheld <br> All his lost comrades and his mother⁠—her <br> Who brought him forth, and trained him when a child. <br> And how he heard the Sirens afterward, <br> And how he came upon the wandering rocks, <br> The terrible Charybdis, and the crags <br> Of Scylla⁠—which no man had ever passed <br> In safety; how his comrades slew for food <br> The oxen of the Sun; how Jupiter, <br> The Thunderer, with a bolt of fire from heaven <br> Smote his swift barque; and how his gallant crew <br> All perished, he alone escaped with life. <br> And how he reached Ogygia’s isle, he told, <br> And met the nymph Calypso, who desired <br> That he would be her husband, and long time <br> Detained and fed him in her vaulted grot, <br> And promised that he ne’er should die, nor know <br> Decay of age, through all the days to come; <br> Yet moved she not the purpose of his heart. <br> And how he next through many hardships came <br> To the Phaeacians, and they welcomed him <br> And honored him as if he were a god, <br> And to his native country in a barque <br> Sent him with ample gifts of brass and gold <br> And raiment. As he uttered this last word, <br> Sleep softly overcame him; all his limbs <br> Lay loose in rest, and all his cares were calmed.</p>
<p class="verse">The blue-eyed Pallas had yet new designs; <br> And when she deemed Ulysses was refreshed <br> With rest and sleep, in that accustomed bed, <br> She called the Morning, daughter of the Dawn, <br> To rise from ocean in her car of gold, <br> And shed her light on men. Ulysses rose <br> From his soft couch, and thus enjoined his spouse:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“O wife! enough of misery have we borne <br> Already⁠—thou in weeping for my long <br> Unhappy absence⁠—I for years withheld <br> By Jupiter and all the other gods <br> From my return to this dear land, although <br> I pined for home. Now since upon this couch <br> We take the place so earnestly desired, <br> Take thou the charge of all that I possess <br> Here in the palace. For the herds and flocks <br> Which those high-handed suitors have devoured, <br> I shall seize many others as a spoil; <br> The rest the Greeks will bring me, till my stalls <br> Are filled again. I hasten to my farm <br> Embowered in trees, to greet the aged man <br> My excellent father, who continually <br> Grieves for me. Prudent as thou art, I give <br> This charge; a rumor, with the rising sun, <br> Will quickly go abroad that I have slain <br> The suitors in the palace. Now withdraw, <br> Thou and thy maidens, to the upper room, <br> And sit and look not forth, nor ask of aught.”</p>
<p class="verse">So spake the chief, and on his shoulders braced <br> His glorious armor. Then he called his son, <br> The herdsman, and the swineherd, bidding them <br> To take in hand their weapons. They obeyed, <br> And, having armed themselves in brass, they threw <br> The portals open. As they all went forth, <br> Ulysses led the way. The early light <br> Was on the earth, but Pallas, shrouding them <br> In darkness, led them quickly through the town.</p>
