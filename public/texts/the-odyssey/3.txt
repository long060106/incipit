<p class="lede">Arrival of Telemachus, with Pallas in the shape of Mentor, at Pylos⁠—His interview with Nestor⁠—Nestor’s narrative of his return from Troy⁠—History of the death of Agamemnon and the revenge of Orestes⁠—Departure of Pallas to heaven⁠—Telemachus sent by Nestor with his son Peisistratus to Menelaus at Sparta.</p>
<p class="verse">Now from the fair broad bosom of the sea <br> Into the brazen vault of heaven the sun <br> Rose shining for the immortals and for men <br> Upon the foodful earth. The voyagers <br> Arrived at Pylos, nobly built, the town <br> Of Neleus. There, upon the ocean-side, <br> They found the people offering coal-black steers <br> To dark-haired Neptune. On nine seats they sat, <br> Five hundred on each seat; nine steers were slain <br> For each five hundred there. While they performed <br> The rite, and, tasting first the entrails, burned <br> The thighs to ocean’s god, the Ithacans <br> Touched land, and, lifting up the good ship’s sail, <br> Furled it and moored the keel, and then stepped out <br> Upon the shore. Forth from the galley came <br> Telemachus, the goddess guiding him, <br> And thus to him the blue-eyed Pallas said:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Telemachus, there now is no excuse, <br> Not even the least, for shamefaced backwardness. <br> Thou hast come hither o’er the deep to ask <br> For tidings of thy father⁠—what far land <br> Conceals him, what the fate that he has met. <br> Go then at once to Nestor, the renowned <br> In horsemanship, and we shall see what plan <br> He hath in mind for thee. Entreat him there <br> That frankly he declare it. He will speak <br> No word of falsehood; he is truly wise.”</p>
<p class="verse">And thus discreet Telemachus replied:⁠— <br> “O Mentor, how shall I approach the chief, <br> And with what salutation? Little skill <br> Have I in courtly phrase, and shame becomes <br> A youth in questioning an aged man.”</p>
<p class="verse">Pallas, the blue-eyed goddess, spake again:⁠— <br> “In part thy mind will prompt thy speech; in part <br> A god will put the words into thy mouth⁠— <br> For well I deem that thou wert neither born <br> Nor trained without the favor of the gods.”</p>
<p class="verse">Thus having said, the blue-eyed Pallas moved <br> With hasty pace before, and in her steps <br> He followed close, until they reached the seats <br> Of those assembled Pylians. Nestor there <br> Sat with his sons, while his companions stood <br> Around him and prepared the feast, and some <br> Roasted the flesh at fires, and some transfixed <br> The parts with spits. As they beheld the approach <br> Of strangers they advanced, and took their hands, <br> And bade them sit. Peisistratus, a son <br> Of Nestor, came the first of all, and took <br> A hand of each, and placed them at the feast <br> On the soft hides that o’er the ocean sand <br> Were spread beside his brother Thrasymedes <br> And his own father; brought for their repast <br> Parts of the entrails, poured for them the wine <br> Into a golden goblet, held it forth <br> In his right hand, and with these words bespake <br> Pallas, the child of aegis-bearing Jove:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Pray, stranger, to King Neptune. Ye have chanced <br> Upon his feast in coming to our coast. <br> And after thy libation poured, and prayer <br> Made to the god, give over to thy friend <br> The goblet of choice wine that he may make <br> Libation also; he, I question not, <br> Prays to the gods; we all have need of them. <br> A younger man is he than thou, and seems <br> In age to be my equal; therefore I <br> Will give the golden goblet first to thee.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake, and in the hands of Pallas placed <br> The goblet of choice wine. Well pleased was she <br> With one so just and so discreet⁠—well pleased <br> That first to her he reached the cup of gold, <br> And thus she prayed to Neptune fervently:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Hear, Neptune, thou who dost embrace the earth, <br> And of thy grace disdain not to bestow <br> These blessings on thy suppliants. First of all <br> Vouchsafe to Nestor and his sons increase <br> Of glory; on the Pylian people next <br> Bestow, for this most sumptuous hecatomb, <br> Large recompense; and, lastly, grant to us⁠— <br> Telemachus and me⁠—a safe return <br> To our own country with the end attained <br> Which brought us hither in our gallant barque.”</p>
<p class="verse">Thus did she pray, while she fulfilled the prayer; <br> And then she handed to Telemachus <br> The fair round goblet, and in words like hers <br> The dear son of Ulysses prayed. Meanwhile <br> The Pylians, having roasted well the flesh <br> And drawn it from the spits, distributing <br> To each his portion, held high festival. <br> And when the calls of hunger and of thirst <br> Were silenced, Nestor, the Gerenian knight, <br> Began discourse, and thus bespake his guests:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“The fitting time is come to ask our guests <br> Who they may be, since now their feast is o’er. <br> Say then, O strangers, who ye are, and whence <br> Ye come along the pathway of the deep. <br> Have ye an errand here, or do ye roam <br> The seas at large, like pirates, braving death, <br> And visiting with ravage foreign states?”</p>
<p class="verse">And then discreet Telemachus replied <br> Boldly⁠—for Pallas strengthened in that hour <br> His heart that he might confidently ask <br> News of his absent father, and so win <br> A worthy fame among the sons of men:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“O Nestor, son of Neleus, pride of Greece! <br> Thou bid’st us tell thee whence we came, and I <br> Will faithfully declare it. We are come <br> From Ithaca, beneath the Neritus, <br> And private, and not general, is the cause <br> Of which I am to speak. I came to ask <br> Concerning my great father, the large-souled <br> And nobly-born Ulysses, who ’tis said <br> With thee, his friend in arms, laid waste the town <br> Of Ilium. We have heard where all the rest <br> Who warred against the Trojans were cut off, <br> And died sad deaths; his fate alone the son <br> Of Saturn hath not chosen to reveal⁠— <br> Whether he fell on land by hostile hands, <br> Or while at sea was whelmed beneath the waves <br> Of Amphitritè. Wherefore to thy knees <br> I come, to ask that thou⁠—if so thou wilt⁠— <br> Relate the manner of his mournful death, <br> As thou didst see it with thine eyes, or else <br> As thou from other wanderers hast heard <br> Its history; for she who brought him forth <br> Bore him to be unhappy. Think thou not <br> To soften aught, through tenderness to me, <br> In thy recital, but in faithful words <br> Tell me the whole, whatever thou hast seen. <br> And I conjure thee, that if, in his life, <br> My father, great Ulysses, ever gave <br> Promise of word or deed for thee, and kept <br> His promise, in the realm of Troy, where ye <br> Achaians bore such hardships, that thou now <br> Remember it and speak without disguise.”</p>
<p class="verse">And Nestor the Gerenian knight replied:⁠— <br> “My friend, since thou recallest to my mind <br> The sufferings borne by us the sons of Greece, <br> Although of peerless valor, in that land, <br> Both when we ranged in ships the darkling sea <br> For booty wheresoe’er Achilles led, <br> And when around King Priam’s populous town <br> We fought, where fell our bravest, know thou then <br> That there the valiant Ajax lies, and there <br> Achilles; there Patroclus, like the gods <br> In council; there my well-beloved son <br> Blameless and brave, Antilochus the swift <br> Of foot and warlike⁠—many woes beside <br> We bore, and who of mortal birth could give <br> Their history? Nay, though thou shouldst remain <br> Five years or six, and ask of all the griefs <br> Endured by the brave Greeks, thou wouldst depart <br> Outwearied to thy home, ere thou hadst heard <br> The whole. Nine years in harassing the foe <br> We passed, beleaguering them and planning wiles <br> Innumerable. Saturn’s son at last <br> With difficulty seemed to close the war. <br> Then was there none who might presume to vie <br> In wisdom with Ulysses; that great man <br> Excelled in every kind of stratagem⁠— <br> Thy father⁠—if indeed thou be his son. <br> I look on thee amazed; all thy discourse <br> Is just like his, and one would ne’er believe <br> A younger man could speak so much like him. <br> While we were there, Ulysses and myself <br> In council or assembly never spake <br> On different sides, but with a like intent <br> We thoughtfully consulted how to guide <br> The Achaians in the way we deemed the best; <br> But after we had overthrown and spoiled <br> King Priam’s lofty city, and set sail <br> For home, and by some heavenly power the Greeks <br> Were scattered, Jupiter ordained for them <br> A sad return. For all were neither wise <br> Nor just, and many drew upon themselves <br> An evil doom⁠—the fatal wrath of her, <br> The blue-eyed maid, who claims her birth from Jove. <br> ’Twas she who kindled strife between the sons <br> Of Atreus. They had called the Achaians all <br> To an assembly, not with due regard <br> To order, at the setting of the sun, <br> And thither came the warriors overpowered <br> With wine. The brother kings set forth the cause <br> Of that assembly. Menelaus first <br> Bade all the Greeks prepare for their return <br> O’er the great deep. That counsel little pleased <br> King Agamemnon, who desired to keep <br> The people longer there, that he might soothe <br> By sacred hecatombs the fiery wrath <br> Of Pallas. Fool! who could not see how vain <br> Were such persuasion, for the eternal gods <br> Are not soon won to change their purposes. <br> They stood disputing thus, with bitter words, <br> Till wrangling noisily on different sides <br> Rose up the well-armed Greeks. The ensuing night <br> We rested, but we cherished in our breasts <br> A mutual hate; so for our punishment <br> Had Jove ordained. With early morn we drew <br> Our ships to the great deep, and put our goods <br> And our deep-bosomed women all on board. <br> Yet half the host went not, but on the shore <br> Remained with Agamemnon, Atreus’ son, <br> And shepherd of the people. All the rest <br> Embarked, weighed anchor, and sailed swiftly thence; <br> A deity made smooth the mighty deep, <br> And when we came to Tenedos we paid <br> Our offerings to the gods and longed for home⁠— <br> Vainly; it pleased not unpropitious Jove <br> To favor our return, and once again <br> He sent among us strife. A part of us <br> Led by Ulysses, that sagacious prince, <br> To please Atrides Agamemnon turned <br> Their well-oared galleys back. But I, with all <br> The vessels of the fleet that followed me, <br> Fled on my way, perceiving that some god <br> Was meditating evil. With us fled, <br> Encouraging his men, the warlike son <br> Of Tydeus. Fair-haired Menelaus came <br> Later to us in Lesbos, where we planned <br> For a long voyage, whether we should sail <br> Around the rugged Chios, toward the isle <br> Of Psyria, keeping that upon the left, <br> Or under Chios pass beside the steeps <br> Of windy Mimas. We besought the god <br> That he would show a sign, and he complied, <br> And bade us to Euboea cross the deep <br> Right in the midst, the sooner to escape <br> All danger. Then the wind blew strong and shrill, <br> And swiftly o’er the fishy gulfs our fleet <br> Flew on, and reached Geraestus in the night. <br> There, having passed the mighty deep, we made <br> To Neptune offerings of many a thigh <br> Of beeves. The fourth day dawned, and now the men <br> Of Diomed, the mighty horseman, son <br> Of Tydeus, stopped at Argos with their fleet, <br> While I went on to Pylos with the wind, <br> Which never, from the moment that the god <br> First sent it o’er the waters, ceased to blow.</p>
<p class="verse">“So, my dear child, I reached my home, nor knew <br> Nor heard from others who among the Greeks <br> Was saved, or who had perished on the way. <br> Yet what I since have heard while here I sit <br> Within my palace thou shalt duly learn. <br> Nor is it what I ought to keep from thee.</p>
<p class="verse">“ ’Tis said the Myrmidonian spearmen, led <br> By great Achilles’ famous son, returned <br> Happily home; as happily the son <br> Of Paeas, Philoctetes the renowned. <br> Idomeneus brought also back to Crete <br> All his companions who survived the war; <br> The sea took none of them. But ye have heard, <br> Though far away, the fate of Atreus’ son⁠— <br> How he came home, and how Aegisthus laid <br> A plot to slay him, yet on his own head <br> Drew heavy punishment⁠—so fortunate <br> It is when he who falls by murder leaves <br> A son; for ’twas the monarch’s son who took <br> Vengeance upon the crafty murderer <br> Aegisthus, by whose hand Atrides died. <br> Thou too, my friend, for thou art large of frame, <br> And of a noble presence, be thou brave, <br> That men in time to come may give thee praise.”</p>
<p class="verse">Then spake discreet Telemachus again:⁠— <br> “O Nestor, son of Neleus, pride of Greece, <br> Ample was his revenge, and far and wide <br> The Greeks will spread his fame to be the song <br> Of future times. O might the gods confer <br> On me an equal power to avenge myself <br> On that importunate, overbearing crew <br> Of suitors, who insult me, and devise <br> Evil against me! But the gods deny <br> Such fortune to my father and to me, <br> And all that now is left me is to bear.”</p>
<p class="verse">Again spake Nestor the Gerenian knight:⁠— <br> “Since thou, my friend, hast spoken words which bring <br> What I have heard to mind⁠—the rumor goes <br> That in thy palace many suitors wait <br> About thy mother, and in spite of thee <br> Do grievous wrong. Now tell me; dost thou yield <br> Willingly, or because the people, swayed <br> By oracles, regard thee as their foe? <br> Thy father yet may come again⁠—who knows?⁠— <br> Alone, or with the other Greeks, to take <br> The vengeance which these violent deeds deserve. <br> Should blue-eyed Pallas deign to favor thee, <br> As once she watched to guard the glorious chief <br> Ulysses in the realm of Troy, where we, <br> The Achaians, bore such hardships⁠—for I ne’er <br> Have seen the gods so openly befriend <br> A man as Pallas there befriended him⁠— <br> Should she thus deign to favor thee and keep <br> Watch over thee, then haply some of these <br> Will never think of marriage rites again.”</p>
<p class="verse">Then spake discreet Telemachus again:⁠— <br> “O aged man! I cannot think thy words <br> Will be fulfilled! for they import too much <br> And they amaze me. What thou sayst I wish <br> May come to pass, but know it cannot be, <br> Not even though the gods should will it so.”</p>
<p class="verse">Then thus the blue-eyed goddess, Pallas, spake:⁠— <br> “Telemachus, what words have passed thy lips? <br> Easily can a god, whene’er he will, <br> In the most distant regions safely keep <br> A man; and I would rather reach my home <br> Securely, after many hardships borne, <br> Than perish suddenly on my return <br> As Agamemnon perished by the guile <br> Of base Aegisthus and the queen. And yet <br> The gods themselves have not the power to save <br> Whom most they cherish from the common doom <br> When cruel fate brings on the last long sleep.”</p>
<p class="verse">Discreet Telemachus made answer thus:⁠— <br> “Let us, O Mentor, talk no more of this, <br> Though much we grieve; he never will return, <br> For his is the black doom of death ordained <br> By the great gods. Now suffer me to ask <br> Of Nestor further, since to him are known, <br> Beyond all other men, the rules of right <br> And prudence. He has governed, so men say, <br> Three generations, and to me he seems <br> In aspect like the ever-living gods. <br> O Nestor, son of Neleus, truly say <br> How died the monarch over mighty realms, <br> Atrides Agamemnon? Where was then <br> His brother Menelaus? By what arts <br> Did treacherous Aegisthus plan his death, <br> And slay a braver warrior than himself? <br> Was not the brother in the Achaian town <br> Of Argos? or was he a wanderer <br> In other lands, which made the murderer bold?”</p>
<p class="verse">The knight, Gerenian Nestor, answered thus:⁠— <br> “I will tell all and truly. Thou hast guessed <br> Rightly and as it happened. Had the son <br> Of Atreus, fair-haired Menelaus, come <br> From Troy, and found Aegisthus yet alive <br> Within the palace, he had never flung <br> The loose earth on his corpse, but dogs and birds <br> Had preyed upon it, lying in the fields <br> Far from the city, and no woman’s voice <br> Of all the Greeks had raised the wail for him. <br> Great was the crime he plotted. We were yet <br> Afar, enduring the hard toils of war, <br> While he, securely couched in his retreat <br> At Argos, famed for steeds, with flattering words <br> Corrupted Agamemnon’s queen. At first <br> The noble Clytemnestra turned away <br> With horror from the crime; for yet her heart <br> Was right, and by her side there stood a bard <br> With whom Atrides, when he went to Troy, <br> Had left his wife with many an earnest charge. <br> But when the gods and fate had spread a net <br> For his destruction, then Aegisthus bore <br> The minstrel to a desert isle, and there <br> Left him to be devoured by birds of prey, <br> And led the queen, as willing as himself, <br> To his own palace. Many a victim’s thigh <br> Upon the hallowed altars of the gods <br> He offered, many a gift of ornaments <br> Woven or wrought in gold he hung within <br> Their temples, since at length the mighty end <br> For which he hardly dared to hope was gained. <br> We sailed together from the coast of Troy, <br> Atrides, Menelaus, and myself, <br> Friends to each other. When the headland height <br> Of Athens, hallowed Sunium, met our eyes, <br> Apollo smote with his still shafts, and slew <br> Phrontis, Onetor’s son, who steered the barque <br> Of Menelaus, holding in his hands <br> The rudder as the galley scudded on⁠— <br> And skilled was he beyond all other men <br> To guide a vessel when the storm was high. <br> So there did Menelaus stay his course, <br> Though eager to go on, that he might lay <br> His friend in earth and pay the funeral rites. <br> But setting sail again with all his fleet <br> Upon the dark-blue sea, all-seeing Jove <br> Decreed a perilous voyage. He sent forth <br> His shrill-voiced hurricane, and heaped on high <br> The mountain waves. There, scattering the barques <br> Far from each other, part he drove to Crete, <br> Where the Cydonians dwell, beside the stream <br> Of Jardanus. A smooth and pointed rock <br> Just on the bounds of Gortys stands amidst <br> The dark-blue deep. The south wind thitherward <br> Sweeps a great sea towards Phoestus, and against <br> The headland on the left, where that small rock <br> Meets and withstands the mighty wave. The ships <br> Were driven on this, and scarce the crews escaped <br> With life; the ships were dashed against the crags <br> And wrecked, save five, and these, with their black prows, <br> Were swept toward Egypt by the winds and waves.</p>
<p class="verse">“Thus adding to his wealth and gathering gold <br> He roamed the ocean in his ships among <br> Men of strange speech. Aegisthus meantime planned <br> His guilty deeds at home; he slew the king <br> Atrides, and the people took his yoke. <br> Seven years in rich Mycenae he bore rule, <br> And on the eighth, to his destruction, came <br> The nobly-born Orestes, just returned <br> From Athens, and cut off that man of blood, <br> The crafty wretch Aegisthus, by whose hand <br> Fell his illustrious father. Then he bade <br> The Argives to the solemn burial-feast <br> Of his bad mother and the craven wretch <br> Aegisthus. Menelaus, that same day, <br> The great in war, arrived, and brought large wealth⁠— <br> So large his galleys could contain no more. <br> “And thou, my friend, be thou not long away, <br> Wandering from home, thy rich possessions left, <br> And in thy palace-halls a lawless crew, <br> Lest they devour thy substance, and divide <br> Thy goods, and thou have crossed the sea in vain. <br> Yet must I counsel and enjoin on thee <br> To visit Menelaus, who has come <br> Just now from lands and nations of strange men, <br> Whence one could hardly hope for a return; <br> Whom once the tempest’s violence had driven <br> Into that great wide sea o’er which the birds <br> Of heaven could scarce fly hither in a year, <br> Such is its fearful vastness. Go thou now, <br> Thou with thy ship and friends; or if thou choose <br> The way by land, a car and steeds are here, <br> And here my sons to guide thee to the town <br> Of hallowed Lacedaemon, there to find <br> The fair-haired Menelaus. Earnestly <br> Beseech of him that he declare the truth. <br> Falsely he will not speak, for he is wise.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake; the sun went down; the darkness crept <br> Over the earth, and blue-eyed Pallas said:⁠— <br> “Most wisely hast thou spoken, ancient man. <br> Now cut ye out the tongues, and mingle wine, <br> That we to Neptune and the other gods <br> May pour libations, and then think of rest; <br> For now the hour is come; the light is gone, <br> Nor at a feast in honor of the gods <br> Should we long sit, but in good time withdraw.”</p>
<p class="verse">Jove’s daughter spake; they hearkened to her words; <br> The heralds came to them, and on their hands <br> Poured water; boys began to fill the bowls <br> To the hard brim, and ministered to each <br> From left to right. Then threw they to the flames <br> The victims’ tongues, and, rising, poured on earth <br> Wine to the gods; and when that rite was paid, <br> And when their thirst was satiate, Pallas rose <br> With nobly-born Telemachus to go <br> To their good ship, but Nestor still detained <br> The twain, and chidingly bespake them thus:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Now Jove and all the other gods forbid <br> That ye should go from me to your good ship, <br> As from some half-clad wretch, too poor to own <br> Mantles and blankets in whose soft warm folds <br> He and his guests might sleep; but I have both⁠— <br> Mantles and blankets⁠—beautifully wrought, <br> And never shall the son of that great man <br> Ulysses lie upon a galley’s deck <br> While I am living. After me I hope <br> My sons, who dwell within my palace-halls, <br> Will duly welcome all who enter here.”</p>
<p class="verse">And thus again the blue-eyed Pallas spake:⁠— <br> “Well hast thou said, my aged friend, and well <br> Doth it become Telemachus to heed <br> Thy words, for that were best. Let him remain <br> With thee and sleep in thine abode, while I <br> Repair to our black ship, encouraging <br> The crew, and setting them their proper tasks, <br> For I am eldest of them all; the rest <br> Are young men yet, and moved by friendship joined <br> Our enterprise; the peers in age are they <br> Of the large-souled Telemachus. Tonight <br> I sleep within the hull of our black ship, <br> And sail with early morning for the land <br> Of the Cauconians, large of soul, from whom <br> A debt is due me, neither new nor small. <br> Send meantime from thy palace in a car, <br> And with thy son, this youth, and be the steeds <br> The fleetest and the strongest in thy stalls.”</p>
<p class="verse">The blue-eyed Pallas, having spoken thus, <br> Passed like an eagle out of sight, and all <br> Were seized with deep amazement as they saw. <br> The aged monarch, wondering at the sight, <br> Took by the hand Telemachus, and said:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Of craven temper, and unapt for war, <br> O friend, thou canst not be, since thus the gods <br> Attend and guide thee in thy youth. And this, <br> Of all the gods whose dwelling is in heaven, <br> Can be no other than the spoiler-queen <br> Pallas, the child of Jove, who also held <br> Thy father in such eminent esteem <br> Among the Grecians. Deign to favor us, <br> O queen! bestow on me and on my sons <br> And on my venerable spouse the meed <br> Of special glory. I will bring to thee <br> A sacrifice, a broad-horned yearling steer, <br> Which never man hath tamed or led beneath <br> The yoke. Her will I bring with gilded horns, <br> And lay an offering on thine altar-fires.”</p>
<p class="verse">Such were his words, and Pallas heard the prayer, <br> And then Gerenian Nestor led the way, <br> And with his sons and sons-in-law approached <br> His glorious palace. When they came within <br> The monarch’s sumptuous halls, each took his place <br> In order on the couches and the thrones. <br> The old man mingled for them as they came <br> A bowl of delicate wine, eleven years old, <br> Drawn by the damsel cupbearer, who took <br> Its cover from the jar. The aged chief <br> Mingled it in the bowl, and, pouring out <br> A part to Pallas, offered earnest prayer <br> To her, who sprang from aegis-bearing Jove.</p>
<p class="verse">When due libations had been made, and all <br> Drank till they wished no more, most went away, <br> Each to his home to sleep; but Nestor made <br> Telemachus, the son of the great chief <br> Ulysses, rest upon a sumptuous couch <br> Within the echoing hall, and near to him <br> The chief of squadrons, skilled to wield the spear, <br> Peisistratus, who only of his sons <br> Abode in Nestor’s halls unwedded yet; <br> While in an inner room of that tall pile <br> The monarch slumbered on a bed of state, <br> Decked for him by the labors of his queen.</p>
<p class="verse">Soon as the daughter of the dawn appeared, <br> The rosy-fingered Morning, Nestor left <br> His bed and went abroad, and took his seat <br> On smooth white stones before his lofty doors, <br> That glistened as with oil, on which before <br> Sat Neleus, wise in council as the gods. <br> But he had yielded to the will of fate, <br> And passed into the Underworld. Now sat <br> Gerenian Nestor in his father’s place, <br> The guardian of the Greeks. Around his seat, <br> Just from the chambers of their rest, his sons <br> Echephron, Stratius, and Aretus came, <br> Perseus, and Thrasymedes; after these <br> Came brave Peisistratus, the sixth and last. <br> They led Telemachus, the godlike youth, <br> And placed him near them. The Gerenian knight <br> Nestor began, and thus bespake his sons:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Do quickly what I ask, dear sons, and aid <br> To render Pallas, first of all the gods, <br> Propitious⁠—Pallas, who has deigned to come, <br> And at a solemn feast to manifest <br> Herself to me. Let one of you go forth <br> Among the fields, and bring a heifer thence, <br> Led by the herdsman. To the dark-hulled ship <br> Of the large-souled Telemachus I bid <br> Another son repair, and bring the crew <br> Save only two; and let another call <br> Laërceus hither, skilled to work in gold, <br> That he may plate with gold the heifer’s horns. <br> Let all the rest remain to bid the maids <br> Within prepare a sumptuous feast, and bring <br> Seats, wood, and limpid water from the fount.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake, and all were busy. From the field <br> The bullock came; from the swift-sailing barque <br> Came the companions of the gallant youth <br> Telemachus; with all his implements⁠— <br> Hammer and anvil, and well-jointed tongs⁠— <br> With which he wrought, the goldsmith also came, <br> And to be present at the sacred rites <br> Pallas came likewise. Nestor, aged knight, <br> Brought forth the gold; the artisan prepared <br> The metal, and about the bullock’s horns <br> Wound it, that Pallas might with pleasure see <br> The victim so adorned. Then Stratius grasped <br> The horns, and, aided by Echephron, led <br> The bullock. From his room Aretus brought <br> A laver filled with water in one hand, <br> And in the other hand a canister <br> Of cakes, while Thrasymedes, great in war, <br> Stood near with a sharp axe, about to smite <br> The victim. Perseus held a vase to catch <br> The blood, while Nestor, aged horseman, took <br> Water and cakes, and offering first a part, <br> And flinging the shorn forelock to the flames, <br> Prayed to the goddess Pallas fervently.</p>
<p class="verse">And now, when they had prayed, and flung the cakes, <br> The large-souled Thrasymedes, Nestor’s son, <br> Struck, where he stood, the blow; the bullock’s strength <br> Gave way. At once the daughters of the king, <br> And his sons’ wives, and queen Eurydicè⁠— <br> Nestor’s chaste wife, and daughter eldest born <br> Of Clymenus, broke forth in shrilly cries. <br> From the great earth the sons then lifted up <br> And held the victim’s head. Peisistratus, <br> The chief of squadrons, slew it. When the blood <br> Had ceased to flow, and life had left its limbs, <br> They quickly severed joint from joint; they hewed <br> The thighs away, and duly covered them <br> With caul, a double fold, on which they laid <br> Raw strips of flesh. The aged monarch burned <br> These over the cleft wood, and poured dark wine <br> Upon them, while beside him stood the youths <br> With five-pronged spits; and when the thighs were burned <br> And entrails tasted, all the rest they carved <br> Into small portions and transfixed with spits, <br> And roasted, holding the sharp spits in hand. <br> Meantime, fair Polycastè, youngest born <br> Of Nestor’s daughters, gave Telemachus <br> The bath; and after he had bathed she shed <br> A rich oil over him, and in a cloak <br> Of noble texture and a tunic robed <br> The prince, who, like a god in presence, left <br> The bath, and took his place where Nestor sat, <br> The shepherd of the people. When the youths <br> Had roasted well and from the spits withdrawn <br> The flesh, they took their places at the feast. <br> Then rose up chosen men, and poured the wine <br> Into the cups of gold; and when at length <br> The thirst and appetite were both allayed, <br> The knight, Gerenian Nestor, thus began:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Rise now, my sons; join to the bright-haired steeds <br> My car, and let Telemachus depart.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake; they hearkened and obeyed, and straight <br> Yoked the swift horses to the car. Then came <br> The matron of the household, laying bread <br> And wine within the car, and dainties such <br> As make a prince’s fare. Telemachus <br> Then climbed into the sumptuous seat. The son <br> Of Nestor and the chief of armed bands, <br> Peisistratus, climbed also, took his place <br> Beside him, grasped the reins, and with the lash <br> Urged on the coursers. Not unwillingly <br> They darted toward the plain, and left behind <br> The lofty Pylos. All that day they shook <br> The yoke on both their necks. The sun went down; <br> The highways lay in darkness when they came <br> To Pherae and the abode of Diocles, <br> Son of Orsilochus, who claimed to be <br> The offspring of Alpheius. They with him <br> Found welcome there, and there that night they slept.</p>
<p class="verse">And when the rosy-fingered Morn appeared, <br> They yoked the horses, climbed the shining car, <br> And issued from the palace gate beneath <br> The sounding portico. Peisistratus <br> Wielded the lash to urge the coursers on, <br> And not unwillingly they flew and reached <br> A land of harvests. Here the travellers found <br> Their journey’s end, so swiftly those fleet steeds <br> Had borne them on. And now the sun went down, <br> And darkness gathered over all the ways.</p>
