<p class="lede">Proposal of Penelope to the suitors to contend for her hand with the bow and arrows of Ulysses⁠—Their ineffectual attempts to bend the bow⁠—Management of Ulysses to obtain the bow, which he bends with ease, and sends an arrow through the twelve rings set up in a row for the purpose.</p>
<p class="verse">Pallas, the goddess of the azure eyes, <br> Woke in the mind of sage Penelope, <br> The daughter of Icarius, this design⁠— <br> To put into the suitors’ hands the bow <br> And gray steel rings, and to propose a game <br> That in the palace was to usher in <br> The slaughter. So she climbed the lofty stair, <br> Up from the hall, and took in her plump hand <br> The fair carved key; its wards were wrought of brass, <br> And ivory was the handle. Soon she reached <br> The furthest room with her attendant maids. <br> There lay the treasures of Ulysses⁠—brass <br> And gold, and steel divinely wrought. There lay <br> His bow unstrung; there lay his quiver charged <br> With arrows; many were the deadly shafts <br> It held, a stranger’s gift, who met him once <br> In Lacedaemon, Iphitus by name, <br> The son of Eurytus, and like the gods <br> In presence. In Messenè met the twain, <br> And in the mansion of Orsilochus, <br> The warlike. Thither had Ulysses come <br> To claim a debt from all the region round; <br> For rovers from Messenè to their ships <br> Had driven and carried off from Ithaca <br> Three hundred sheep and those who tended them. <br> For this Ulysses, though a stripling yet, <br> Came that long voyage, on an embassy, <br> Sent by his father and the other chiefs. <br> And Iphitus had come in search of steeds <br> Which he had lost⁠—twelve mares, and under them <br> Twelve hardy mules, their foals. That errand brought <br> The doom of death upon him. For he came, <br> In journeying, to the abode of Hercules, <br> The mighty hero-son of Jupiter, <br> Famed for his labors, who, in his own house, <br> Slew Iphitus, the stranger. Cruel wretch! <br> Who reverenced not the vengeance of the gods, <br> Nor what was due to his own board, at which <br> He placed his guest, and slew him afterward, <br> And in his stables kept the goodly mares. <br> ’Twas when this guest was seeking for his steeds <br> He met Ulysses, and bestowed on him <br> The bow, which mighty Eurytus once bore, <br> And dying in his lofty palace left <br> The weapon to his son. Ulysses gave <br> In turn a trenchant sword and massive lance, <br> A pledge of kindly hospitality, <br> Begun, but not continued till they sat <br> Each at the other’s table; for the son <br> Of Jove first took the life of him who gave <br> The bow, the godlike son of Eurytus. <br> That bow Ulysses, when he went to war <br> In his black galleys, never took with him, <br> But left it in his palace, to be kept <br> In memory of a beloved friend, <br> And only bore it in his own domain.</p>
<p class="verse">Now when the glorious lady reached the room, <br> And stood upon the threshold, wrought of oak <br> And polished by the workman’s cunning hand, <br> Who stretched the line upon it, and set up <br> Its posts, and hung its shining doors, she loosed <br> With a quick touch the thong that held the ring, <br> Put in the key, and with a careful aim <br> Struck back the sounding bolts. As when a bull <br> Roars in the field, such sound the beautiful doors, <br> Struck with the key, gave forth, and instantly <br> They opened to her. Up the lofty floor <br> She stepped, where stood the coffer that contained <br> The perfumed garments. Reaching forth her hand, <br> The queen took down the bow, that hung within <br> Its shining case, and sat her down, and laid <br> The case upon her knees, and, drawing forth <br> The monarch’s bow, she wept aloud. As soon <br> As that new gush of tears had ceased to fall, <br> Back to the hall she went, and that proud throng <br> Of suitors, bearing in her hand the bow <br> Unstrung, and quiver, where the arrows lay <br> Many and deadly. Her attendant maids <br> Brought also down a coffer, where were laid <br> Much brass and steel, provided by the king <br> For games like these. The glorious lady then, <br> In presence of the suitors, stood beside <br> The columns that upheld the stately roof. <br> She held a lustrous veil before her cheeks, <br> And, while on either side of her a maid <br> Stood modestly, bespake the suitors thus:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Hear, noble suitors! ye who throng these halls, <br> And eat and drink from day to day, while long <br> My husband has been gone; your sole excuse <br> For all this lawlessness the claim ye make <br> That I become a bride. Come then, for now <br> A contest is proposed. I bring to you <br> The mighty bow that great Ulysses bore. <br> Whoe’er among you he may be whose hand <br> Shall bend this bow, and send through these twelve rings <br> An arrow, him I follow hence, and leave <br> This beautiful abode of my young years, <br> With all its plenty⁠—though its memory, <br> I think, will haunt me even in my dreams.”</p>
<p class="verse">She spake, and bade the master of the swine, <br> The good Eumaeus, place the bow and rings <br> Of hoary steel before the suitor-train. <br> In tears he bore the bow and laid it down. <br> The herdsman also wept to see again <br> His master’s bow. Antinoüs called to both <br> With a loud voice, and chid them angrily:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Ye silly rustics, who can never see <br> Beyond the hour, why trouble with your tears <br> The lady who had grief enough besides <br> For her lost husband? Sit and share the feast <br> In silence, or go forth and leave the bow; <br> A difficult contest it will be for us, <br> Nor, as I think, will this fair bow be bent <br> With ease, since surely there is no man here <br> Such as Ulysses was. I saw him once, <br> While but a child, and still remember him.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake, yet in his secret heart believed <br> That he should bend the bow, and send a shaft <br> Through all the rings. And yet he was the first <br> To taste the steel⁠—an arrow from the hand <br> Of the great chief Ulysses⁠—whom he wronged <br> In his own palace, and to equal wrong <br> Encouraged others. Then Telemachus <br> Rose in his sacred might, and thus began:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Alas! it must be that Saturnian Jove <br> Has made me lose my wits. Wise as she is, <br> My mother promises to leave her home <br> And follow someone else, and yet I laugh, <br> And am delighted in my foolish heart. <br> Come then, since such a contest is proposed, <br> Ye suitors! and for such a woman too. <br> The like is not in all the lands of Greece, <br> Argos, Mycenae, or the hallowed shore <br> Of Pylos, or in Ithaca itself, <br> Or the dark mainland coast. Ye know it well; <br> Why should I praise my mother? Come then, all; <br> Let there be no excuses for delay, <br> Nor longer leave the bow untried, that we <br> May see the event. I too am moved to try; <br> And if I bend the bow, and send a shaft <br> Through all the rings, my gracious mother then <br> Will not, to my great grief, renounce her home, <br> And, following another, leave me here, <br> Although my prowess even now might win <br> The glorious prizes that my father won.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake and, rising, from his shoulders took <br> The purple cloak, and laid the trenchant sword <br> Aside; and first he placed the rings of steel <br> In order, opening for them in the ground <br> A long trench by a line, and stamping close <br> The earth around them. All admired the skill <br> With which he ranged them, never having seen <br> The game before. And then he took his place <br> Upon the threshold, and essayed the bow; <br> And thrice he made the attempt, and thrice gave o’er, <br> Yet hoping still to draw the cord, and send <br> An arrow through the rings. He would have drawn <br> The bow at the fourth trial, but a nod <br> Given by his father caused him to forbear, <br> Though eager for the attempt. And then again <br> The princely youth bespake the suitors thus:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Well, this is strange! I may hereafter prove <br> A craven and a weakling, or perchance <br> Am yet too young, and cannot trust my arm <br> To do me right against the man who first <br> Assaults me. Come then, ye whose strength excels <br> My own, and try the bow, and end the strife.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake, and setting down the bow to lean <br> Against the firm smooth panels of the wall, <br> And the swift shaft against the bow’s fair curve, <br> He took again his seat upon the throne <br> From which he rose. And then Eupeithes’ son, <br> Antinoüs, to the crowd of suitors said:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Rise one by one, my friends, from right to left. <br> Begin where he begins who pours the wine.”</p>
<p class="verse">So spake Antinoüs, and the rest approved. <br> Then rose Leiodes, son of Oenops, first. <br> He was their seer, and always had his seat <br> Beside the ample bowl. From deeds of wrong <br> He shrank with hatred, and was sore incensed <br> Against the suitors all. He took the bow <br> And shaft, and, going to the threshold, stood <br> And tried the bow, yet bent it not; it galled <br> His hands, for they were soft, and all unused <br> To such a task; and thus at length he spake:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“O friends, I bend it not; another hand <br> Must try. This bow, upon this very spot, <br> Will take from many a prince the breath of life. <br> And better were it thus to die, by far, <br> Than, living, fail of that intent for which <br> We haunt this place, and still from day to day <br> Assemble. There is many a one whose wish <br> And hope are strong to wed Penelope, <br> The consort of Ulysses; but so soon <br> As he shall see and try the hero’s bow <br> Let him with marriage presents seek to gain <br> Some other bride among the long-robed dames, <br> Achaia’s daughters. Let him leave the queen <br> To wed the suitor who shall bring to her <br> The richest gifts, and him whom fate appoints.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake, and setting down the bow to lean <br> Against the firm smooth panels of the wall, <br> And the swift shaft against the bow’s fair curve, <br> He took again his seat upon the throne <br> From which he rose. Antinoüs then took up <br> The word and answered, and reproached him thus:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“What words are these, Leiodes, that have passed <br> Thy lips? harsh words and fearful⁠—that this bow <br> Shall take from many princes here the breath <br> Of life, and all because thou hast no power <br> To bend it? Thy good mother bore thee not <br> To draw the bow and send the arrow forth, <br> But others of the noble suitor-train <br> Are here, by whom this bow shall yet be bent.”</p>
<p class="verse">Then to Melanthius, keeper of the goats, <br> Antinoüs gave this bidding. “Light a fire <br> With speed, Melanthius, in the palace here, <br> And place a seat before it. Lay a fleece <br> Upon the seat, and bring us from within <br> An ample roll of fat, that we young men <br> By warming and anointing may make soft <br> The bow, and draw the cord, and end the strife.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake; Melanthius kindled instantly <br> A glowing fire, and near it placed a seat, <br> And on the seat a fleece, and from within <br> Brought forth an ample roll of fat, with which <br> The young men, having warmed it, smeared the bow <br> And tried, but bent it not, too weak by far <br> For such a feat. Antinoüs kept aloof, <br> He and the godlike youth Eurymachus <br> Two princes who in might excelled the rest.</p>
<p class="verse">The herdsman of Ulysses meantime left <br> The palace, and with him the swineherd went, <br> And after them Ulysses. When they all <br> Were now without the gate and palace court, <br> Ulysses spake to them, and blandly said:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Herdsman and swineherd, shall I say to you <br> Somewhat, or shall I keep it back? My heart <br> Moves me to say it. Should Ulysses come, <br> Led by some god, and suddenly, what aid <br> Would he receive from you? Would ye take part <br> With him, or with the suitors? Frankly speak; <br> And tell me what your hearts would bid you do.”</p>
<p class="verse">Then answered thus the keeper of the herds: <br> “O Father Jove! wouldst thou but grant my wish, <br> And let some god conduct him hither, then <br> Shall it be seen what might is in these hands!”</p>
<p class="verse">So also did Eumaeus offer prayer <br> To all the deities, that speedily <br> The wise Ulysses might return; and when <br> The chief perceived in all its truth the thought <br> And purpose of their hearts, he spake and said:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Know, then, that I myself am he, at home <br> Again, returning in the twentieth year, <br> And after many sufferings, to the land <br> That saw my birth. I know that I am come <br> Welcome to you alone of all my train <br> Of servants, since I hear no others pray <br> For my return. Hear, then, what I engage <br> Shall be hereafter. If some god o’ercome <br> For me these arrogant suitors, I will give <br> To each of you a wife and lands, and build <br> For each a house near mine, and ye shall be <br> The friends and brothers of Telemachus <br> Thenceforth. And now, that ye may surely know <br> And trust me, I will show a token here⁠— <br> A scar which once the white tooth of a boar <br> Made, when long since, on the Parnassian mount, <br> I hunted with Autolycus’s sons.”</p>
<p class="verse">Thus having said, he drew from the broad scar <br> The covering rags; they looked and knew it well, <br> And wept, and round Ulysses threw their arms, <br> And kissed in that embrace the hero’s head <br> And shoulders, while Ulysses also kissed <br> Their heads and hands. The sun would have gone down <br> Upon their weeping, but for him. He said:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Cease now from tears, lest someone from the hall <br> Should see us, and report of us within. <br> Now let us enter, not in company⁠— <br> I first, and ye thereafter, one by one, <br> And let the sign be this: the others all⁠— <br> The haughty suitors⁠—will refuse to me <br> The bow and quiver. When thou bearest it, <br> My noble friend Eumaeus, through the halls, <br> Bring it and place it in my hands, and charge <br> The women to make fast the solid doors; <br> And then if any one of them should hear <br> A groan or other noise of men within, <br> Let her not issue forth, but silently <br> Pursue her task. Meantime be it thy care, <br> My good Philoetius, with a key to lock <br> The portals of the court and fix the chain.”</p>
<p class="verse">Thus having said, into that noble pile <br> He passed again, and took the seat from which <br> He lately rose, and afterward, in turn, <br> Entered the servants of the godlike chief.</p>
<p class="verse">Eurymachus was busy with the bow, <br> Turning and warming it before the blaze <br> On both its sides. He could not bend it thus. <br> There came a deep sigh from his boastful heart, <br> And greatly was he vexed, and sadly said:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Alas! great cause of grief indeed is here <br> For me and all. ’Tis not that I lament <br> So much the losing of the bride, although <br> That also vexes me⁠—there yet remain <br> Many fair ladies of the Achaian stock, <br> Both in the seagirt lands of Ithaca <br> And other regions⁠—yet if we be found <br> To fall in strength of arm so far below <br> The great Ulysses that we cannot bend <br> His bow, our sons will hear of it with shame.”</p>
<p class="verse">Eupeithes’ son, Antinoüs, answered thus: <br> “Not so, Eurymachus, as thou thyself <br> Shouldst know. This day is held a solemn feast <br> Of Phoebus by the people. Who would draw <br> The bow today? Nay, lay it by in peace, <br> And suffer all the rings to stand as now; <br> For no man, as I think, will dare to come <br> Into the palace of Laertes’ son <br> And take them hence. Let him who bears the cup <br> Begin to serve the wine, that, having poured <br> Part to the gods, we may lay down the bow, <br> And with the morning let Melanthius come⁠— <br> The goatherd⁠—bringing with him from the flock <br> The choicest goats, that we may burn the thighs, <br> An offering to the god of archery, <br> Apollo. Then will we again essay <br> The bow, and bring the contest to an end.”</p>
<p class="verse">So spake Antinoüs, and they all approved. <br> Then heralds came, and on the suitors’ hands <br> Poured water; youths filled up the cups with wine, <br> Beginning at the right, and gave to each <br> His share; and when they all had poured a part, <br> And each had drunk, the shrewd Ulysses thus <br> With artful speech bespake the suitor-train:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Hearken, ye suitors of the illustrious queen, <br> To what my heart is prompting me to say; <br> But chiefly to Eurymachus I make <br> My suit, and to Antinoüs, who so well <br> Hath counselled to lay by the bow and trust <br> The gods. Tomorrow Phoebus will bestow <br> The needed strength on whomsoe’er he will; <br> But let me take that polished bow, and try <br> Among you, whether still the power that dwelt <br> In these once pliant limbs abides in them, <br> Or whether happily it has passed from me <br> Amid my wanderings and a life of want.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake, and all were vehemently moved <br> With anger, for they feared that he would bend <br> The bow, and thus Antinoüs, railing, spake:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Thou worthless vagabond, without a spark <br> Of reason, art thou not content to sit <br> And banquet with the proudest, where no part <br> Of all the feast escapes thee, hearing all <br> That we are saying, which no other man, <br> Stranger and beggar, is allowed to hear! <br> This good wine makes thee foolish, as wine oft <br> Makes those who swallow it too greedily, <br> And drink not with due stint. It maddened once <br> Eurytion, the famed Centaur, in the halls <br> Of the large-souled Pirithoüs. He had come <br> Among the Lapithae, and when inflamed <br> With wine to madness, in those very halls <br> Did lawless deeds. The heroes were incensed. <br> They rushed upon him, dragged him through the porch <br> And out of doors, and there cut off his nose <br> And ears, and he departed, frenzied still, <br> And bearing in bewilderment of mind <br> His punishment, whence war arose between <br> Centaurs and men; yet surely he had brought <br> The evil on himself, when overcome <br> With wine. Such fearful mischief I foretell <br> Will light on thee, if thou shouldst bend this bow, <br> Nor canst thou hope for favor here among <br> The people. We will send thee speedily, <br> In a black galley, to King Echetus, <br> The enemy of human kind, from whom <br> Thou shalt find no escape. Drink, then, in peace <br> Thy wine, and seek no strife with younger men.”</p>
<p class="verse">Then spake the sage Penelope again: <br> “Truly, Antinoüs, it becomes thee not, <br> Nor is it just, to vex the stranger guests <br> Who seek the palace of Telemachus. <br> Dost thou, then, think that, should this stranger bend, <br> Proud as he is of his great strength of arm, <br> The mighty bow that once Ulysses bore, <br> He leads me hence a bride? No hope of that <br> Is in his heart, and let no one of you <br> Who banquet here allow a thought like that <br> To vex him; ’tis a thing that cannot be.”</p>
<p class="verse">Then to the queen, Eurymachus, the son <br> Of Polybus, replied: “We do not fear, <br> Sage daughter of Icarius, that this man <br> Will lead thee hence a bride; it cannot be. <br> We fear the speech of men and women both. <br> The very meanest of the Achaian race <br> Will say: ‘Degenerate men are these, who seek <br> To wed the consort of a glorious chief, <br> Not one of whom can draw the bow he bore; <br> And now there comes a wandering beggar-man, <br> Who draws the bow with ease, and sends a shaft <br> Through all the rings of steel.’ Thus will they speak, <br> And this will be to us a cause of shame!”</p>
<p class="verse">And then the sage Penelope rejoined: <br> “Eurymachus, it cannot be that those <br> Should earn the general praise who make the wealth <br> Of a most worthy man their spoil, and bring <br> Dishonor on his house. The stranger’s frame <br> Is powerful and well knit; he claims to be <br> Of noble parentage. Now let him take <br> The bow, and we will see the event; but this <br> I promise, and will make my promise good, <br> If he should bend it⁠—if Apollo give <br> To him that glory⁠—he shall have from me <br> A tunic and a cloak, fair garments both, <br> And a keen javelin, his defence against <br> Both dogs and men, a two-edged sword besides, <br> And sandals for his feet, and I engage <br> To send him whither he desires to go.”</p>
<p class="verse">Then spake discreet Telemachus again: <br> “Mother, in all Achaia there is none <br> Who has more power than I can claim, to grant <br> Or to deny the bow to whom I will. <br> No one of those who rule the rugged coast <br> Of Ithaca, or isles where Elis breeds <br> Her mares, may interpose to thwart my will, <br> If on the stranger I bestow the bow <br> To be his own, and bid him take it hence. <br> Withdraw, O queen, into thy bower; direct <br> Thy household tasks, the distaff and the web, <br> And bid thy maidens speed the work. The bow <br> Belongs to men, and most to me; for here, <br> Within these walls, the authority is mine.”</p>
<p class="verse">The queen, astonished, heard him and withdrew, <br> But kept her son’s wise sayings in her heart. <br> And then ascending to her bower, among <br> Her maids, she wept her well-beloved lord, <br> Ulysses, till the blue-eyed Pallas came, <br> And poured upon her lids the balm of sleep.</p>
<p class="verse">Meantime the worthy swineherd bore the bow <br> In hand, and all along the palace-halls <br> The suitor-crew were chiding him aloud, <br> And thus an insolent youth among them spake:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Thou awkward swineherd, whither goest thou <br> With the curved bow? Thy own fleet dogs which thou <br> Hast reared shall soon devour thee, far from men <br> And midst thy herds of swine, if we find grace <br> With Phoebus and the other deathless gods.”</p>
<p class="verse">Such were their words; the swineherd where he stood <br> Set down the bow in fear, for many a voice <br> Called to him in the hall. On the other side <br> Shouted Telemachus with threatening words:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Nay, father, carry on the bow, nor think <br> To stop at every man’s command; lest I, <br> Though younger than thyself, cast stones at thee, <br> And chase thee to the fields, for I in strength <br> Excel thee. Would that I excelled as far <br> In strength of arm the suitors in these halls, <br> Then would I roughly through the palace-gates <br> Drive many who are plotting mischief now.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake, and all with hearty laughter heard <br> His words, and for their sake allowed their wrath <br> Against the prince to cool. The swineherd went <br> Forward, along the hall, and, drawing near <br> The wise Ulysses, gave into his hands <br> The bow; and then he called the nurse aside, <br> Dame Eurycleia, and bespake her thus:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Sage Eurycleia, from Telemachus <br> I charge thee to make fast the solid doors, <br> And then, if any of the maids should hear <br> A groan or other noise of men within, <br> Let her not issue forth, but silently <br> Pursue the task in hand, and keep her place.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake, nor were his words in vain. The dame <br> Made fast the doors of that magnificent hall, <br> While silently Philoetius hastened forth <br> And locked the portals of the high-walled court. <br> A cable of the barque of Byblos lay <br> Beneath the portico⁠—it once had served <br> A galley⁠—and with this the herdsman tied <br> The portals, and, returning, took the seat <br> Whence he had risen, but ever kept his eye <br> Fixed on his lord. Ulysses, meantime, held <br> The bow, and, turning it, intently eyed <br> Side after side, and tried each part in turn, <br> For fear that worms, while he was far away, <br> Had pierced the horn. At this, a youth among <br> The suitors, turning to his neighbor, said:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Lo an inspector and a judge of bows! <br> Perhaps he has a bow like that at home, <br> Or else would make one like it. How he shifts <br> The thing with busy hands from side to side⁠— <br> The vagabond, well trained in knavish tricks!”</p>
<p class="verse">Then also said another insolent youth: <br> “May he in all things be as fortunate <br> As now, when he shall try to bend that bow!”</p>
<p class="verse">Such was their talk; but when the wary chief <br> Had poised and shrewdly scanned the mighty bow, <br> Then, as a singer, skilled to play the harp, <br> Stretches with ease on its new fastenings <br> A string, the twisted entrails of a sheep, <br> Made fast at either end, so easily <br> Ulysses bent that mighty bow. He took <br> And drew the cord with his right hand; it twanged <br> With a clear sound as when a swallow screams. <br> The suitors were dismayed, and all grew pale. <br> Jove in loud thunder gave a sign from heaven. <br> The much-enduring chief, Ulysses, heard <br> With joy the friendly omen, which the son <br> Of crafty Saturn sent him. He took up <br> A winged arrow, that before him lay <br> Upon a table, drawn; the others still <br> Were in the quiver’s womb; the Greeks were yet <br> To feel them. This he set with care against <br> The middle of the bow, and toward him drew <br> The cord and arrow-notch, just where he sat, <br> And, aiming opposite, let fly the shaft. <br> He missed no ring of all; from first to last <br> The brass-tipped arrow threaded every one. <br> Then to Telemachus Ulysses said:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Telemachus, the stranger sitting here <br> Hath not disgraced thee. I have neither missed <br> The rings, nor found it hard to bend the bow; <br> Nor has my manly strength decayed, as these <br> Who seek to bring me to contempt pretend; <br> And now the hour is come when we prepare <br> A supper for the Achaians, while the day <br> Yet lasts, and after supper the delights <br> Of song and harp, which nobly grace a feast.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake, and nodded to Telemachus, <br> His well-beloved son, who girded on <br> His trenchant sword, and took in hand his spear, <br> And, armed with glittering brass for battle, came <br> And took his station by his father’s seat.</p>
