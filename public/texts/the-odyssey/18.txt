<p class="lede">Ulysses insulted by the beggar Irus⁠—Amusement of the suitors, who encourage the quarrel⁠—Victory of Ulysses in the combat with Irus⁠—Manoeuvre of Penelope to obtain presents from the suitors, and its success⁠—Ulysses insulted by Eurymachus⁠—His reply⁠—The cupbearer struck down by a footstool flung at Ulysses by Eurymachus.</p>
<p class="verse">There came a common beggar, wont to ask <br> Alms through the town of Ithaca, well known <br> For greediness of stomach, gluttonous <br> And a wine-bibber, but of little strength <br> And courage, though he seemed of powerful mould. <br> Arnaeus was the name which at his birth <br> His mother gave him, but the young men called <br> The fellow Irus, for it was his wont <br> To go on errands, as a messenger, <br> When he was ordered. Coming now, he thought <br> To drive Ulysses out of his own house, <br> And railed at him, and said in winged words:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Hence with thee! leave the porch, old man, at once, <br> Lest thou be taken by the foot and dragged <br> Away from it. Dost thou not see how all <br> Around us nod, to bid me drag thee out? <br> I am ashamed to do it. Rise and go, <br> Else haply we may have a strife of blows.”</p>
<p class="verse">Ulysses, the sagacious, frowned and said: <br> “Wretch! there is nothing that I do or say <br> To harm thee aught. I do not envy thee <br> What others give thee, though the dole be large; <br> And ample is this threshold for us both. <br> Nor shouldst thou envy others, for thou seemst <br> A straggler like myself. The gods bestow <br> Wealth where they list. But do not challenge me <br> To blows, lest, aged as I am, thou rouse <br> My anger, and I make thy breast and lips <br> Hideous with blood. Tomorrow then will be <br> A quiet day for me, since thou, I trust, <br> In all the time to come, wilt never more <br> Enter the palace of Laertes’ son.”</p>
<p class="verse">The beggar Irus angrily rejoined: <br> “Ye gods! this glutton prattles volubly, <br> Like an old woman at the chimney-side. <br> Yet could I do him mischief, smiting him <br> On both his sides, and dashing from his cheeks <br> The teeth to earth, as men are wont to deal <br> With swine that eat the wheat. Now gird thyself, <br> Let these men see us fighting. How canst thou <br> Think to contend with one so young as I?”</p>
<p class="verse">Thus fiercely did they wrangle as they stood <br> Beside the polished threshold and before <br> The lofty gates. The stout Antinoüs heard, <br> And, laughing heartily, bespake the rest:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Here, friends, is what we never yet have had. <br> Behold the pleasant pastime which the gods <br> Provide for us. These men⁠—the stranger here, <br> And Irus⁠—quarrel, and will come to blows. <br> Let us stand by and bring the combat on.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake. All rose with laughter and came round <br> The ragged beggars, while Eupeithes’ son, <br> Antinoüs, in these words harangued the rest:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Ye noble suitors, hear me. At the fire <br> Already lie the paunches of two goats, <br> Preparing for our evening meal, and both <br> Are filled with fat and blood. Whoever shows <br> Himself the better man in this affray, <br> And conquers, he shall take the one of these <br> He chooses, and shall ever afterward <br> Feast at our table, and no man but he <br> Shall ever come among us asking alms.”</p>
<p class="verse">He ended. All approved his words, and thus <br> Ulysses, craftily dissembling, said:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“O friends, it is not well that one so old <br> As I, and broken by calamity, <br> Should fight a younger man; but hunger bids, <br> And I may be o’ercome by blows. But now <br> Swear all a solemn oath, that none of you, <br> To favor Irus, wickedly will raise <br> His mighty hand to smite me, and so aid <br> My adversary to my overthrow.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake; the suitor-train, assenting, took <br> The oath, and when they all were duly sworn, <br> The highborn prince Telemachus began:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“O stranger, if thy manly heart be moved <br> To drive him hence, fear no one else of all <br> The Achaians. Whosoever strikes at thee <br> Has many to contend with. I am here <br> The host. Antinoüs and Eurymachus, <br> Wise men and kings, agree with me in this.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake, and all approved. Ulysses drew <br> And girt his tatters round his waist and showed <br> His large and shapely thighs. Unclothed appeared <br> His full broad shoulders, and his manly breast <br> And sinewy arms. Minerva stood by him, <br> And with a mighty breadth of limb endued <br> The shepherd of the people. Earnestly <br> The suitors gazed, and wondered at the sight, <br> And each one, turning to his neighbor, said:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Irus, poor Irus, on himself has drawn <br> An evil fate, for what a sinewy thigh <br> His adversary shows beneath his rags!”</p>
<p class="verse">So talked they, while the heart of Irus sank <br> Within him; yet the attendants girding him <br> Forcibly drew him forward, sore afraid, <br> The muscles quivering over every limb. <br> And then Antinoüs spake, and chid him thus:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Now, boaster, thou deservest not to live, <br> Nay, nor to have been born, if thou dost fear <br> And quake at meeting one so old as he, <br> So broken with the hardships he has borne. <br> And now I tell thee what will yet be done, <br> Should he approve himself the better man, <br> And conquer. I will have thee sent on board <br> A galley to Epirus, and its king, <br> The foe of all men living, Echetus, <br> And he will pare away thy nose and ears <br> With the sharp steel, and, wrenching out the parts <br> Of shame, will cast them to be torn by dogs.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake, and Irus shook through all his frame <br> With greater terror, yet they dragged him on <br> Into the midst. Both champions lifted up <br> Their arms. The godlike, much-enduring man, <br> Ulysses, pondered whether so to strike <br> His adversary that the breath of life <br> Might leave him as he fell, or only smite <br> To stretch him on the earth. As thus he mused, <br> The lighter blow seemed wisest, lest the Greeks <br> Should know who dealt it. When the hands of both <br> Were thus uplifted, Irus gave a blow <br> On his right shoulder, while Ulysses smote <br> Irus beneath the ear, and broke the bone <br> Within, and brought the red blood from his mouth. <br> He fell amid the dust, and shrieked and gnashed <br> His teeth, and beat with jerking feet the ground. <br> The suitor-train threw up their hands and laughed <br> Till breathless, while Ulysses seized his feet <br> And drew him o’er the threshold to the court <br> And the porch doors, and there, beside the wall, <br> Set him to lean against it, gave a staff <br> Into his hands, and said in winged words:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Sit there, and scare away the dogs and swine, <br> But think not, wretched creature, to bear rule <br> Over the stranger and the beggar tribe, <br> Or worse than this may happen to thee yet.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake, and o’er his shoulders threw the scrip <br> That yawned with chinks, and by a twisted thong <br> Was fastened; then he turned to take his seat <br> Upon the threshold, while the suitor-train <br> Went back into the palace with gay shouts <br> Of laughter, and bespake him blandly thus:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Stranger, may Jove and all the other gods <br> Grant thee what thou desirest, and whate’er <br> Is pleasant to thee! Thou hast put an end <br> To this importunate beggar’s rounds among <br> The people. We shall send him off at once <br> Into Epirus, and to Echetus, <br> Its king, the foe of every living man.”</p>
<p class="verse">So talked the suitors, and the omen made <br> Ulysses glad. Meantime Antinoüs placed <br> The mighty paunch before the victor, filled <br> With blood and fat, and from the canister <br> Amphinomus brought forth two loaves, and raised <br> A golden cup and drank to him, and said:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Hail, guest and father! happy be thy days <br> Henceforth, though dark with many sorrows now!”</p>
<p class="verse">Ulysses, the sagacious, answered thus: <br> “Amphinomus, thou seemest most discreet, <br> And such thy father is, of whom I hear <br> A worshipful report, the good and rich <br> Dulichian Nisus. Thou, as I am told, <br> Art son to him, and thou art seemingly <br> A man of pertinent speech. I therefore say <br> To thee, and bid thee hear and mark me well, <br> No being whom earth nourishes to breathe <br> Her air and move upon her face is more <br> The sport of circumstance than man. For while <br> The gods give health, and he is strong of limb, <br> He thinks no evil in the coming days <br> Will overtake him. When the blessed gods <br> Visit him with afflictions, these he bears <br> Impatiently and with a fretful mind. <br> Such is the mood of man, while yet he dwells <br> On earth; it changes as the All-Father gives <br> The sunshine or withholds it. I was once <br> Deemed fortunate among my fellow-men, <br> And many things that were unjust I did; <br> For in my strength and in my father’s power, <br> And valor of my brothers, I had put <br> My trust. Let no man, therefore, dare to be <br> Unjust in aught, but tranquilly enjoy <br> Whatever good the gods vouchsafe to give. <br> Yet are these suitors guilty of foul wrong, <br> Wasting the substance and dishonoring <br> The wife of one who will not, as I deem, <br> Remain long distant from his friends and home, <br> But is already near. O, may some god <br> Remove thee from this danger to thy home! <br> Nor mayst thou meet him when he shall return <br> To his own land. For when he comes once more <br> Beneath this roof, and finds the suitors here, <br> Not without bloodshed will their parting be.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake, and, pouring out a part, he drank <br> The wine, and gave the goblet to the prince, <br> Who crossed the hall, and sorrowfully shook <br> His head, for now already did his heart <br> Forebode the coming evil. Not by this <br> Did he escape his death. Minerva laid <br> A snare for him, that he might fall beneath, <br> The strong arm of Telemachus. He went <br> And took the seat from which he lately rose.</p>
<p class="verse">Then blue-eyed Pallas moved Penelope, <br> Sage daughter of Icarius, to appear <br> Before the suitors, that their base intent <br> Might be more fully seen, and she might win <br> More honor from her husband and her son. <br> Wherefore she forced a laugh, and thus began:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Eurynomè, I would at length appear, <br> Though not till now, before the suitor-train, <br> Detested as they are. I there would speak <br> A word of timely warning to my son, <br> And give him counsel not to trust himself <br> Too much among the suitors, who are fair <br> In speech, but mean him foully in their hearts.”</p>
<p class="verse">Eurynomè, the household matron, said: <br> “Assuredly, my child, thou speakest well. <br> Go now, and warn thy son, and keep back naught. <br> First bathe, and, ere thou go, anoint thy cheeks, <br> Nor show them stained with tears. It is not well <br> To sorrow without end. For now thy son <br> Is grown, and thou beholdest him at length <br> What thou didst pray the gods, when he was born, <br> That he might yet become, a bearded man.”</p>
<p class="verse">And then the sage Penelope rejoined: <br> “Though anxious for my sake, persuade me not, <br> Eurynomè, to bathe, nor to anoint <br> My cheeks with oil. The gods inhabiting <br> Olympus took away their comeliness <br> When in his roomy ships my husband sailed; <br> But bid Antinoe come, and call with her <br> Hippodameïa, that they both may stand <br> Beside me in the hall. I will not go <br> Alone among the men, for very shame.”</p>
<p class="verse">She spake, the aged dame went forth to bear <br> The message, and to bring the women back. <br> While blue-eyed Pallas had yet other cares, <br> She brought a balmy sleep, and shed it o’er <br> The daughter of Icarius, as she lay <br> Reclined upon her couch, her limbs relaxed <br> In rest. The glorious goddess gave a dower <br> Of heavenly graces, that the Achaian chiefs <br> Might look on her amazed. She lighted up <br> Her fair face with a beauty all divine, <br> Such as the queenly Cytherea wears <br> When in the mazes of the dance she joins <br> The Graces. Then she made her to the sight <br> Of loftier stature and of statelier size, <br> And fairer than the ivory newly carved. <br> This having done, the gracious power withdrew, <br> While from the palace came the white-armed maids, <br> And prattled as they came. The balmy sleep <br> Forsook their mistress at the sound. She passed <br> Her hands across her cheeks, and thus she spake:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“ ’Twas a sweet sleep that, in my wretchedness, <br> Wrapped me just now. Would that, this very hour, <br> The chaste Diana by so soft a death <br> Might end me, that my days might be no more <br> Consumed in sorrow for a husband lost, <br> Of peerless worth, the noblest of the Greeks.”</p>
<p class="verse">She spake, and from the royal bower went down, <br> Yet not alone; two maidens went with her. <br> And when that most august of womankind <br> Drew near the suitors, at the door she stopped <br> Of that magnificent hall, and o’er her cheeks <br> Let fall the lustrous veil, while on each side <br> A modest maiden stood. The suitors all <br> Felt their knees tremble, and were sick with love, <br> And all desired her. Then the queen bespake <br> Telemachus, her well-beloved son:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Telemachus, thy judgment is not firm, <br> Nor dost thou think aright. While yet a boy <br> Thy thought was wiser. Now that thou art grown, <br> And on the verge of manhood, so that one <br> Who comes from far and sees thy noble part <br> And stature well may say thou art the son <br> Of a most fortunate father, yet to think <br> And judge discreetly thou art not as then, <br> For what a deed is this which has been done <br> Even here! Thou hast allowed a stranger guest <br> To be assaulted rudely. How is this? <br> If one who sits a guest beneath our roof <br> Be outraged thus, be sure it brings to thee <br> Great shame and rank dishonor among men.”</p>
<p class="verse">To this discreet Telemachus replied: <br> “Mother, I cannot take it ill that thou <br> Shouldst be offended. But of many things <br> I have a clear discernment, and can weigh <br> The good and bad. I was till now a child, <br> Yet even now I cannot always see <br> The wiser course. These men bewilder me, <br> As, sitting side by side, they lay their plots <br> Against me, and I have no helper here. <br> When Irus and the stranger fought, the strife <br> Had no such issue as the suitors wished. <br> The stranger conquered. Would to Father Jove, <br> To Pallas and Apollo, that the crew <br> Of suitors here might sit with nodding heads <br> Struck down upon the spot, within these halls <br> Or in the courts, and all with powerless limbs, <br> As Irus sits beside the gate and nods, <br> Like one o’ercome with wine, nor can he stand <br> Upon his feet, nor go to where he dwells, <br> If home he has, so feeble are his limbs.”</p>
<p class="verse">So talked the twain awhile; then interposed <br> Eurymachus, and thus bespake the queen:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Sage daughter of Icarius! if all those <br> Who in Iäsian Argos have their homes <br> Should once behold thee, a still larger crowd <br> Of suitors would tomorrow come and feast <br> Within thy halls, so much dost thou excel <br> In mind and form and face all womankind.”</p>
<p class="verse">To this the sage Penelope replied: <br> “Eurymachus, the immortals took away <br> Such grace of form and face as once was mine, <br> What time the sons of Argos sailed for Troy, <br> And with them went Ulysses, my espoused. <br> Should he return, and take again in charge <br> My household, greater would my glory be, <br> And prized more highly. I am wretched now, <br> Such woes the gods have heaped upon my head. <br> He, when he left his native island, grasped <br> My right hand at the wrist, and said to me: <br> ‘Think not, dear wife, that all the well-armed Greeks <br> Will come back safe from Troy. The Trojan men, <br> They say, are brave in war, expert to cast <br> The spear and wing the arrow, skilled to rein <br> The rapid steeds by which the bloody strife <br> Of battlefields is hurried to its close; <br> And therefore whether God will bring me back, <br> Or I shall fall in Troy, I cannot know. <br> Take charge of all things here. I leave with thee <br> My father and my mother in these halls. <br> Be kind to them as now, nay, more than now, <br> Since I shall not be here. When thou shalt see <br> My son a bearded man, take to thyself <br> A husband, whom thou wilt, and leave thy house.’ <br> Such were his words, and they have been fulfilled. <br> The night will come in which I must endure <br> This hateful marriage, wretched that I am, <br> To whom the will of Jupiter forbids <br> All consolation, and this bitter thought <br> Weighs evermore upon my heart and soul. <br> The custom was not thus in other times; <br> When suitors wooed a noble wife, the child <br> Of some rich house, contending for her smile, <br> They came with beeves and fatling sheep to feast <br> The damsel’s friends, and gave munificent gifts, <br> But wasted not the wealth that was not theirs.”</p>
<p class="verse">She spake, Ulysses was rejoiced to see <br> That thus she sought to draw from each a gift, <br> With fair and artful words. Yet were his thoughts <br> Intent on other plans. Eupeithes’ son, <br> Antinoüs, thus made answer to the queen:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Sage daughter of Icarius, only deign <br> To take the gifts which any of the Greeks <br> Will bring⁠—nor is it gracious to reject <br> A present⁠—yet be sure we go not hence, <br> To our estates nor elsewhere, till thou make <br> A bridegroom of the best Achaian here.”</p>
<p class="verse">So spake Antinoüs. All approved his words, <br> And each sent forth a herald for his gift. <br> The herald of Antinoüs brought to him <br> A robe of many colors, beautiful <br> And ample, with twelve golden clasps, which each <br> Had its well-fitted eye. Eurymachus <br> Received a golden necklace, richly wrought, <br> And set with amber beads, that glowed as if <br> With sunshine. To Eurydamas there came <br> A pair of earrings, each a triple gem, <br> Daintily fashioned and of exquisite grace. <br> Two servants bore them. From Pisander’s house⁠— <br> Son of the Prince Polyctor⁠—there was brought <br> A collar of rare beauty. Thus did each <br> Bestow a different yet becoming gift. <br> And then that most august of women went <br> Back to the upper chambers with her maids, <br> Who bore the sumptuous presents, while below <br> The suitors turned them to the dance and song, <br> Amused till evening came. Its darkness stole <br> Over their pastime. Then they brought and placed <br> Three hearths to light the palace, heaping them <br> With wood, well dried and hard and newly cleft. <br> With this they mingled flaming brands. The maids <br> Of the great sufferer, Ulysses, fed <br> The fire by turns. To them the hero spake:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Ye maidens of a sovereign absent long, <br> Withdraw to where your highborn mistress sits; <br> There turn the spindle, seeking to amuse <br> Her lonely hours; there comb with your own hands <br> The fleece, and I will see that these have light. <br> Even though they linger till the Morn is here <br> In her bright car, they cannot overcome <br> My patience. I am practised to endure.”</p>
<p class="verse">So spake he, and the maidens, as they heard, <br> Cast at each other meaning looks, and laughed, <br> And one Melantho, of the rosy cheeks, <br> Railed at him impudently. She was born <br> To Dolius, but Penelope had reared <br> The damsel as a daughter of her own, <br> And given her, for her pleasure, many things; <br> Yet for the sorrows of Penelope <br> Melantho little cared. Eurymachus <br> Had made the girl his paramour. She spake, <br> And chid Ulysses with unmannerly words:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Outlandish wretch! thou must be one whose brain <br> Is turned, since thou wilt neither go to sleep <br> Within a smithy, nor in any place <br> Of public shelter, but wilt stay and prate <br> Among this company with no restraint <br> Or reverence. Either wine has stolen away <br> Thy senses, or thy natural mood, perchance, <br> Prompts thee to chatter idly. Art thou proud <br> Of conquering Irus, that poor vagabond? <br> Beware lest someone of robuster arms <br> Than Irus seize and thrust thee out of doors <br> With a bruised head and face begrimed with blood.”</p>
<p class="verse">The sage Ulysses frowned on her and said: <br> “Impudent one, Telemachus shall hear <br> From me the saucy words which thou hast said, <br> And he will come and hew thee limb from limb.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake; the damsels, frightened at his words, <br> Fled through the hall, and shook in every limb <br> With terror, lest his threat should be fulfilled. <br> He meantime stood beside the kindled hearths <br> And fed the flames, and, looking on the crowd <br> Of suitors, brooded in his secret heart <br> O’er plans that would not fail to be fulfilled.</p>
<p class="verse">But Pallas suffered not the suitors yet <br> To cease from railing speeches, all the more <br> To wound the spirit of Laertes’ son. <br> Eurymachus, the son of Polybus, <br> Began to scoff at him, and thus he spake <br> To wake the ready laughter of the rest:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Hear me, ye suitors of the illustrious queen. <br> I speak the thought that comes into my mind. <br> Led by some god, no doubt, this man has come <br> Into the palace; for the light we have <br> Of torches seems to issue from the crown <br> Of his bald pate, a head without a hair.”</p>
<p class="verse">So said Eurymachus, and then bespake <br> Ulysses, the destroyer of walled towns:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Stranger, if I accept thee, wilt thou serve <br> Upon the distant parts of my estate? <br> There shalt thou have fair wages, and shalt bring <br> The stones in heaps together, and shalt plant <br> Tall trees, and I will feed thee through the year, <br> And give thee clothes, and sandals for thy feet. <br> But thou art used, no doubt, to idle ways, <br> And never dost thou work with willing hands, <br> But dost prefer to roam the town and beg, <br> Purveying for thy gluttonous appetite.”</p>
<p class="verse">Ulysses, the sagacious, answered thus:⁠— <br> “Eurymachus, if we were matched in work <br> Against each other in the time of spring <br> When days are long, and both were mowing grass, <br> And I had a curved scythe in hand and thou <br> Another, that we might keep up the strife <br> Till nightfall, fasting, mid the abundant grass; <br> Or if there were a yoke of steers to drive, <br> The sturdiest of their kind, sleek, large, well fed, <br> Of equal age, and equal strength to bear <br> The labor, and both strong, and if the field <br> Were of four acres, with a soil through which <br> The plough could cleave its way⁠—then shouldst thou see <br> How evenly my furrow would be turned. <br> Or should the son of Saturn send today <br> War from abroad, and I had but a shield, <br> Two spears, and, fitted to my brows, a helm <br> Of brass, thou wouldst behold me pressing on <br> Among the foremost warriors, and would see <br> No cause to rail at my keen appetite. <br> But arrogantly thou dost bear thyself, <br> And pitilessly; thou in thine own eyes <br> Art great and mighty, since thou dost consort <br> With few, and those are not the best of men. <br> Yet should Ulysses come to his own land, <br> These gates that seem so wide would suddenly <br> Become too narrow for thee in thy flight.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake. Eurymachus grew yet more wroth, <br> And frowned on him, and said in winged words:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Wretch! I shall do thee mischief. Thou art bold, <br> And babblest unabashed among us all. <br> The wine, perhaps, is in thy foolish head, <br> Or thou art always thus, and ever prone <br> To prattle impudently. Art thou proud <br> Of conquering Irus, that poor vagabond?”</p>
<p class="verse">Thus having said, he brandished in the air <br> A footstool; but Ulysses, to escape <br> The anger of Eurymachus, sat down <br> Before the knees of the Dulichian prince, <br> Amphinomus. The footstool flew, and struck <br> On the right arm the cupbearer. Down fell <br> The beaker ringing; he who bore it lay <br> Stretched in the dust. Then in those shadowy halls <br> The suitors rose in tumult. One of them <br> Looked at another by his side, and said:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Would that this vagabond had met his death <br> Ere he came hither. This confusion, then, <br> Had never been. ’Tis for a beggar’s sake <br> We wrangle, and the feast will henceforth give <br> No pleasure; we shall go from bad to worse.”</p>
<p class="verse">Then rose in majesty Telemachus, <br> And said: “Ye are not in your senses sure, <br> Unhappy men, who cannot eat and drink <br> In peace. Some deity, no doubt, has moved <br> Your minds to frenzy. Now, when each of you <br> Has feasted well, let each withdraw to sleep, <br> Just when he will. I drive no man away.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake; the suitors heard, and bit their lips, <br> And wondered at Telemachus, who spake <br> So resolutely. Then Amphinomus, <br> The son of Nisus Aretiades, <br> Stood forth, harangued the suitor-crowd, and said:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“O friends! let no one here with carping words <br> Seek to deny what is so justly said, <br> Nor yet molest the stranger, nor do harm <br> To any of the servants in the halls <br> Of the great chief Ulysses. Now let him <br> Who brings the guests their wine begin and fill <br> The cups, that, pouring to the gods their part, <br> We may withdraw to sleep. The stranger here <br> Leave me within the palace, and in charge <br> Of him to whom he came, Telemachus.”</p>
<p class="verse">He ended. All were pleased, and Mutlus then, <br> Hero and herald from Dulichium’s coast, <br> And follower of the prince Amphinomus, <br> Mingled a jar of wine, and went to each, <br> Dispensing it. They to the blessed gods <br> Poured first a part, and then they drank themselves <br> The generous juice. And when the wine was poured, <br> And they had drunk what each desired, they went <br> Homeward to slumber, each in his abode.</p>
