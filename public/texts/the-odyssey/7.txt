<p class="lede">Return of Nausicaä to the city, followed by Ulysses⁠—Palace and garden of Alcinoüs⁠—Reception of Ulysses by the queen and her husband⁠—Narrative given by Ulysses of his voyage and shipwreck.</p>
<p class="verse">So prayed Ulysses the great sufferer. <br> The strong mules bore the damsel toward the town, <br> And when she reached her father’s stately halls <br> She stopped beneath the porch. Her brothers came <br> Around her, like in aspect to the gods, <br> And loosed the mules, and bore the garments in. <br> She sought her chamber, where an aged dame <br> Attendant there, an Epirote, and named <br> Eurymedusa, lighted her a fire. <br> She by the well-oared galleys had been brought <br> Beforetime from Epirus, and was given <br> To king Alcinoüs, ruler over all <br> Phaeacia’s sons, who hearkened to his voice <br> As if he were a god. ’Twas she who reared <br> White-armed Nausicaä in the royal halls, <br> Tended her hearth, and dressed her evening meal.</p>
<p class="verse">Now rose Ulysses up, and townward turned <br> His steps, while friendly Pallas wrapt his way <br> In darkness, lest someone among the sons <br> Of the Phaeacians with unmannerly words <br> Might call to him or ask him who he was. <br> And just as he was entering that fair town <br> The blue-eyed Pallas met him, in the form <br> Of a young virgin with an urn. She stood <br> Before him, and Ulysses thus inquired:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Wilt thou, my daughter, guide me to the house <br> Where dwells Alcinoüs, he who rules this land? <br> I am a stranger, who have come from far <br> After long hardships, and of all who dwell <br> Within this realm I know not even one.”</p>
<p class="verse">Pallas, the blue-eyed goddess, thus replied:⁠— <br> “Father and stranger, I will show the house; <br> The dwelling of my own good father stands <br> Close by it. Follow silently, I pray, <br> And I will lead. Look not on any man <br> Nor ask a question; for the people here <br> Affect not strangers, nor do oft receive <br> With kindly welcome him who comes from far. <br> They trust in their swift barques, which to and fro, <br> By Neptune’s favor, cross the mighty deep. <br> Their galleys have the speed of wings or thought.”</p>
<p class="verse">Thus Pallas spake, and quickly led the way. <br> He followed in her steps. They saw him not⁠— <br> Those trained Phaeacian seamen⁠—for the power <br> That led him, Pallas of the amber hair, <br> Forbade the sight, and threw a friendly veil <br> Of darkness over him. Ulysses saw, <br> Wondering, the haven and the gallant ships, <br> The marketplace where heroes thronged, the walls <br> Long, lofty, and beset with palisades, <br> A marvel to the sight. But when they came <br> To the king’s stately palace, thus began <br> The blue-eyed goddess, speaking to the chief:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Father and stranger, here thou seest the house <br> Which thou hast bid me show thee. Thou wilt find <br> The princes, nurslings of the gods, within, <br> Royally feasting. Enter, and fear not; <br> The bold man ever is the better man, <br> Although he come from far. Thou first of all <br> Wilt see the queen. Aretè is the name <br> The people give her. She is of a stock <br> The very same from which Alcinoüs <br> The king derives his lineage. For long since <br> Nausithoüs, its founder, was brought forth <br> To Neptune, the great Shaker of the shores, <br> By Peribaea, fairest of her sex, <br> And youngest daughter of Eurymedon, <br> The large of soul, who ruled the arrogant brood <br> Of giants, and beheld that guilty race <br> Cut off, and perished by a fate like theirs. <br> Her Neptune wooed; she bore to him a son, <br> Large-souled Nausithoüs, whom Phaeacia owned <br> Its sovereign. To Nausithoüs were born <br> Rhexenor and Alcinoüs. He who bears <br> The silver bow, Apollo, smote to death <br> Rhexenor, newly wedded, in his home. <br> He left no son, and but one daughter, named <br> Aretè; her Alcinoüs made his wife, <br> And honored her as nowhere else on earth <br> Is any woman honored who bears charge <br> Over a husband’s household. From their hearts <br> Her children pay her reverence, and the king <br> And all the people, for they look on her <br> As if she were a goddess. When she goes <br> Abroad into the streets, all welcome her <br> With acclamations. Never does she fail <br> In wise discernment, but decides disputes <br> Kindly and justly between man and man. <br> And if thou gain her favor, there is hope <br> That thou mayst see thy friends once more, and stand <br> In thy tall palace on thy native soil.”</p>
<p class="verse">The blue-eyed Pallas, having spoken thus, <br> Departed o’er the barren deep. She left <br> The pleasant isle of Scheria, and repaired <br> To Marathon and to the spacious streets <br> Of Athens, entering there the massive halls <br> Where dwelt Erectheus, while Ulysses toward <br> The gorgeous palace of Alcinoüs turned <br> His steps, yet stopped and pondered ere he crossed <br> The threshold. For on every side beneath <br> The lofty roof of that magnanimous king <br> A glory shone as of the sun or moon. <br> There from the threshold, on each side, were walls <br> Of brass that led towards the inner rooms, <br> With blue steel cornices. The doors within <br> The massive building were of gold, and posts <br> Of silver on the brazen threshold stood, <br> And silver was the lintel, and above <br> Its architrave was gold; and on each side <br> Stood gold and silver mastiffs, the rare work <br> Of Vulcan’s practised skill, placed there to guard <br> The house of great Alcinoüs, and endowed <br> With deathless life, that knows no touch of age. <br> Along the walls within, on either side, <br> And from the threshold to the inner rooms, <br> Were firmly planted thrones on which were laid <br> Delicate mantles, woven by the hands <br> Of women. The Phaeacian princes here <br> Were seated; here they ate and drank, and held <br> Perpetual banquet. Slender forms of boys <br> In gold upon the shapely altars stood, <br> With blazing torches in their hands to light <br> At eve the palace guests; while fifty maids <br> Waited within the halls, where some in querns <br> Ground small the yellow grain; some wove the web <br> Or twirled the spindle, sitting, with a quick <br> Light motion, like the aspen’s glancing leaves. <br> The well-wrought tissues glistened as with oil. <br> As far as the Phaeacian race excel <br> In guiding their swift galleys o’er the deep, <br> So far the women in their woven work <br> Surpass all others. Pallas gives them skill <br> In handiwork and beautiful design. <br> Without the palace-court, and near the gate, <br> A spacious garden of four acres lay. <br> A hedge enclosed it round, and lofty trees <br> Flourished in generous growth within⁠—the pear <br> And the pomegranate, and the apple-tree <br> With its fair fruitage, and the luscious fig <br> And olive always green. The fruit they bear <br> Falls not, nor ever fails in winter time <br> Nor summer, but is yielded all the year. <br> The ever-blowing west-wind causes some <br> To swell and some to ripen; pear succeeds <br> To pear; to apple apple, grape to grape, <br> Fig ripens after fig. A fruitful field <br> Of vines was planted near; in part it lay <br> Open and basking in the sun, which dried <br> The soil, and here men gathered in the grapes, <br> And there they trod the winepress. Farther on <br> Were grapes unripened yet, which just had cast <br> The flower, and others still which just began <br> To redden. At the garden’s furthest bound <br> Were beds of many plants that all the year <br> Bore flowers. There gushed two fountains: one of them <br> Ran wandering through the field; the other flowed <br> Beneath the threshold to the palace-court, <br> And all the people filled their vessels there. <br> Such were the blessings which the gracious gods <br> Bestowed on King Alcinoüs and his house.</p>
<p class="verse">Ulysses, the great sufferer, standing there, <br> Admired the sight; and when he had beheld <br> The whole in silent wonderment, he crossed <br> The threshold quickly, entering the hall <br> Where the Phaeacian peers and princes poured <br> Wine from their goblets to the sleepless one, <br> The Argus-queller, to whose deity <br> They made the last libations when they thought <br> Of slumber. The great sufferer, concealed <br> In a thick mist, which Pallas raised and cast <br> Around him, hastened through the hall and came <br> Close to Aretè and Alcinoüs, <br> The royal pair. Then did Ulysses clasp <br> Aretè’s knees, when suddenly the cloud <br> Raised by the goddess vanished. All within <br> The palace were struck mute as they beheld <br> The man before them. Thus Ulysses prayed:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Aretè, daughter of the godlike chief <br> Rhexenor! to thy husband I am come <br> And to thy knees, from many hardships borne, <br> And to these guests, to whom may the good gods <br> Grant to live happily, and to hand down, <br> Each one to his own children, in his home, <br> The wealth and honors which the people’s love <br> Bestowed upon him. Grant me, I entreat, <br> An escort, that I may behold again <br> And soon my own dear country. I have passed <br> Long years in sorrow, far from all I love.”</p>
<p class="verse">He ended, and sat down upon the hearth <br> Among the ashes, near the fire, and all <br> Were silent utterly. At length outspake <br> Echeneus, oldest and most eloquent chief <br> Of the Phaeacians; large his knowledge was <br> Of things long past. With generous intent, <br> And speaking to the assembly, he began:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Alcinoüs, this is not a seemly sight⁠— <br> A stranger sitting on the hearth among <br> The cinders. All the others here await <br> Thy order, and move not. I pray thee, raise <br> The stranger up, and seat him on a throne <br> Studded with silver. Be thy heralds called, <br> And bid them mingle wine, which we may pour <br> To Jove, the god of thunders, who attends <br> And honors every suppliant. Let the dame <br> Who oversees the palace feast provide <br> Our guest a banquet from the stores within.”</p>
<p class="verse">This when the reverend king Alcinoüs heard, <br> Forthwith he took Ulysses by the hand⁠— <br> That man of wise devices⁠—raised him up <br> And seated him upon a shining throne, <br> From which he bade Laodamas arise, <br> His manly son, whose seat was next to his.</p>
<p class="verse">“Now mingle wine, Protonoüs, in a vase, <br> For all within the palace, to be poured <br> To Jove, the god of thunders, who attends <br> And honors every suppliant.” As he spake <br> Protonoüs mingled the delicious wines, <br> And passed from right to left, distributing <br> The cups to all; and when they all had poured <br> A part to Jove, and all had drunk their fill, <br> Alcinoüs took the word, and thus he said:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Princes and chiefs of the Phaeacians, hear, <br> I speak as my heart bids me. Since the feast <br> Is over, take your rest within your homes. <br> Tomorrow shall the Senators be called <br> In larger concourse. We will pay our guest <br> Due honor in the palace, worshipping <br> The gods with solemn sacrifice. And then <br> Will we bethink us how to send him home, <br> That with no hindrance and no hardship borne <br> Under our escort he may come again <br> Gladly and quickly to his native land, <br> Though far away it lie, and that no wrong <br> Or loss may happen to him ere he set <br> Foot on its soil; and there must he endure <br> Whatever, when his mother brought him forth, <br> Fate and the unrelenting Sisters spun <br> For the newborn. But should he prove to be <br> One of the immortals who has come from heaven, <br> Then have the gods a different design. <br> For hitherto the gods have shown themselves <br> Visibly at our solemn hecatombs, <br> And sat with us, and feasted like ourselves, <br> And when the traveller meets with them alone, <br> They never hide themselves; for we to them <br> Are near of kin, as near as is the race <br> Of Cyclops and the savage giant brood.”</p>
<p class="verse">Ulysses the sagacious answered him:⁠— <br> “Nay, think not so, Alcinoüs. I am not <br> In form or aspect as the immortals are, <br> Whose habitation is the ample heaven. <br> But I am like whomever thou mayst know, <br> Among mankind, inured to suffering; <br> To them shouldst thou compare me. I could tell <br> Of bitterer sorrows yet, which I have borne; <br> Such was the pleasure of the gods. But now <br> Leave me, whatever have my hardships been, <br> To take the meal before me. Naught exceeds <br> The impatient stomach’s importunity <br> When even the afflicted and the sorrowful <br> Are forced to heed its call. So even now, <br> Midst all the sorrow that is in my heart, <br> It bids me eat and drink, and put aside <br> The thought of my misfortunes till itself <br> Be satiate. But, ye princes, with the dawn <br> Provide for me, in my calamity, <br> The means to reach again my native land. <br> For, after all my hardships, I would die <br> Willingly, could I look on my estates, <br> My servants, and my lofty halls once more.”</p>
<p class="verse">He ended; they approved his words, and bade <br> Set forward on his homeward way the guest <br> Who spake so wisely. When they all had made <br> Libations and had drunk, they each withdrew <br> To sleep at home, and left the noble chief <br> Ulysses in the palace, where with him <br> Aretè and her godlike husband sat, <br> While from the feast the maidens bore away <br> The chargers. The white-armed Aretè then <br> Began to speak; for when she cast her eyes <br> On the fair garments which Ulysses wore, <br> She knew the mantle and the tunic well, <br> Wrought by herself and her attendant maids, <br> And thus with winged words bespake the chief:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Stranger, I first must ask thee who thou art, <br> And of what race of men. From whom hast thou <br> Received those garments? Sure thou dost not say <br> That thou art come from wandering o’er the sea.”</p>
<p class="verse">Ulysses, the sagacious, answered thus:⁠— <br> “ ’Twere hard, O sovereign lady, to relate <br> In order all my sufferings, for the gods <br> Of heaven have made them many; yet will I <br> Tell all thou askest of me, and obey <br> Thy bidding. Far within the ocean lies <br> An island named Ogygia, where abides <br> Calypso, artful goddess, with bright locks, <br> Daughter of Atlas, and of dreaded power. <br> No god consorts with her, nor anyone <br> Of mortal birth. But me in my distress <br> Some god conveyed alone to her abode, <br> When, launching his white lightning, Jupiter <br> Had cloven in the midst of the black sea <br> My galley. There my gallant comrades all <br> Perished, but I in both my arms held fast <br> The keel of my good ship, and floated on <br> Nine days till, on the tenth, in the dark night, <br> The gods had brought me to Ogygia’s isle, <br> Where dwells Calypso of the radiant hair <br> And dreaded might, who kindly welcomed me, <br> And cherished me, and would have made my life <br> Immortal, and beyond the power of age <br> In all the coming time. And there I wore <br> Seven years away, still moistening with my tears <br> The ambrosial raiment which the goddess gave. <br> But when the eighth year had begun its round <br> She counselled my departure, whether Jove <br> Had so required, or she herself had changed <br> Her purpose. On a raft made strong with clamps <br> She placed me, sent on board an ample store <br> Of bread and pleasant wine, and made me put <br> Ambrosial garments on, and gave a soft <br> And favorable wind. For seventeen days <br> I held my steady course across the deep, <br> And on the eighteenth day the shadowy heights <br> Of your own isle appeared, and then my heart, <br> Ill-fated as I was, rejoiced. Yet still <br> Was I to struggle with calamities <br> Sent by earthshaking Neptune, who called up <br> The winds against me, and withstood my way, <br> And stirred the boundless ocean to its depths. <br> Nor did the billows surfer me to keep <br> My place, but swept me, groaning, from the raft, <br> Whose planks they scattered. Still I labored through <br> The billowy depth, and swam, till wind and wave <br> Drove me against your coast. As there I sought <br> To land, I found the surges hurrying me <br> Against huge rocks that lined the frightful shore, <br> But, turning back, I swam again and reached <br> A river and the landing-place I wished. <br> Smooth, without rocks, and sheltered from the wind. <br> I swooned, but soon revived. Ambrosial night <br> Came on. I left the Jove-descended stream <br> And slept among the thickets, drawing round <br> My limbs the withered leaves, while on my lids <br> A deity poured bounteously the balm <br> Of slumber. All night long, among the leaves, <br> I slept, with all that sorrow in my heart, <br> Till morn, till noon. Then as the sun went down <br> The balmy slumber left me, and I saw <br> Thy daughter’s handmaids sporting on the shore, <br> And her among them, goddess-like. To her <br> I came a suppliant, nor did she receive <br> My suit unkindly as a maid so young <br> Might do, for youth is foolish. She bestowed <br> Food and red wine abundantly, and gave, <br> When I had bathed, the garments I have on. <br> Thus is my tale of suffering truly told.”</p>
<p class="verse">And then Alcinoüs answered him and said:⁠— <br> “Stranger, one duty hath my child o’erlooked⁠— <br> To bid thee follow hither with her maids, <br> Since thou didst sue to her the first of all.”</p>
<p class="verse">Ulysses, the sagacious, thus replied:⁠— <br> “Blame not for that, O hero, I entreat, <br> Thy faultless daughter. She commanded me <br> To follow with her maids, but I refrained <br> For fear and awe of thee, lest, at the sight, <br> Thou mightest be displeased; for we are prone <br> To dark misgivings⁠—we, the sons of men.” <br> Again Alcinoüs spake: “The heart that beats <br> Within my bosom is not rashly moved <br> To wrath, and better is the temperate mood. <br> This must I say, O Father Jupiter, <br> And Pallas and Apollo! I could wish <br> That, being as thou art, and of like mind <br> With me, thou wouldst receive to be thy bride <br> My daughter, and be called my son-in-law, <br> And here abide. A palace I would give, <br> And riches, shouldst thou willingly remain. <br> Against thy will let no Phaeacian dare <br> To keep thee here. May Father Jove forbid! <br> And that thou mayst be sure of my intent, <br> I name tomorrow for thy voyage home. <br> Sleep in thy bed till then; and they shall row <br> O’er the calm sea thy galley, till thou come <br> To thine own land and home, or wheresoe’er <br> Thou wilt, though further off the coast should be <br> Than far Euboea, most remote of lands⁠— <br> So do the people of our isle declare, <br> Who saw it when they over sea conveyed <br> The fair-haired Rhadamanthus, on his way <br> To visit Tityus, son of Earth. They went <br> Thither, accomplishing with little toil <br> Their voyage in the compass of a day, <br> And brought the hero to our isle again. <br> Now shalt thou learn, and in thy heart confess, <br> How much our galleys and our youths excel <br> With bladed oars to stir the whirling brine.”</p>
<p class="verse">So spake the king, and the great sufferer <br> Ulysses heard with gladness, and preferred <br> A prayer, and called on Jupiter and said:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Grant, Father Jove, that all the king has said <br> May be fulfilled! so shall his praise go forth <br> Over the foodful earth, and never die, <br> And I shall see my native land again.”</p>
<p class="verse">So they conferred. White-armed Aretè spake, <br> And bade her maidens in the portico <br> Place couches, and upon them lay fair rugs <br> Of purple dye, and tapestry on these, <br> And for the outer covering shaggy cloaks. <br> Forth from the hall they issued, torch in hand; <br> And when with speed the ample bed was made, <br> They came and summoned thus the chief to rest:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Rise, stranger, go to rest; thy bed is made.” <br> Thus spake the maidens, and the thought of sleep <br> Was welcome to Ulysses. So that night <br> On his deep couch the noble sufferer <br> Slumbered beneath the sounding portico. <br> Alcinoüs laid him down in a recess <br> Within his lofty palace, near to whom <br> The queen his consort graced the marriage-bed.</p>
