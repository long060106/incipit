<p class="lede">Return of Ulysses to the island of Circè⁠—Her counsels respecting his homeward voyage⁠—The sirens⁠—Escape from Scylla and Charybdis⁠—His arrival at Trinacria⁠—Slaughter of the oxen of the Sun by his companions⁠—A tempest, in consequence, by which his companions all perish, and he only escapes by swimming to the island of Calypso.</p>
<p class="verse">“Now when our barque had left Océanus <br> And entered the great deep, we reached the isle <br> Aeaea, where the Morning, child of Dawn, <br> Abides, and holds her dances, and the Sun <br> Goes up from earth. We landed there and drew <br> Our galley up the beach; we disembarked <br> And laid us down to sleep beside the sea, <br> And waited for the holy Morn to rise.</p>
<p class="verse">“Then when the rosy-fingered Morn appeared, <br> The child of Dawn, I sent my comrades forth <br> To bring from Circè’s halls Elpenor’s corse. <br> And where a headland stretched into the deep <br> We hewed down trees, and held the funeral rites <br> With many tears; and having there consumed <br> The body and the arms with fire, we built <br> A tomb, and reared a column to the dead, <br> And on its summit fixed a tapering oar.</p>
<p class="verse">“All this was duly done; yet was the news <br> Of our return from Hades not concealed <br> From Circè. She attired herself in haste <br> And came; her maids came with her, bringing bread <br> And store of meats and generous wine; and thus <br> Spake the wise goddess, standing in the midst:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“ ‘Ah, daring ones! who, yet alive, have gone <br> Down to the abode of Pluto; twice to die <br> Is yours, while others die but once. Yet now <br> Take food, drink wine, and hold a feast today, <br> And with the dawn of morning ye shall sail; <br> And I will show the way, and teach you all <br> Its dangers, so that ye may not lament <br> False counsels followed, either on the land <br> Or on the water, to your grievous harm.’</p>
<p class="verse">“She spake, and our confiding minds were swayed <br> Easily by her counsels. All that day <br> Till set of sun we sat and banqueted <br> Upon the abundant meats and generous wines; <br> And when the Sun went down, and darkness came, <br> The crew beside the fastenings of our barque <br> Lay down to sleep, while Circè took my hand, <br> Led me apart, and made me sit, and took <br> Her seat before me, and inquired of all <br> That I had seen. I told her faithfully, <br> And then the mighty goddess Circè said:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“ ‘Thus far is well; now needfully attend <br> To what I say, and may some deity <br> Help thee remember it! Thou first wilt come <br> To where the Sirens haunt. They throw a spell <br> O’er all who pass that way. If unawares <br> One finds himself so nigh that he can hear <br> Their voices, round him nevermore shall wife <br> And lisping children gather, welcoming <br> His safe return with joy. The Sirens sit <br> In a green field, and charm with mellow notes <br> The comer, while beside them lie in heaps <br> The bones of men decaying underneath <br> The shrivelled skins. Take heed and pass them by. <br> First fill with wax well kneaded in the palm <br> The ears of thy companions, that no sound <br> May enter. Hear the music, if thou wilt, <br> But let thy people bind thee, hand and foot, <br> To the good ship, upright against the mast, <br> And round it wind the cord, that thou mayst hear <br> The ravishing notes. But shouldst thou then entreat <br> Thy men, commanding them to set thee free, <br> Let them be charged to bind thee yet more fast <br> With added bands. And when they shall have passed <br> The Sirens by, I will not judge for thee <br> Which way to take; consider for thyself; <br> I tell thee of two ways. There is a pile <br> Of beetling rocks, where roars the mighty surge <br> Of dark-eyed Amphitritè; these are called <br> The Wanderers by the blessed gods. No birds <br> Can pass them safe, not even the timid doves, <br> Which bear ambrosia to our father Jove, <br> But ever doth the slippery rock take off <br> Someone, whose loss the God at once supplies, <br> To keep their number full. To these no barque <br> Guided by man has ever come, and left <br> The spot unwrecked; the billows of the deep <br> And storms of fire in air have scattered wide <br> Timbers of ships and bodies of drowned men. <br> One only of the barques that plough the deep <br> Has passed them safely⁠—<i>Argo</i>, known to all <br> By fame, when coming from Aeaeta home⁠— <br> And her the billows would have dashed against <br> The enormous rocks, if Juno, for the sake <br> Of Jason, had not come to guide it through.</p>
<p class="verse">“ ‘Two are the rocks; one lifts to the broad heaven <br> Its pointed summit, where a dark gray cloud <br> Broods, and withdraws not; never is the sky <br> Clear o’er that peak, not even in summer days <br> Or autumn; nor can man ascend its steeps, <br> Or venture down⁠—so smooth the sides, as if <br> Man’s art had polished them. There in the midst <br> Upon the western side toward Erebus <br> There yawns a shadowy cavern; thither thou, <br> Noble Ulysses, steer thy barque, yet keep <br> So far aloof that, standing on the deck, <br> A youth might send an arrow from a bow <br> Just to the cavern’s mouth. There Scylla dwells, <br> And fills the air with fearful yells; her voice <br> The cry of whelps just littered, but herself <br> A frightful prodigy⁠—a sight which none <br> Would care to look on, though he were a god. <br> Twelve feet are hers, all shapeless; six long necks, <br> A hideous head on each, and triple rows <br> Of teeth, close set and many, threatening death. <br> And half her form is in the cavern’s womb, <br> And forth from that dark gulf her heads are thrust, <br> To look abroad upon the rocks for prey⁠— <br> Dolphin, or dogfish, or the mightier whale, <br> Such as the murmuring Amphitritè breeds <br> In multitudes. No mariner can boast <br> That he has passed by Scylla with a crew <br> Unharmed; she snatches from the deck, and bears <br> Away in each grim mouth, a living man.</p>
<p class="verse">“ ‘Another rock, Ulysses, thou wilt see, <br> Of lower height, so near her that a spear, <br> Cast by the hand, might reach it. On it grows <br> A huge wild fig-tree with luxuriant leaves. <br> Below, Charybdis, of immortal birth, <br> Draws the dark water down; for thrice a day <br> She gives it forth, and thrice with fearful whirl <br> She draws it in. O, be it not thy lot <br> To come while the dark water rushes down! <br> Even Neptune could not then deliver thee. <br> Then turn thy course with speed toward Scylla’s rock, <br> And pass that way; ’twere better far that six <br> Should perish from the ship than all be lost’</p>
<p class="verse">“She spake, and I replied: ‘O goddess, deign <br> To tell me truly, cannot I at once <br> Escape Charybdis and defend my friends <br> Against the rage of Scylla when she strikes?’</p>
<p class="verse">“I spake; the mighty goddess answered me:⁠— <br> ‘Rash man! dost thou still think of warlike deeds, <br> And feats of strength? And wilt thou not give way <br> Even to the deathless gods? That pest is not <br> Of mortal mould; she cannot die, she is <br> A thing to tremble and to shudder at, <br> And fierce, and never to be overcome. <br> There is no room for courage; flight is best. <br> And if thou shouldst delay beside the rock <br> To take up arms, I fear lest once again <br> She fall on thee with all her heads, and seize <br> As many men. Pass by the monster’s haunt <br> With all the speed that thou canst make, and call <br> Upon Crataeis, who brought Scylla forth <br> To be the plague of men, and who will calm <br> Her rage, that she assault thee not again.</p>
<p class="verse">“ ‘Then in thy voyage shalt thou reach the isle <br> Trinacria, where, in pastures of the Sun, <br> His many beeves and fading sheep are fed⁠— <br> Seven herds of oxen, and as many flocks <br> Of sheep, and fifty in each flock and herd. <br> They never multiply; they never die. <br> Two shepherdesses tend them, goddesses, <br> Nymphs with redundant locks⁠—Lampelia one, <br> The other Phaëthusa. These the nymph <br> Naeëra to the overgoing Sun <br> Brought forth, and when their queenly mother’s care <br> Had reared them, she appointed them to dwell <br> In far Trinacria, there to keep the flocks <br> And oxen of their father. If thy thoughts <br> Be fixed on thy return, so that thou leave <br> These flocks and herds unharmed, ye all will come <br> To Ithaca, though after many toils. <br> But if thou rashly harm them, I foretell <br> Destruction to thy ship and all its crew; <br> And if thyself escape, thou wilt return <br> Late and in sorrow, all thy comrades lost.’</p>
<p class="verse">“She spake; the Morning on her golden throne <br> Looked forth; the glorious goddess went her way <br> Into the isle, I to my ship, and bade <br> The men embark and cast the hawsers loose. <br> And straight they went on board, and duly manned <br> The benches, smiting as they sat with oars <br> The hoary waters. Circè, amber-haired, <br> The mighty goddess of the musical voice, <br> Sent a fair wind behind our dark-prowed ship <br> That gayly bore us company, and filled <br> The sails. When we had fairly ordered all <br> On board our galley, we sat down, and left <br> The favoring wind and helm to bear us on, <br> And thus in sadness I bespake the crew:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“ ‘My friends! it were not well that one or two <br> Alone should know the oracles I heard <br> From Circè, great among the goddesses; <br> And now will I disclose them, that ye all, <br> Whether we are to die or to escape <br> The doom of death, may be forewarned. And first <br> Against the wicked Sirens and their song <br> And flowery bank she warns us. I alone <br> May hear their voice, but ye must bind me first <br> With bands too strong to break, that I may stand <br> Upright against the mast; and let the cords <br> Be fastened round it. If I then entreat <br> And bid you loose me, make the bands more strong.’</p>
<p class="verse">“Thus to my crew I spake, and told them all <br> That they should know, while our good ship drew near <br> The island of the Sirens, prosperous gales <br> Wafting it gently onward. Then the breeze <br> Sank to a breathless calm; some deity <br> Had hushed the winds to slumber. Straightway rose <br> The men and furled the sails and laid them down <br> Within the ship, and sat and made the sea <br> White with the beating of their polished blades, <br> Made of the fir-tree. Then I took a mass <br> Of wax and cut it into many parts, <br> And kneaded each with a strong hand. It grew <br> Warm with the pressure, and the beams of him <br> Who journeys round the earth, the monarch Sun. <br> With this I filled the ears of all my men <br> From first to last. They bound me, in their turn, <br> Upright against the mast-tree, hand and foot, <br> And tied the cords around it. Then again <br> They sat and threshed with oars the hoary deep. <br> And when, in running rapidly, we came <br> So near the Sirens as to hear a voice <br> From where they sat, our galley flew not by <br> Unseen by them, and sweetly thus they sang:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“ ‘O world-renowned Ulysses! thou who art <br> The glory of the Achaians, turn thy barque <br> Landward, that thou mayst listen to our lay <br> No man has passed us in his galley yet, <br> Ere he has heard our warbled melodies. <br> He goes delighted hence a wiser man; <br> For all that in the spacious realm of Troy <br> The Greeks and Trojans by the will of Heaven <br> Endured we know, and all that comes to pass <br> In all the nations of the fruitful earth.’ ”</p>
<p class="verse">’Twas thus they sang, and sweet the strain. I longed <br> To listen, and with nods I gave the sign <br> To set me free; they only plied their oars <br> The faster. Then upsprang Eurylochus <br> And Perimedes, and with added cords <br> Bound me, and drew the others still more tight. <br> And when we now had passed the spot, and heard <br> No more the melody the Sirens sang, <br> My comrades hastened from their ears to take <br> The wax, and loosed the cords and set me free.</p>
<p class="verse">“As soon as we had left the isle, I saw <br> Mist and a mountain billow, and I heard <br> The thunder of the waters. From the hands <br> Of my affrighted comrades flew the oars, <br> The deep was all in uproar; but the ship <br> Stopped there, for all the rowers ceased their task. <br> I went through all the ship exhorting them <br> With cheerful words, man after man, and said:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“ ‘Reflect, my friends, that we are not untried <br> In evil fortunes, nor in sadder plight <br> Are we than when within his spacious cave <br> The brutal Cyclops held us prisoners; <br> Yet through my valor we escaped, and through <br> My counsels and devices, and I think <br> That ye will live to bear this day’s events <br> In memory like those. Now let us act. <br> Do all as I advise; go to your seats <br> Upon the benches, smiting with your oars <br> These mighty waves, and haply Jove will grant <br> That we escape the death which threatens us. <br> Thee, helmsman, I adjure⁠—and heed my words, <br> Since to thy hands alone is given in charge <br> Our gallant vessel’s rudder⁠—steer thou hence <br> From mist and tumbling waves, and well observe <br> The rock, lest where it juts into the sea <br> Thou heed it not, and bring us all to wreck.’</p>
<p class="verse">“I spake, and quickly all obeyed my words. <br> Yet said I naught of Scylla⁠—whom we now <br> Could not avoid⁠—lest all the crew in fear <br> Should cease to row, and crowd into the hold. <br> And then did I forget the stern command <br> Which Circè gave me, not to arm myself <br> For combat. In my shining arms I cased <br> My limbs, and took in hand two ponderous spears, <br> And went on deck, and stood upon the prow⁠— <br> For there it seemed to me that Scylla first <br> Would show herself⁠—that monster of the rocks⁠— <br> To seize my comrades. Yet I saw her not, <br> Though weary grew my eyes with looking long <br> And eagerly upon those dusky cliffs.</p>
<p class="verse">“Sadly we sailed into the strait, where stood <br> On one hand Scylla, and the dreaded rock <br> Charybdis on the other, drawing down <br> Into her horrid gulf the briny flood; <br> And as she threw it forth again, it tossed <br> And murmured as upon a glowing fire <br> The water in a cauldron, while the spray, <br> Thrown upward, fell on both the summit-rocks; <br> And when once more she swallowed the salt sea, <br> It whirled within the abyss, while far below <br> The bottom of blue sand was seen. My men <br> Grew pale with fear; we looked into the gulf <br> And thought our end was nigh. Then Scylla snatched <br> Six of my comrades from our hollow barque, <br> The best in valor and in strength of arm. <br> I looked to my good ship; I looked to them, <br> And saw their hands and feet still swung in air <br> Above me, while for the last time on earth <br> They called my name in agony of heart. <br> As when an angler on a jutting rock <br> Sits with his taper rod, and casts his bait <br> To snare the smaller fish, he sends the horn <br> Of a wild bull that guards his line afar <br> Into the water, and jerks out a fish, <br> And throws it gasping shoreward; so were they <br> Uplifted gasping to the rocks, and there <br> Scylla devoured them at her cavern’s mouth, <br> Stretching their hands to me with piercing cries <br> Of anguish. ’Twas in truth the saddest sight, <br> Whatever I have suffered and where’er <br> Have roamed the waters, that mine eyes have seen.</p>
<p class="verse">“Escaping thus the rocks, the dreaded haunt <br> Of Scylla and Charybdis, we approached <br> The pleasant island of the Sun, where grazed <br> The oxen with broad foreheads, beautiful, <br> And flocks of sheep, the fatlings of the god <br> Who makes the round of heaven. While yet at sea <br> I heard from my black ship the low of herds <br> In stables, and the bleatings of the flocks, <br> And straightway came into my thought the words <br> Of the blind seer Tiresias, him of Thebes, <br> And of Aeaean Circè, who had oft <br> Warned me to shun the island of the god <br> Whose light is sweet to all. And then I said <br> To my companions with a sorrowing heart:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“ ‘My comrades, sufferers as ye are, give ear. <br> I shall disclose the oracles which late <br> Tiresias and Aeaean Circè gave. <br> The goddess earnestly admonished me <br> Not to approach the island of the Sun, <br> Whose light is sweet to all, for there she said <br> Some great misfortune lay in wait for us. <br> Now let us speed the ship and pass the isle.’</p>
<p class="verse">“I spake; their hearts were broken as they heard, <br> And bitterly Eurylochus replied:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“ ‘Austere art thou, Ulysses; thou art strong <br> Exceedingly; no labor tires thy limbs; <br> They must be made of iron, since thy will <br> Denies thy comrades, overcome with toil <br> And sleeplessness, to tread the land again, <br> And in that isle amid the waters make <br> A generous banquet. Thou wouldst have us sail <br> Into the swiftly coming night, and stray <br> Far from the island, through the misty sea. <br> By night spring up the mighty winds that make <br> A wreck of ships, and how can one escape <br> Destruction, should a sudden hurricane <br> Rise from the south or the hard-blowing west, <br> Such as, in spite of all the sovereign gods, <br> Will cause a ship to founder in the deep? <br> Let us obey the dark-browed Night, and take <br> Our evening meal, remaining close beside <br> Our gallant barque, and go on board again <br> When morning breaks, and enter the wide sea.’</p>
<p class="verse">“So spake Eurylochus; the rest approved. <br> And then I knew that some divinity <br> Was meditating evil to our band, <br> And I bespake him thus in winged words:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“ ‘Eurylochus, ye force me to your will, <br> Since I am only one. Now all of you <br> Bind yourselves to me firmly, by an oath, <br> That if ye haply here shall meet a herd <br> Of beeves or flock of sheep, ye will not dare <br> To slay a single ox or sheep, but feed <br> Contented on the stores that Circè gave.’</p>
<p class="verse">“I spake, and readily my comrades swore <br> As I required; and when that solemn oath <br> Was taken, to the land we brought and moored <br> Our galley in a winding creek, beside <br> A fountain of sweet water. From the deck <br> Stepped my companions and made ready there <br> Their evening cheer. They ate and drank till thirst <br> And hunger were appeased, and then they thought <br> Of those whom Scylla from our galley’s deck <br> Snatched and devoured; they thought and wept till sleep <br> Stole softly over them amid their tears. <br> Now came the third part of the night; the stars <br> Were sinking when the Cloud-compeller Jove <br> Sent forth a violent wind with eddying gusts, <br> And covered both the earth and sky with clouds, <br> And darkness fell from heaven. When Morning came, <br> The rosy-fingered daughter of the Dawn, <br> We drew the ship into a spacious grot. <br> There were the seats of nymphs, and there we saw <br> The smooth fair places where they danced. I called <br> A council of my men, and said to them:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“ ‘My friends, in our good ship are food and drink; <br> Abstain we from these beeves, lest we be made <br> To suffer; for these herds and these fair flocks <br> Are sacred to a dreaded god, the Sun⁠— <br> The all-beholding and all-hearing Sun.’</p>
<p class="verse">“I spake, and all were swayed by what I said <br> Full easily. A month entire the gales <br> Blew from the south, and after that no wind <br> Save east and south. While yet we had our bread <br> And ruddy wine, my comrades spared the beeves, <br> Moved by the love of life. But when the stores <br> On board our galley were consumed, they roamed <br> The island in their need, and sought for prey, <br> And snared with barbed hooks the fish and birds⁠— <br> Whatever came to hand⁠—till they were gaunt <br> With famine. Meantime I withdrew alone <br> Into the isle, to supplicate the gods, <br> If haply one of them might yet reveal <br> The way of my return. As thus I strayed <br> Into the land, apart from all the rest, <br> I found a sheltered nook where no wind came, <br> And prayed with washen hands to all the gods <br> Who dwell in heaven. At length they bathed my lids <br> In a soft sleep. Meantime, Eurylochus <br> With fatal counsels thus harangued my men:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“ ‘Hear, my companions, sufferers as ye are, <br> The words that I shall speak. All modes of death <br> Are hateful to the wretched race of men; <br> But this of hunger, thus to meet our fate, <br> Is the most fearful. Let us drive apart <br> The best of all the oxen of the Sun, <br> And sacrifice them to the immortal ones <br> Who dwell in the broad heaven. And if we come <br> To Ithaca, our country, we will there <br> Build to the Sun, whose path is o’er our heads, <br> A sumptuous temple, and endow its shrine <br> With many gifts and rare. But if it be <br> His will, approved by all the other gods, <br> To sink our barque in anger, for the sake <br> Of these his high-horned oxen, I should choose <br> Sooner to gasp my life away amid <br> The billows of the deep, than pine to death <br> By famine in this melancholy isle.’</p>
<p class="verse">“So spake Eurylochus; the crew approved. <br> Then from the neighboring herd they drove the best <br> Of all the beeves; for near the dark-prowed ship <br> The fair broad-fronted herd with crooked horns <br> Were feeding. Round the victims stood my crew, <br> And, offering their petitions to the gods, <br> Held tender oak-leaves in their hands, just plucked <br> From a tall tree, for in our good ship’s hold <br> Was no white barley now. When they had prayed, <br> And slain and dressed the beeves, they hewed away <br> The thighs and covered them with double folds <br> Of caul, and laid raw slices over these. <br> Wine had they not to pour in sacrifice <br> Upon the burning flesh; they poured instead <br> Water, and roasted all the entrails thus. <br> Now when the thighs were thoroughly consumed, <br> And entrails tasted, all the rest was carved <br> Into small portions, and transfixed with spits.</p>
<p class="verse">“Just then the gentle slumber left my lids. <br> I hurried to the shore and my good ship, <br> And, drawing near, perceived the savory steam <br> From the burnt-offering. Sorrowfully then <br> I called upon the ever-living gods:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“ ‘O Father Jove, and all ye blessed gods, <br> Who live forever, ’twas a cruel sleep <br> In which ye lulled me to my grievous harm; <br> My comrades here have done a fearful wrong.’</p>
<p class="verse">“Lampetia, of the trailing robes, in haste <br> Flew to the Sun, who journeys round the earth, <br> To tell him that my crew had slain his beeves, <br> And thus in anger he bespake the gods:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“ ‘O Father Jove, and all ye blessed gods <br> Who never die, avenge the wrong I bear <br> Upon the comrades of Laertes’ son, <br> Ulysses, who have foully slain my beeves, <br> In which I took delight whene’er I rose <br> Into the starry heaven, and when again <br> I sank from heaven to earth. If for the wrong <br> They make not large amends, I shall go down <br> To Hades, there to shine among the dead.’</p>
<p class="verse">“The cloud-compelling Jupiter replied:⁠— <br> ‘Still shine, O Sun! among the deathless gods <br> And mortal men, upon the nourishing earth. <br> Soon will I cleave, with a white thunderbolt, <br> Their galley in the midst of the black sea.’</p>
<p class="verse">“This from Calypso of the radiant hair <br> I heard thereafter; she herself, she said, <br> Had heard it from the herald Mercury.</p>
<p class="verse">“When to the ship I came, beside the sea, <br> I sternly chid them all, man after man, <br> Yet could we think of no redress; the beeves <br> Were dead; and now with prodigies the gods <br> Amazed my comrades⁠—the skins moved and crawled, <br> The flesh both raw and roasted on the spits <br> Lowed with the voice of oxen. Six whole days <br> My comrades feasted, taking from the herd <br> The Sun’s best oxen. When Saturnian Jove <br> Brought the seventh day, the tempest ceased; the wind <br> Fell, and we straightway went on board. We set <br> The mast upright, and, spreading the white sails, <br> We ventured on the great wide sea again.</p>
<p class="verse">“When we had left the isle, and now appeared <br> No other land, but only sea and sky, <br> The son of Saturn caused a lurid cloud <br> To gather o’er the galley, and to cast <br> Its darkness on the deep. Not long our ship <br> Ran onward, ere the furious west-wind rose <br> And blew a hurricane. A strong blast snapped <br> Both ropes that held the mast; the mast fell back; <br> The tackle dropped entangled to the hold; <br> The mast, in falling on the galley’s stern, <br> Dashed on the pilot’s head and crushed the bones, <br> And from the deck he plunged like one who dives <br> Into the deep; his gallant spirit left <br> The limbs at once. Jove thundered from on high, <br> And sent a thunderbolt into the ship, <br> That, quaking with the fearful blow, and filled <br> With stifling sulphur, shook my comrades off <br> Into the deep. They floated round the ship <br> Like seamews; Jupiter had cut them off <br> From their return. I moved from place to place, <br> Still in the ship, until the tempest’s force <br> Parted the sides and keel. Before the waves <br> The naked keel was swept. The mast had snapped <br> Just at the base, but round it was a thong <br> Made of a bullock’s hide; with this I bound <br> The mast and keel together, took my seat <br> Upon them, and the wild winds bore me on.</p>
<p class="verse">“The west-wind ceased to rage; but in its stead <br> The south-wind blew, and brought me bitter grief. <br> I feared lest I must measure back my way <br> To grim Charybdis. All night long I rode <br> The waves, and with the rising sun drew near <br> The rock of Scylla and the terrible <br> Charybdis as her gulf was drawing down <br> The waves of the salt sea. There as I came <br> I raised myself on high till I could grasp <br> The lofty fig-tree, and I clung to it <br> As clings a bat⁠—for I could neither find <br> A place to plant my feet, nor could I climb, <br> So distant were the roots, so far apart <br> The long huge branches overshadowing <br> Charybdis. Yet I firmly kept my hold <br> Till she should throw the keel and mast again <br> Up from the gulf. They, as I waited long, <br> Came up again, though late⁠—as late as one <br> Who long has sat adjudging strifes between <br> Young suitors pleading in the marketplace <br> Rises and goes to take his evening meal; <br> So late the timbers of my barque returned, <br> Thrown from Charybdis. Then I dropped amid <br> The dashing waves, and came with hands and feet <br> On those long timbers in the midst, that they <br> Might bear my weight. I sat on them and rowed <br> With both my hands. The father of the gods <br> And mortals suffered not that I should look <br> On Scylla’s rock again, else had I not <br> Escaped a cruel death. For nine long days <br> I floated on the waters; on the tenth <br> The gods at nightfall bore me to an isle⁠— <br> Ogygia, where Calypso, amber-haired, <br> A mighty goddess, skilled in song, abides, <br> Who kindly welcomed me, and cherished me. <br> Why should I speak of this? Here in these halls <br> I gave the history yesterday to thee <br> And to thy gracious consort, and I hate <br> To tell again a tale once fully told.”</p>
