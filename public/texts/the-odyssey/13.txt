<p class="lede">Departure of Ulysses from the court of Alcinoüs⁠—Arrival of the ship at Ithaca⁠—Ulysses carried on shore by the Phaeacians while asleep, and left⁠—His treasures landed also⁠—The ship, while returning, transformed by Neptune into a rock⁠—Dialogue between Pallas and Ulysses concerning the destruction of the suitors⁠—Concealment of the treasures in a cave⁠—Transformation of Ulysses into an old man.</p>
<p class="verse">He spake, and all within those shadowy halls <br> Were silent; all were held in mute delight. <br> Alcinoüs then took up the word and said:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Since thou hast come, Ulysses, as a guest, <br> To this high pile and to these brazen rooms, <br> So long a sufferer, thou must not depart <br> Upon thy homeward way a wanderer still. <br> And this let me enjoin on each of you <br> Who in this palace drink at our repasts <br> The choice red wine, and listen to the bard: <br> Already in a polished chest are laid <br> Changes of raiment, works of art in gold, <br> And other gifts, which the Phaeacian chiefs <br> Have destined for our guest; now let us each <br> Bestow an ample tripod and a vase, <br> And we in an assembly of the realm <br> Will see the cost repaid, since otherwise <br> Great would the burden be that each must bear.” <br> So spake Alcinoüs; they approved, and sought <br> Their homes to sleep, but when the child of Dawn, <br> The rosy-fingered Morn, appeared, they came, <br> All bringing to the ship their gifts of brass <br> In honor of the guest. The mighty prince <br> Alcinoüs, going through the ship, bestowed <br> The whole beneath the benches, that no one <br> Of those who leaned to pull the oar might thence <br> Meet harm or hindrance. Then they all went back <br> To the king’s palace, and prepared a feast.</p>
<p class="verse">The mighty prince Alcinoüs offered up <br> For them an ox to cloud-compelling Jove, <br> The son of Saturn, ruler over all. <br> They burned the thighs, and held high festival, <br> And all was mirth. Divine Demodocus <br> The bard, whom all men reverenced, sang to them. <br> Meantime Ulysses often turned to look <br> At the bright Sun, and longed to see him set, <br> So eager was the hero to set sail <br> Upon his homeward way. As when a swain <br> Awaits his evening meal, for whom all day <br> Two dark-brown steers have dragged the solid plough <br> Through fallow grounds, and welcome is the hour <br> Of sunset, calling him to his repast, <br> And wearily he walks with failing knees, <br> So welcome to Ulysses did the light <br> Of day go down. Then did he hold discourse <br> With the Phaeacians, lovers of the sea, <br> And chiefly with Alcinoüs, speaking thus:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“O monarch most illustrious of thy race, <br> Alcinoüs, now when ye have duly poured <br> Wine to the gods, be pleased to send me hence <br> In peace, and fare ye well! All that my heart <br> Could wish have ye provided bounteously⁠— <br> An escort and rich gifts; and may the gods <br> Bestow their blessing with them! May I meet <br> My blameless wife again, and find my friends <br> Prosperous! And ye whom I shall leave behind, <br> Long may ye make the wives of your young years <br> And children happy! May the gods vouchsafe <br> To crown with every virtue you and them, <br> And may no evil light upon your isle!”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake; the assembly all approved his words, <br> And bade send forth the stranger on his way, <br> Who spake so nobly. Then the mighty prince <br> Alcinoüs turned, and to the herald said:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Now mix the wine, Pontonoüs, in a jar, <br> And bear a part to all beneath our roof, <br> That we with prayers to Father Jupiter <br> May send the stranger to his native land.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake; Pontonoüs mingled for the guests <br> The generous wine, and went with it to each, <br> Who poured it on the ground, from where they sat, <br> To all the dwellers of the ample heaven; <br> And then the great Ulysses, rising up, <br> Placed the round goblet in Aretè’s hands, <br> And thus bespake the queen with winged words:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Farewell, O queen, through the long years, till age <br> And death, which are the lot of all, shall come. <br> Now I depart, but mayst thou, here among <br> Thy people, and the children of thy love, <br> And King Alcinoüs, lead a happy life!”</p>
<p class="verse">So spake the highborn chieftain, and withdrew, <br> And crossed the threshold. King Alcinoüs sent <br> A herald with him to direct his way <br> To the fleet ship and border of the deep. <br> Aretè also sent her servant-maids⁠— <br> One bearing a fresh cloak and tunic, one <br> A coffer nobly wrought, and yet a third <br> Bread and red wine; and when they reached the ship <br> Beside the sea, the diligent crew received <br> Their burdens, and bestowed within the hold <br> The food and drink, but spread upon the deck <br> And at the stern a mat and linen sheet, <br> That there Ulysses undisturbed might sleep. <br> He went on board and silently lay down, <br> While all the rowers in due order took <br> Their seats upon the benches. Loosing first <br> The hawser from the perforated rock, <br> They bent them to their task, and flung the brine <br> Up from the oar, while on the chieftain’s lids <br> Lighted a sweet and deep and quiet sleep, <br> Most like to death. As, smitten by the lash, <br> Four harnessed stallions spring on high and dart <br> Across the plain together; so the prow <br> Rose leaping forward, while behind it rolled <br> A huge dark billow of the roaring sea. <br> Safely and steadily the galley ran, <br> Nor could a falcon, swiftest of the birds, <br> Have kept beside it, with such speed it flew, <br> Bearing a hero who was like the gods <br> In wisdom, and whose sufferings in the wars <br> And voyages among the furious waves <br> Were great and many, though he slumbered now <br> In peace, forgetful of misfortunes past.</p>
<p class="verse">Now when that brightest star, the harbinger <br> Of Morning, daughter of the Dawn, arose, <br> The barque had passed the sea, and reached the isle.</p>
<p class="verse">A port there is in Ithaca, the haunt <br> Of Phorcys, Ancient of the Sea. Steep shores <br> Stretch inward toward each other, and roll back <br> The mighty surges which the hoarse winds hurl <br> Against them from the ocean, while within <br> Ships ride without their hawsers when they once <br> Have passed the haven’s mouth. An olive-tree <br> With spreading branches at the farther end <br> Of that fair haven stands, and overbrows <br> A pleasant shady grotto of the nymphs <br> Called Naiads. Cups and jars of stone are ranged <br> Within, and bees lay up their honey there. <br> There from their spindles wrought of stone the nymphs <br> Weave their sea-purple robes, which all behold <br> With wonder; there are ever-flowing springs. <br> Two are the entrances: one toward the north <br> By which men enter; but a holier one <br> Looks toward the south, nor ever mortal foot <br> May enter there. By that way pass the gods.</p>
<p class="verse">They touched the land, for well they knew the spot. <br> The galley, urged so strongly by the arms <br> Of those who plied the oar, ran up the beach <br> Quite half her length. And then the crew came forth <br> From the good ship, and first they lifted out <br> Ulysses with the linen and rich folds <br> Of tapestry, and laid him on the sands <br> In a deep slumber. Then they also took <br> The presents from the hold, which, as he left <br> Their isle, the princes of Phaeacia gave <br> By counsel of wise Pallas. These they piled <br> Close to the olive-tree, without the way, <br> That none, in passing, ere Ulysses woke, <br> Might do their owner wrong. Then homeward sailed <br> The crew; but Neptune, who could not forge <br> The threats which he had uttered long before <br> Against the godlike chief Ulysses, thus <br> Sought to explore the will of Jupiter:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“O Father Jove! I shall no more be held <br> In honor with the gods, since mortal men, <br> The people of Phaeacia, though their race <br> Is of my lineage, do not honor me. <br> I meant Ulysses should not reach his home <br> Save with much suffering, though I never thought <br> To hinder his return, for thou hadst given <br> Thy promise and thy nod that it should be. <br> Yet these Phaeacians, in a gallant barque, <br> Have borne him o’er the deep, and while he slept, <br> Have laid him down in Ithaca, and given <br> Large gifts, abundant store of brass and gold, <br> And woven work, more than he could have brought <br> From captured Ilium, if he had returned <br> Safely, with all his portion of the spoil.”</p>
<p class="verse">Then cloud-compelling Jupiter replied: <br> “Earth-shaker, ruler of a mighty realm! <br> What hast thou said? The gods deny thee not <br> Due honor; perilous it were for them <br> To show contempt for one who stands in age <br> And might above them all. But if among <br> The sons of men be one who puts such trust <br> In his own strength as not to honor thee, <br> Do as seems good to thee, and as thou wilt.”</p>
<p class="verse">Promptly the god who shakes the shores replied; <br> “What thou dost bid me I would do at once, <br> But that I fear and would avoid thy wrath. <br> I would destroy that fair Phaeacian barque <br> In its return across the misty sea <br> From bearing home Ulysses, that no more <br> May the Phaeacians lend an escort thus <br> To wandering men, and I would also cause <br> A lofty mount to rise and hide their town.”</p>
<p class="verse">Then spake again the Cloud-compeller Jove: <br> “Thus were it best, my brother: when the crowd <br> Of citizens already see the ship <br> Approaching, then transform it to a rock <br> In semblance of a galley, that they all <br> May gaze in wonder; thus wilt thou have caused <br> A lofty mount to stand before their town.”</p>
<p class="verse">This when the shaker of the shores had heard, <br> He flew to Scheria, the Phaeacian isle, <br> And stood, until that galley, having crossed <br> The sea, came swiftly scudding. He drew near <br> And smote it with his open palm, and made <br> The ship a rock, fast rooted in the bed <br> Of the deep sea, and then he went his way.</p>
<p class="verse">Then winged words were spoken in that throng <br> Of the Phaeacians, wielders of long oars, <br> And far renowned in feats of seamanship. <br> And, looking on each other, thus they said:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Ha! what has stayed our good ship on the sea? <br> This moment we beheld her hastening home.”</p>
<p class="verse">’Twas thus they talked, unweeting of the cause. <br> But then Alcinoüs to the assembly said:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Yes! now I call to mind the ancient words <br> Of prophecy⁠—my father’s⁠—who was wont <br> To say that Neptune sorely is displeased <br> That we should give to every man who comes <br> Safe escort to his home. In coming times⁠— <br> Such was my father’s prophecy⁠—the god <br> Would yet destroy a well-appointed barque <br> Of the Phaeacians on the misty deep <br> Returning from an escort, and would cause <br> A lofty mount to stand before our town. <br> So prophesied the aged man; his words <br> Are here fulfilled. Now do as I appoint, <br> And let us all obey. Henceforth refrain <br> From bearing to their homes the strangers thrown <br> Upon our coast; and let us sacrifice <br> To Neptune twelve choice bullocks of the herd, <br> That he may pity us, nor hide our town <br> With a huge mountain from the sight of men.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake, and they were awed and straightway brought <br> The bullocks for the sacrifice. So prayed <br> To sovereign Neptune the Phaeacian chiefs <br> And princes, standing round the altar-fires.</p>
<p class="verse">Now woke the great Ulysses from his sleep <br> In his own land, and yet he knew it not. <br> Long had he been away, and Pallas now, <br> The goddess-child of Jove, had cast a mist <br> Around him, that he might not yet be known <br> To others, and that she might tell him first <br> What he should learn; nor even might his wife, <br> Nor friends, nor people, know of his return, <br> Ere he avenged upon the suitor crew <br> His wrongs, and therefore all things wore to him <br> Another look⁠—the footways stretching far, <br> The bights where ships were moored, the towering rocks, <br> And spreading trees. He rose and stood upright, <br> And gazed upon his native coast and wept, <br> And smote his thigh, and said in bitter grief:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Ah me! what region am I in, among <br> What people? lawless, cruel, and unjust? <br> Or are they hospitable men, who fear <br> The gods? And where shall I bestow these goods, <br> And whither go myself? Would that they all <br> Were still with the Phaeacians, and that I <br> Had found some other great and mighty king <br> Kindly to welcome me, and send me back <br> To my own land. I know not where to place <br> These treasures, and I must not leave them here, <br> Lest others come and seize them as a spoil. <br> Nay, these Phaeacian chiefs and counsellors <br> Were not, in all things, either wise or just. <br> They gave their word to land me on the coast <br> Of pleasant Ithaca, and have not kept <br> Their promise. O, may Jove avenge this wrong! <br> He who protects the suppliant, who beholds <br> All men with equal eye, and punishes <br> The guilty. Now will I review my stores <br> And number them again, that I may see <br> If those who left me here have taken aught.”</p>
<p class="verse">Thus having said, he numbered all his gifts⁠— <br> Beautiful tripods, cauldrons, works of gold, <br> And gorgeous woven raiment; none of these <br> Were wanting. Then he pined to see again <br> His native isle, and slowly paced the beach <br> Of the loud sea, lamenting bitterly. <br> There Pallas came to meet him in the shape <br> Of a young shepherd, delicately formed, <br> As are the sons of kings. A mantle lay <br> Upon her shoulder in rich folds; her feet <br> Shone in their sandals: in her hand she bore <br> A javelin. As Ulysses saw, his heart <br> Was glad within him, and he hastened on, <br> And thus accosted her with winged words:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Fair youth, who art the first whom I have met <br> Upon this shore, I bid thee hail, and hope <br> Thou meetest me with no unkind intent. <br> Protect what thou beholdest here and me; <br> I make my suit to thee as to a god, <br> And come to thy dear knees. And tell, I pray, <br> That I may know the truth, what land is this? <br> What people? who the dwellers? may it be <br> A pleasant isle, or is it but the shore <br> Of fruitful mainland shelving to the sea?”</p>
<p class="verse">And then the goddess, blue-eyed Pallas, said: <br> “Of simple mind art thou, unless perchance <br> Thou comest from afar, if thou dost ask <br> What country this may be. It is not quite <br> A nameless region; many know it well <br> Of those who dwell beneath the rising sun, <br> And those, behind, in Evening’s dusky realm. <br> Rugged it is, and suited ill to steeds, <br> Yet barren it is not, though level grounds <br> Are none within its borders. It is rich <br> In corn and wine, for seasonable rains <br> And dews refresh its soil. Large flocks of goats <br> And herds of beeves are pastured here; all kinds <br> Of trees are in its forests, and its springs <br> Are never dry. The fame of Ithaca, <br> Stranger, has travelled to the Trojan coast, <br> Though that, I hear, lies far away from Greece.”</p>
<p class="verse">She spake; Ulysses, the great sufferer, <br> Rejoiced to be in his own land, whose name <br> Pallas, the child of aegis-bearing Jove, <br> Had just now uttered. Then with winged words <br> He spake, but not the truth; his artful speech <br> Put that aside, forever in his breast <br> The power of shrewd invention was awake:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“In the broad fields of Crete, that lie far off <br> Beyond the sea, I heard of Ithaca, <br> To which I now am come with these my goods. <br> I left as many for my sons and fled, <br> For I had slain Orsilochus, the fleet <br> Of foot, the dear son of Idomeneus, <br> Who overcame by swiftness in the race <br> The foremost runners in the realm of Crete. <br> He sought to rob me wholly of my share <br> Of Trojan spoil, for which I had endured <br> Hardships in war with heroes, and at sea <br> Among the angry waves. The cause was this: <br> I would not in the siege of Troy submit <br> To serve his father, but, apart from him, <br> I led a troop, companions of my own. <br> The youth returning from the fields I met, <br> And smote him with the spear⁠—for near the way <br> I lay in ambush with a single friend. <br> A night exceeding dark was in the sky; <br> No human eye beheld, nor did he know <br> Who took his life. When I had slain him thus <br> With the sharp spear I hastened to a ship <br> Of the Phoenicians, and besought their aid, <br> And gave them large reward, and bade them steer <br> To Pylos, bearing me, and leave me there, <br> Or where the Epeians hold the hallowed coast <br> Of Elis. But the force of adverse winds <br> Drove them unwilling thence; they meant no fraud. <br> We wandered hither, just at night we came; <br> And rowing hard, the seamen brought their ship <br> Within the port. No word was said of food, <br> Though great our need. All disembarked in haste <br> And lay upon the shore. Deep was the sleep <br> That stole upon my weary limbs. The men <br> Took from the hold my goods, and, bearing them <br> To where I slumbered on the sand, set sail <br> For populous Sidonia, leaving me <br> Here quite alone with sorrow in my heart.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake; the blue-eyed goddess, Pallas, smiled, <br> And touched the chief caressingly. She seemed <br> A beautiful and stately woman now, <br> Such as are skilled in works of rare device, <br> And thus she said to him in winged words:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Full shrewd were he, a master of deceit, <br> Who should surpass thee in the ways of craft, <br> Even though he were a god⁠—thou unabashed <br> And prompt with shifts, and measureless in wiles! <br> Thou canst not even in thine own land refrain <br> From artful figments and misleading words, <br> As thou hast practised from thy birth. But now <br> Speak we of other matters, for we both <br> Are skilled in stratagem. Thou art the first <br> Of living men in counsel and in speech, <br> And I am famed for foresight and for craft <br> Among the immortals. Dost thou not yet know <br> Pallas Athenè, child of Jove, whose aid <br> Is present to defend thee in all time <br> Of peril, and but lately gained for thee <br> The favor of the whole Phaeacian race? <br> And hither am I come to frame for thee <br> Wise counsels, and to hide away the stores <br> Given by the opulent Phaeacian chiefs <br> At thy departure. I shall also tell <br> What thou must yet endure beneath the roof <br> Of thine own palace, by the will of fate. <br> Yet bear it bravely, since thou must, nor speak <br> To any man or woman of thyself <br> And of thy wandering hither, but submit <br> To many things that grieve thee, silently, <br> And bear indignities from violent men.”</p>
<p class="verse">Ulysses, the sagacious, thus rejoined: <br> “O goddess, it is hard for mortal man <br> To know thee when he meets thee, though his sight <br> Be of the sharpest, for thou puttest on <br> At pleasure any form. Yet this I know, <br> That thou wert kind to me when we, the sons <br> Of Greece, were warring in the realm of Troy. <br> But when we had o’erthrown the lofty town <br> Of Priam, and embarked, and when some god <br> Had scattered the Achaians, after that, <br> Daughter of Jove, I never saw thee more, <br> Never perceived thee entering my barque <br> And guarding me from danger⁠—but I roamed <br> Ever from place to place, my heart weighed down <br> By sorrow, till the gods delivered me, <br> And till thy counsels in the opulent realm <br> Of the Phaeacians brought my courage back, <br> And thou thyself didst guide me to the town. <br> And now in thy great father’s name I pray⁠— <br> For yet I cannot think that I am come <br> To pleasant Ithaca, but have been thrown <br> Upon some other coast, and fear that thou <br> Art jesting with me, and hast spoken thus <br> But to deceive me⁠—tell me, is it true <br> That I am in my own beloved land?”</p>
<p class="verse">And then the goddess, blue-eyed Pallas, said: <br> “Such ever are thy thoughts, and therefore I <br> Must not forsake thee in thy need. I know <br> How prompt thy speech, how quick thy thought, how shrewd <br> Thy judgment. If another man had come <br> From such long wanderings, he had flown at once <br> Delighted to his children and his wife <br> In his own home. But thou desirest not <br> To ask or hear of them till thou hast put <br> Thy consort to the trial of her truth⁠— <br> Her who now sits within thy halls and waits <br> In vain for thee, and in perpetual grief <br> And weeping wears her nights and days away. <br> I never doubted⁠—well, in truth, I knew <br> That thou, with all thy comrades lost, wouldst reach <br> Thy country, but I dreaded to withstand <br> My father’s brother Neptune, who was wroth, <br> And fiercely wroth, for that thou hadst deprived <br> His well-beloved son of sight. But now <br> Attend, and I will show thee Ithaca <br> By certain tokens; mark them and believe. <br> The port of Phorcys, Ancient of the Deep, <br> Is here; and there the spreading olive-tree, <br> Just at the haven’s head; and, close beside, <br> The cool dark grotto, sacred to the nymphs <br> Called Naiads⁠—a wide-vaulted cave where once <br> Thou earnest oft with chosen hecatombs, <br> An offering to the nymphs⁠—and here thou seest <br> The mountain Neritus with all his woods.”</p>
<p class="verse">So spake the goddess, and dispersed the mist, <br> And all the scene appeared. Ulysses saw <br> Well pleased, rejoicing in his own dear land, <br> And, stooping, kissed the bountiful earth, and raised <br> His hands, and thus addressed the nymphs in prayer:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Nymphs, Naiads, born to Jove, I did not hope <br> To be with you again. With cheerful prayers <br> I now salute you. We shall bring you soon <br> Our offerings, as of yore, if graciously <br> Jove’s daughter, huntress-queen, shall grant me yet <br> To live, and bless my well-beloved son.”</p>
<p class="verse">And then the goddess, blue-eyed Pallas, said: <br> “Be of good cheer, and let no anxious thought <br> Disturb thy mind. Let us bestir ourselves <br> To hide away the treasures thou hast brought <br> Within this hallowed grot in some recess <br> Where they may lie in safety; afterward <br> Will we take counsel what should next be done.”</p>
<p class="verse">The goddess said these words, and took her way <br> Into the shadowy cavern, spying out <br> Its hiding-places; while Ulysses brought <br> The treasures thither in his arms⁠—the gold, <br> The enduring brass, the raiment nobly wrought⁠— <br> Which the Phaeacians gave him. These they laid <br> Together in due order; Pallas then, <br> The daughter of the Aegis-bearer Jove, <br> Closed up the opening with a massive rock. <br> Then, sitting by the sacred olive-tree, <br> They plotted to destroy the haughty crew <br> Of suitors, and the blue-eyed Pallas said:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“O nobly born, and versed in many wiles, <br> Son of Laertes! now the hour is come <br> To think how thou shalt lay avenging hands <br> Upon the shameless crew who, in thy house, <br> For three years past have made themselves its lords, <br> And wooed thy noble wife and brought her gifts, <br> While, pining still for thy return, she gave <br> Hopes to each suitor, and by messages <br> Made promises to all, though cherishing <br> A different purpose in her secret heart.”</p>
<p class="verse">Ulysses, the sagacious, answered her: <br> “Ah me, I should have perished utterly, <br> By such an evil fate as overtook <br> Atrides Agamemnon, in the halls <br> Of my own palace, but for thee, whose words, <br> O goddess, have revealed what I should know. <br> Now counsel me how I may be avenged. <br> Be ever by my side, and strengthen me <br> With courage, as thou didst when we o’erthrew <br> The towery crest of Ilium. Would thou wert <br> Still my ally, as then! I would engage, <br> O blue-eyed Pallas, with three hundred foes, <br> If thou, dread goddess, wouldst but counsel me.”</p>
<p class="verse">And then the blue-eyed Pallas spake again: <br> “I will be present with thee. When we once <br> Begin the work, thou shalt not leave my sight; <br> And many a haughty suitor with his blood <br> And brains shall stain thy spacious palace floor. <br> Now will I change thine aspect, so that none <br> Shall know thee. I will wither thy fair skin, <br> And it shall hang on crooked limbs; thy locks <br> Of auburn I will cause to fall away, <br> And round thee fling a cloak which all shall see <br> With loathing. I will make thy lustrous eyes <br> Dull to the sight, and thus shalt thou appear <br> A squalid wretch to all the suitor train, <br> And to thy wife, and to the son whom thou <br> Didst leave within thy palace. Then at first <br> Repair thou to the herdsman, him who keeps <br> Thy swine; for he is loyal, and he loves <br> Thy son and the discreet Penelope. <br> There wilt thou find him as he tends his swine, <br> That find their pasturage beside the rock <br> Of Corax, and by Arethusa’s fount. <br> On nourishing acorns they are fed, and drink <br> The dark clear water, whence the flesh of swine <br> Is fattened. There remain, and carefully <br> Inquire of all that thou wouldst know, while I, <br> Taking my way to Sparta, the abode <br> Of lovely women, call Telemachus, <br> Thy son, Ulysses, who hath visited <br> King Menelaus in his broad domain, <br> To learn if haply thou art living yet.”</p>
<p class="verse">Ulysses, the sagacious, answered her: <br> “Why didst not thou, to whom all things are known, <br> Tell him concerning me? Must he too roam <br> And suffer on the barren deep, and leave <br> To others his estates, to be their spoil?”</p>
<p class="verse">And then the blue-eyed goddess spake again: <br> “Let not that thought distress thee. It was I <br> Who sent him thither, that he might deserve <br> The praise of men. No evil meets him there; <br> But in the halls of Atreus’ son he sits, <br> Safe mid the abounding luxuries. ’Tis true <br> That even now the suitors lie in wait, <br> In their black ship, to slay him ere he reach <br> His native land; but that will hardly be <br> Before the earth shall cover many a one <br> Of the proud suitors who consume thy wealth.”</p>
<p class="verse">So Pallas spake, and touched him with her wand, <br> And caused the blooming skin to shrivel up <br> On his slow limbs, and the fair hair to fall, <br> And with an old man’s wrinkles covered all <br> His frame, and dimmed his lately glorious eyes. <br> Another garb she gave⁠—a squalid vest; <br> A ragged, dirty cloak, all stained with smoke; <br> And over all the huge hide of a stag, <br> From which the hair was worn. A staff, beside, <br> She gave, and shabby scrip with many a rent, <br> Tied with a twisted thong. This said and done, <br> They parted; and the goddess flew to seek <br> Telemachus in Sparta’s sacred town.</p>
