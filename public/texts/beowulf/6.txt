<p>The famous race of Spear-Danes.</p>
<p>Lo! the Spear-Danes’ glory through splendid achievements</p>
<p>The folk-kings’ former fame we have heard of,</p>
<p>How princes displayed then their prowess-in-battle.</p>
<p>Scyld, their mighty king, in honor of whom they are often called Scyldings. He is the great-grandfather of Hrothgar, so prominent in the poem.</p>
<p>Oft Scyld the Scefing from scathers in numbers</p>
<p>5</p>
<p>From many a people their mead-benches tore.</p>
<p>Since first he found him friendless and wretched,</p>
<p>The earl had had terror: comfort he got for it,</p>
<p>Waxed ’neath the welkin, world-honor gained,</p>
<p>Till all his neighbors o’er sea were compelled to</p>
<p>10</p>
<p>Bow to his bidding and bring him their tribute:</p>
<p>An excellent atheling! After was borne him</p>
<p>A son is born to him, who receives the name of Beowulf—a name afterwards made so famous by the hero of the poem.</p>
<p>A son and heir, young in his dwelling,</p>
<p>Whom God-Father sent to solace the people.</p>
<p>He had marked the misery malice had caused them,</p>
<p>15</p>
<p><sup>1</sup>That reaved of their rulers they wretched had erstwhile<sup>2</sup></p>
<p>Long been afflicted. The Lord, in requital,</p>
<p>Wielder of Glory, with world-honor blessed him.</p>
<p>Famed was Beowulf, far spread the glory</p>
<p>Of Scyld’s great son in the lands of the Danemen.</p>
<p>The ideal Teutonic king lavishes gifts on his vassals. 20</p>
<p>So the carle that is young, by kindnesses rendered</p>
<p>The friends of his father, with fees in abundance</p>
<p>Must be able to earn that when age approacheth</p>
<p>Eager companions aid him requitingly,</p>
<p>When war assaults him serve him as liegemen:</p>
<p>25</p>
<p>By praise-worthy actions must honor be got</p>
<p>’Mong all of the races. At the hour that was fated</p>
<p>Scyld dies at the hour appointed by Fate.</p>
<p>Scyld then departed to the All-Father’s keeping</p>
<p>Warlike to wend him; away then they bare him</p>
<p>To the flood of the current, his fond-loving comrades,</p>
<p>30</p>
<p>As himself he had bidden, while the friend of the Scyldings</p>
<p>Word-sway wielded, and the well-lovèd land-prince</p>
<p>Long did rule them.<sup>3</sup> The ring-stemmèd vessel,</p>
<p>Bark of the atheling, lay there at anchor,</p>
<p>Icy in glimmer and eager for sailing;</p>
<p>By his own request, his body is laid on a vessel and wafted seaward. 35</p>
<p>The belovèd leader laid they down there,</p>
<p>Giver of rings, on the breast of the vessel,</p>
<p>The famed by the mainmast. A many of jewels,</p>
<p>Of fretted embossings, from far-lands brought over,</p>
<p>Was placed near at hand then; and heard I not ever</p>
<p>40</p>
<p>That a folk ever furnished a float more superbly</p>
<p>With weapons of warfare, weeds for the battle,</p>
<p>Bills and burnies; on his bosom sparkled</p>
<p>Many a jewel that with him must travel</p>
<p>On the flush of the flood afar on the current.</p>
<p>45</p>
<p>And favors no fewer they furnished him soothly,</p>
<p>Excellent folk-gems, than others had given him</p>
<p>He leaves Daneland on the breast of a bark.</p>
<p>Who when first he was born outward did send him</p>
<p>Lone on the main, the merest of infants:</p>
<p>And a gold-fashioned standard they stretched under heaven</p>
<p>50</p>
<p>High o’er his head, let the holm-currents bear him,</p>
<p>Seaward consigned him: sad was their spirit,</p>
<p>Their mood very mournful. Men are not able</p>
<p>No one knows whither the boat drifted.</p>
<p>Soothly to tell us, they in halls who reside,<sup>4</sup></p>
<p>Heroes under heaven, to what haven he hied.</p>
<p>[1] For the ‘Þæt’ of verse 15, Sievers suggests ‘Þá’ (= which). If this be accepted, the sentence ‘He had … afflicted’ will read: <i>He</i> (<i>i.e.</i> God) <i>had perceived the malice-caused sorrow which they, lordless, had formerly long endured</i>.</p>
<p>[2] For ‘aldor-léase’ (15) Gr. suggested ‘aldor-ceare’: <i>He perceived their distress, that they formerly had suffered life-sorrow a long while</i>.</p>
<p>[3] A very difficult passage. ‘Áhte’ (31) has no object. H. supplies ‘geweald’ from the context; and our translation is based upon this assumption, though it is far from satisfactory. Kl. suggests ‘lændagas’ for ‘lange’: <i>And the beloved land-prince enjoyed (had) his transitory days (i.e. lived)</i>. B. suggests a dislocation; but this is a dangerous doctrine, pushed rather far by that eminent scholar.</p>
<p>[4] The reading of the H.-So. text has been quite closely followed; but some eminent scholars read ‘séle-rædenne’ for ‘sele-rædende.’ If that be adopted, the passage will read: <i>Men cannot tell us, indeed, the order of Fate, etc.</i> ‘Sele-rædende’ has two things to support it: (1) v. 1347; (2) it affords a parallel to ‘men’ in v. 50.</p>
