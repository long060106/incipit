<p>Beowulf courteously replies.</p>
<p>The chief of the strangers rendered him answer,</p>
<p>War-troopers’ leader, and word-treasure opened:</p>
<p>We are Geats.</p>
<p>“We are sprung from the lineage of the people of Geatland,</p>
<p>And Higelac’s hearth-friends. To heroes unnumbered</p>
<p>My father Ecgtheow was well-known in his day. 5</p>
<p>My father was known, a noble head-warrior</p>
<p>Ecgtheow titled; many a winter</p>
<p>He lived with the people, ere he passed on his journey,</p>
<p>Old from his dwelling; each of the counsellors</p>
<p>Widely mid world-folk well remembers him.</p>
<p>Our intentions towards King Hrothgar are of the kindest. 10</p>
<p>We, kindly of spirit, the lord of thy people,</p>
<p>The son of King Healfdene, have come here to visit,</p>
<p>Folk-troop’s defender: be free in thy counsels!</p>
<p>To the noble one bear we a weighty commission,</p>
<p>The helm of the Danemen; we shall hide, I ween,</p>
<p>Is it true that a monster is slaying Danish heroes? 15</p>
<p>Naught of our message. Thou know’st if it happen,</p>
<p>As we soothly heard say, that some savage despoiler,</p>
<p>Some hidden pursuer, on nights that are murky</p>
<p>By deeds very direful ’mid the Danemen exhibits</p>
<p>Hatred unheard of, horrid destruction</p>
<p>20</p>
<p>And the falling of dead. From feelings least selfish</p>
<p>I can help your king to free himself from this horrible creature.</p>
<p>I am able to render counsel to Hrothgar,</p>
<p>How he, wise and worthy, may worst the destroyer,</p>
<p>If the anguish of sorrow should ever be lessened,<sup>1</sup></p>
<p>Comfort come to him, and care-waves grow cooler,</p>
<p>25</p>
<p>Or ever hereafter he agony suffer</p>
<p>And troublous distress, while towereth upward</p>
<p>The handsomest of houses high on the summit.”</p>
<p>The coast-guard reminds Beowulf that it is easier to say than to do.</p>
<p>Bestriding his stallion, the strand-watchman answered,</p>
<p>The doughty retainer: “The difference surely</p>
<p>30</p>
<p>’Twixt words and works, the warlike shield-bearer</p>
<p>Who judgeth wisely well shall determine.</p>
<p>This band, I hear, beareth no malice</p>
<p>I am satisfied of your good intentions, and shall lead you to the palace.</p>
<p>To the prince of the Scyldings. Pass ye then onward</p>
<p>With weapons and armor. I shall lead you in person;</p>
<p>35</p>
<p>To my war-trusty vassals command I shall issue</p>
<p>To keep from all injury your excellent vessel,</p>
<p>Your boat shall be well cared for during your stay here.</p>
<p>Your fresh-tarred craft, ’gainst every opposer</p>
<p>Close by the sea-shore, till the curved-neckèd bark shall</p>
<p>Waft back again the well-beloved hero</p>
<p>40</p>
<p>O’er the way of the water to Weder dominions.</p>
<p>He again compliments Beowulf.</p>
<p>To warrior so great ’twill be granted sure</p>
<p>In the storm of strife to stand secure.”</p>
<p>Onward they fared then (the vessel lay quiet,</p>
<p>The broad-bosomed bark was bound by its cable,</p>
<p>45</p>
<p>Firmly at anchor); the boar-signs glistened<sup>2</sup></p>
<p>Bright on the visors vivid with gilding,</p>
<p>Blaze-hardened, brilliant; the boar acted warden.</p>
<p>The heroes hastened, hurried the liegemen,</p>
<p>The land is perhaps rolling.</p>
<p>Descended together, till they saw the great palace,</p>
<p>50</p>
<p>The well-fashioned wassail-hall wondrous and gleaming:</p>
<p>Heorot flashes on their view.</p>
<p>’Mid world-folk and kindreds that was widest reputed</p>
<p>Of halls under heaven which the hero abode in;</p>
<p>Its lustre enlightened lands without number.</p>
<p>Then the battle-brave hero showed them the glittering</p>
<p>55</p>
<p>Court of the bold ones, that they easily thither</p>
<p>Might fare on their journey; the aforementioned warrior</p>
<p>Turning his courser, quoth as he left them:</p>
<p>The coast-guard, having discharged his duty, bids them God-speed.</p>
<p>“’Tis time I were faring; Father Almighty</p>
<p>Grant you His grace, and give you to journey</p>
<p>60</p>
<p>Safe on your mission! To the sea I will get me</p>
<p>’Gainst hostile warriors as warden to stand.”</p>
<p>[1] ‘Edwendan’ (280) B. takes to be the subs. ‘edwenden’ (cf. 1775); and ‘bisigu’ he takes as gen. sing., limiting ‘edwenden’: <i>If reparation for sorrows is ever to come</i>. This is supported by t.B.</p>
<p>[2] Combining the emendations of B. and t.B., we may read: <i>The boar-images glistened … brilliant, protected the life of the war-mooded man</i>. They read ‘ferh-wearde’ (305) and ‘gúðmódgum men’ (306).</p>
