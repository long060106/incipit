<p>Wiglaf defends Beowulf.</p>
<p>Then I heard that at need of the king of the people</p>
<p>The upstanding earlman exhibited prowess,</p>
<p>Vigor and courage, as suited his nature;</p>
<p><sup>1</sup>He his head did not guard, but the high-minded liegeman’s</p>
<p>5</p>
<p>Hand was consumed, when he succored his kinsman,</p>
<p>So he struck the strife-bringing strange-comer lower,</p>
<p>Earl-thane in armor, that <i>in</i> went the weapon</p>
<p>Gleaming and plated, that ’gan then the fire<sup>2</sup></p>
<p>Beowulf draws his knife,</p>
<p>Later to lessen. The liegelord himself then</p>
<p>10</p>
<p>Retained his consciousness, brandished his war-knife,</p>
<p>Battle-sharp, bitter, that he bare on his armor:</p>
<p>and cuts the dragon.</p>
<p>The Weder-lord cut the worm in the middle.</p>
<p>They had felled the enemy (life drove out then<sup>3</sup></p>
<p>Puissant prowess), the pair had destroyed him,</p>
<p>15</p>
<p>Land-chiefs related: so a liegeman should prove him,</p>
<p>A thaneman when needed. To the prince ’twas the last of</p>
<p>His era of conquest by his own great achievements,</p>
<p>Beowulf’s wound swells and burns.</p>
<p>The latest of world-deeds. The wound then began</p>
<p>Which the earth-dwelling dragon erstwhile had wrought him</p>
<p>20</p>
<p>To burn and to swell. He soon then discovered</p>
<p>That bitterest bale-woe in his bosom was raging,</p>
<p>Poison within. The atheling advanced then,</p>
<p>He sits down exhausted.</p>
<p>That along by the wall, he prudent of spirit</p>
<p>Might sit on a settle; he saw the giant-work,</p>
<p>25</p>
<p>How arches of stone strengthened with pillars</p>
<p>The earth-hall eternal inward supported.</p>
<p>Then the long-worthy liegeman laved with his hand the</p>
<p>Wiglaf bathes his lord’s head.</p>
<p>Far-famous chieftain, gory from sword-edge,</p>
<p>Refreshing the face of his friend-lord and ruler,</p>
<p>30</p>
<p>Sated with battle, unbinding his helmet.</p>
<p>Beowulf answered, of his injury spake he,</p>
<p>His wound that was fatal (he was fully aware</p>
<p>He had lived his allotted life-days enjoying</p>
<p>The pleasures of earth; then past was entirely</p>
<p>35</p>
<p>His measure of days, death very near):</p>
<p>Beowulf regrets that he has no son.</p>
<p>“My son I would give now my battle-equipments,</p>
<p>Had any of heirs been after me granted,</p>
<p>Along of my body. This people I governed</p>
<p>Fifty of winters: no king ’mong my neighbors</p>
<p>40</p>
<p>Dared to encounter me with comrades-in-battle,</p>
<p>Try me with terror. The time to me ordered</p>
<p>I bided at home, mine own kept fitly,</p>
<p>Sought me no snares, swore me not many</p>
<p>I can rejoice in a well-spent life.</p>
<p>Oaths in injustice. Joy over all this</p>
<p>45</p>
<p>I’m able to have, though ill with my death-wounds;</p>
<p>Hence the Ruler of Earthmen need not charge me</p>
<p>With the killing of kinsmen, when cometh my life out</p>
<p>Forth from my body. Fare thou with haste now</p>
<p>Bring me the hoard, Wiglaf, that my dying eyes may be refreshed by a sight of it.</p>
<p>To behold the hoard ’neath the hoar-grayish stone,</p>
<p>50</p>
<p>Well-lovèd Wiglaf, now the worm is a-lying,</p>
<p>Sore-wounded sleepeth, disseized of his treasure.</p>
<p>Go thou in haste that treasures of old I,</p>
<p>Gold-wealth may gaze on, together see lying</p>
<p>The ether-bright jewels, be easier able,</p>
<p>55</p>
<p>Having the heap of hoard-gems, to yield my</p>
<p>Life and the land-folk whom long I have governed.”</p>
<p>[1] B. renders: <i>He</i> (<i>W</i>.) did not regard his (<i>the dragon’s</i>) <i>head</i> (since Beowulf had struck it without effect), <i>but struck the dragon a little lower down.—</i>One crux is to find out <i>whose head</i> is meant; another is to bring out the antithesis between ‘head’ and ‘hand.’</p>
<p>[2] ‘Þæt þæt fýr’ (2702), S. emends to ‘þá þæt fýr’ = <i>when the fire began to grow less intense afterward</i>. This emendation relieves the passage of a plethora of conjunctive <i>þæt</i>’s.</p>
<p>[3] For ‘gefyldan’ (2707), S. proposes ‘gefylde.’ The passage would read: <i>He felled the foe (life drove out strength), and they then both had destroyed him, chieftains related</i>. This gives Beowulf the credit of having felled the dragon; then they combine to annihilate him.—For ‘ellen’ (2707), Kl. suggests ‘e(a)llne.’—The reading ‘<i>life drove out strength</i>’ is very unsatisfactory and very peculiar. I would suggest as follows: Adopt S.’s emendation, remove H.’s parenthesis, read ‘ferh-ellen wræc,’ and translate: <i>He felled the foe, drove out his life-strength</i> (that is, made him <i>hors de combat</i>), <i>and then they both, etc</i>.</p>
