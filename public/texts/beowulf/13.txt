<p>Hrothgar responds.</p>
<p>Hrothgar discoursed, helm of the Scyldings:</p>
<p>“To defend our folk and to furnish assistance,<sup>1</sup></p>
<p>Thou soughtest us hither, good friend Beowulf.</p>
<p>Reminiscences of Beowulf’s father, Ecgtheow.</p>
<p>The fiercest of feuds thy father engaged in,</p>
<p>5</p>
<p>Heatholaf killed he in hand-to-hand conflict</p>
<p>’Mid Wilfingish warriors; then the Wederish people</p>
<p>For fear of a feud were forced to disown him.</p>
<p>Thence flying he fled to the folk of the South-Danes,</p>
<p>The race of the Scyldings, o’er the roll of the waters;</p>
<p>10</p>
<p>I had lately begun then to govern the Danemen,</p>
<p>The hoard-seat of heroes held in my youth,</p>
<p>Rich in its jewels: dead was Heregar,</p>
<p>My kinsman and elder had earth-joys forsaken,</p>
<p>Healfdene his bairn. He was better than I am!</p>
<p>15</p>
<p>That feud thereafter for a fee I compounded;</p>
<p>O’er the weltering waters to the Wilfings I sent</p>
<p>Ornaments old; oaths did he swear me.</p>
<p>Hrothgar recounts to Beowulf the horrors of Grendel’s persecutions.</p>
<p>It pains me in spirit to any to tell it,</p>
<p>What grief in Heorot Grendel hath caused me,</p>
<p>20</p>
<p>What horror unlooked-for, by hatred unceasing.</p>
<p>Waned is my war-band, wasted my hall-troop;</p>
<p>Weird hath offcast them to the clutches of Grendel.</p>
<p>God can easily hinder the scather</p>
<p>From deeds so direful. Oft drunken with beer</p>
<p>My thanes have made many boasts, but have not executed them. 25</p>
<p>O’er the ale-vessel promised warriors in armor</p>
<p>They would willingly wait on the wassailing-benches</p>
<p>A grapple with Grendel, with grimmest of edges.</p>
<p>Then this mead-hall at morning with murder was reeking,</p>
<p>The building was bloody at breaking of daylight,</p>
<p>30</p>
<p>The bench-deals all flooded, dripping and bloodied,</p>
<p>The folk-hall was gory: I had fewer retainers,</p>
<p>Dear-beloved warriors, whom death had laid hold of.</p>
<p>Sit down to the feast, and give us comfort.</p>
<p>Sit at the feast now, thy intents unto heroes,<sup>2</sup></p>
<p>Thy victor-fame show, as thy spirit doth urge thee!”</p>
<p>A bench is made ready for Beowulf and his party. 35</p>
<p>For the men of the Geats then together assembled,</p>
<p>In the beer-hall blithesome a bench was made ready;</p>
<p>There warlike in spirit they went to be seated,</p>
<p>Proud and exultant. A liegeman did service,</p>
<p>Who a beaker embellished bore with decorum,</p>
<p>The gleeman sings 40</p>
<p>And gleaming-drink poured. The gleeman sang whilom</p>
<p>The heroes all rejoice together.</p>
<p>Hearty in Heorot; there was heroes’ rejoicing,</p>
<p>A numerous war-band of Weders and Danemen.</p>
<p>[1] B. and S. reject the reading given in H.-So., and suggested by Grtvg. B. suggests for 457-458:</p>
<blockquote>wáere-ryhtum Þú, wine mín Béowulf, and for ár-stafum úsic sóhtest.</blockquote>
<p>This means: <i>From the obligations of clientage, my friend Beowulf, and for assistance thou hast sought us</i>.—This gives coherence to Hrothgar’s opening remarks in VIII., and also introduces a new motive for Beowulf’s coming to Hrothgar’s aid.</p>
<p>[2] <i>Sit now at the feast, and disclose thy purposes to the victorious heroes, as thy spirit urges</i>.—Kl. reaches the above translation by erasing the comma after ‘meoto’ and reading ‘sige-hrèðsecgum.’—There are other and bolder emendations and suggestions. Of these the boldest is to regard ‘meoto’ as a verb (imperative), and read ‘on sæl’: <i>Think upon gayety, etc</i>.—All the renderings are unsatisfactory, the one given in our translation involving a zeugma.</p>
