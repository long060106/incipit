<p>Hrothgar sees no way of escape from the persecutions of Grendel.</p>
<p>So Healfdene’s kinsman constantly mused on</p>
<p>His long-lasting sorrow; the battle-thane clever</p>
<p>Was not anywise able evils to ’scape from:</p>
<p>Too crushing the sorrow that came to the people,</p>
<p>5</p>
<p>Loathsome and lasting the life-grinding torture,</p>
<p>Beowulf, the Geat, hero of the poem, hears of Hrothgar’s sorrow, and resolves to go to his assistance.</p>
<p>Greatest of night-woes. So Higelac’s liegeman,</p>
<p>Good amid Geatmen, of Grendel’s achievements</p>
<p>Heard in his home:<sup>1</sup> of heroes then living</p>
<p>He was stoutest and strongest, sturdy and noble.</p>
<p>10</p>
<p>He bade them prepare him a bark that was trusty;</p>
<p>He said he the war-king would seek o’er the ocean,</p>
<p>The folk-leader noble, since he needed retainers.</p>
<p>For the perilous project prudent companions</p>
<p>Chided him little, though loving him dearly;</p>
<p>15</p>
<p>They egged the brave atheling, augured him glory.</p>
<p>With fourteen carefully chosen companions, he sets out for Dane-land.</p>
<p>The excellent knight from the folk of the Geatmen</p>
<p>Had liegemen selected, likest to prove them</p>
<p>Trustworthy warriors; with fourteen companions</p>
<p>The vessel he looked for; a liegeman then showed them,</p>
<p>20</p>
<p>A sea-crafty man, the bounds of the country.</p>
<p>Fast the days fleeted; the float was a-water,</p>
<p>The craft by the cliff. Clomb to the prow then</p>
<p>Well-equipped warriors: the wave-currents twisted</p>
<p>The sea on the sand; soldiers then carried</p>
<p>25</p>
<p>On the breast of the vessel bright-shining jewels,</p>
<p>Handsome war-armor; heroes outshoved then,</p>
<p>Warmen the wood-ship, on its wished-for adventure.</p>
<p>The vessel sails like a bird</p>
<p>The foamy-necked floater fanned by the breeze,</p>
<p>Likest a bird, glided the waters,</p>
<p>In twenty four hours they reach the shores of Hrothgar’s dominions 30</p>
<p>Till twenty and four hours thereafter</p>
<p>The twist-stemmed vessel had traveled such distance</p>
<p>That the sailing-men saw the sloping embankments,</p>
<p>The sea cliffs gleaming, precipitous mountains,</p>
<p>Nesses enormous: they were nearing the limits</p>
<p>35</p>
<p>At the end of the ocean.<sup>2</sup> Up thence quickly</p>
<p>The men of the Weders clomb to the mainland,</p>
<p>Fastened their vessel (battle weeds rattled,</p>
<p>War burnies clattered), the Wielder they thanked</p>
<p>That the ways o’er the waters had waxen so gentle.</p>
<p>They are hailed by the Danish coast guard 40</p>
<p>Then well from the cliff edge the guard of the Scyldings</p>
<p>Who the sea-cliffs should see to, saw o’er the gangway</p>
<p>Brave ones bearing beauteous targets,</p>
<p>Armor all ready, anxiously thought he,</p>
<p>Musing and wondering what men were approaching.</p>
<p>45</p>
<p>High on his horse then Hrothgar’s retainer</p>
<p>Turned him to coastward, mightily brandished</p>
<p>His lance in his hands, questioned with boldness.</p>
<p>His challenge</p>
<p>“Who are ye men here, mail-covered warriors</p>
<p>Clad in your corslets, come thus a-driving</p>
<p>50</p>
<p>A high riding ship o’er the shoals of the waters,</p>
<p><sup>3</sup>And hither ’neath helmets have hied o’er the ocean?</p>
<p>I have been strand-guard, standing as warden,</p>
<p>Lest enemies ever anywise ravage</p>
<p>Danish dominions with army of war-ships.</p>
<p>55</p>
<p>More boldly never have warriors ventured</p>
<p>Hither to come; of kinsmen’s approval,</p>
<p>Word-leave of warriors, I ween that ye surely</p>
<p>He is struck by Beowulf’s appearance.</p>
<p>Nothing have known. Never a greater one</p>
<p>Of earls o’er the earth have <i>I</i> had a sight of</p>
<p>60</p>
<p>Than is one of your number, a hero in armor;</p>
<p>No low-ranking fellow<sup>4</sup> adorned with his weapons,</p>
<p>But launching them little, unless looks are deceiving,</p>
<p>And striking appearance. Ere ye pass on your journey</p>
<p>As treacherous spies to the land of the Scyldings</p>
<p>65</p>
<p>And farther fare, I fully must know now</p>
<p>What race ye belong to. Ye far-away dwellers,</p>
<p>Sea-faring sailors, my simple opinion</p>
<p>Hear ye and hearken: haste is most fitting</p>
<p>Plainly to tell me what place ye are come from.”</p>
<p>[1] ‘From hám’ (194) is much disputed. One rendering is: <i>Beowulf, being away from home, heard of Hrothgar’s troubles, etc</i>. Another, that adopted by S. and endorsed in the H.-So. notes, is: <i>B. heard from his neighborhood (neighbors),</i> i.e. <i>in his home, etc</i>. A third is: <i>B., being at home, heard this as occurring away from home</i>. The H.-So. glossary and notes conflict.</p>
<p>[2] ‘Eoletes’ (224) is marked with a (?) by H.-So.; our rendering simply follows his conjecture.—Other conjectures as to ‘eolet’ are: (1) <i>voyage</i>, (2) <i>toil</i>, <i>labor</i>, (3) <i>hasty journey</i>.</p>
<p>[3] The lacuna of the MS at this point has been supplied by various conjectures. The reading adopted by H.-So. has been rendered in the above translation. W., like H.-So., makes ‘ic’ the beginning of a new sentence, but, for ‘helmas bæron,’ he reads ‘hringed stefnan.’ This has the advantage of giving a parallel to ‘brontne ceol’ instead of a kenning for ‘go.’—B puts the (?) after ‘holmas’, and begins a new sentence at the middle of the line. Translate: <i>What warriors are ye, clad in armor, who have thus come bringing the foaming vessel over the water way, hither over the seas? For some time on the wall I have been coast guard, etc</i>. S. endorses most of what B. says, but leaves out ‘on the wall’ in the last sentence. If W.’s ‘hringed stefnan’ be accepted, change line 51 above to, <i>A ring-stemmed vessel hither o’ersea</i>.</p>
<p>[4] ‘Seld-guma’ (249) is variously rendered: (1) <i>housecarle</i>; (2) <i>home-stayer</i>; (3) <i>common man</i>. Dr. H. Wood suggests <i>a man-at-arms in another’s house</i>.</p>
