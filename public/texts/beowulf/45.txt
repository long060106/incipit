<p>Wiglaf sends the news of Beowulf’s death to liegemen near by.</p>
<p>Then he charged that the battle be announced at the hedge</p>
<p>Up o’er the cliff-edge, where the earl-troopers bided</p>
<p>The whole of the morning, mood-wretched sat them,</p>
<p>Bearers of battle-shields, both things expecting,</p>
<p>5</p>
<p>The end of his lifetime and the coming again of</p>
<p>The liegelord belovèd. Little reserved he</p>
<p>Of news that was known, who the ness-cliff did travel,</p>
<p>But he truly discoursed to all that could hear him:</p>
<p>The messenger speaks.</p>
<p>“Now the free-giving friend-lord of the folk of the Weders,</p>
<p>10</p>
<p>The folk-prince of Geatmen, is fast in his death-bed,</p>
<p>By the deeds of the dragon in death-bed abideth;</p>
<p>Along with him lieth his life-taking foeman</p>
<p>Slain with knife-wounds: he was wholly unable</p>
<p>To injure at all the ill-planning monster</p>
<p>Wiglaf sits by our dead lord. 15</p>
<p>With bite of his sword-edge. Wiglaf is sitting,</p>
<p>Offspring of Wihstan, up over Beowulf,</p>
<p>Earl o’er another whose end-day hath reached him,</p>
<p>Head-watch holdeth o’er heroes unliving,<sup>1</sup></p>
<p>Our lord’s death will lead to attacks from our old foes.</p>
<p>For friend and for foeman. The folk now expecteth</p>
<p>20</p>
<p>A season of strife when the death of the folk-king</p>
<p>To Frankmen and Frisians in far-lands is published.</p>
<p>The war-hatred waxed warm ’gainst the Hugmen,</p>
<p>Higelac’s death recalled.</p>
<p>When Higelac came with an army of vessels</p>
<p>Faring to Friesland, where the Frankmen in battle</p>
<p>25</p>
<p>Humbled him and bravely with overmight ’complished</p>
<p>That the mail-clad warrior must sink in the battle,</p>
<p>Fell ’mid his folk-troop: no fret-gems presented</p>
<p>The atheling to earlmen; aye was denied us</p>
<p>Merewing’s mercy. The men of the Swedelands</p>
<p>30</p>
<p>For truce or for truth trust I but little;</p>
<p>But widely ’twas known that near Ravenswood Ongentheow</p>
<p>Hæthcyn’s fall referred to.</p>
<p>Sundered Hæthcyn the Hrethling from life-joys,</p>
<p>When for pride overweening the War-Scylfings first did</p>
<p>Seek the Geatmen with savage intentions.</p>
<p>35</p>
<p>Early did Ohthere’s age-laden father,</p>
<p>Old and terrible, give blow in requital,</p>
<p>Killing the sea-king, the queen-mother rescued,</p>
<p>The old one his consort deprived of her gold,</p>
<p>Onela’s mother and Ohthere’s also,</p>
<p>40</p>
<p>And then followed the feud-nursing foemen till hardly,</p>
<p>Reaved of their ruler, they Ravenswood entered.</p>
<p>Then with vast-numbered forces he assaulted the remnant,</p>
<p>Weary with wounds, woe often promised</p>
<p>The livelong night to the sad-hearted war-troop:</p>
<p>45</p>
<p>Said he at morning would kill them with edges of weapons,</p>
<p>Some on the gallows for glee to the fowls.</p>
<p>Aid came after to the anxious-in-spirit</p>
<p>At dawn of the day, after Higelac’s bugle</p>
<p>And trumpet-sound heard they, when the good one proceeded</p>
<p>50</p>
<p>And faring followed the flower of the troopers.</p>
<p>[1] ‘Hige-méðum’ (2910) is glossed by H. as dat. plu. (= for the dead). S. proposes ‘hige-méðe,’ nom. sing. limiting Wigláf; i.e. <i>W., mood-weary, holds head-watch o’er friend and foe</i>.—B. suggests taking the word as dat. inst. plu. of an abstract noun in -‘u.’ The translation would be substantially the same as S.’s.</p>
