<p>A wounded spirit.</p>
<p>“Then bruised in his bosom he with bitter-toothed missile</p>
<p>Is hurt ’neath his helmet: from harmful pollution</p>
<p>He is powerless to shield him by the wonderful mandates</p>
<p>Of the loath-cursèd spirit; what too long he hath holden</p>
<p>5</p>
<p>Him seemeth too small, savage he hoardeth,</p>
<p>Nor boastfully giveth gold-plated rings,<sup>1</sup></p>
<p>The fate of the future flouts and forgetteth</p>
<p>Since God had erst given him greatness no little,</p>
<p>Wielder of Glory. His end-day anear,</p>
<p>10</p>
<p>It afterward happens that the bodily-dwelling</p>
<p>Fleetingly fadeth, falls into ruins;</p>
<p>Another lays hold who doleth the ornaments,</p>
<p>The nobleman’s jewels, nothing lamenting,</p>
<p>Heedeth no terror. Oh, Beowulf dear,</p>
<p>15</p>
<p>Best of the heroes, from bale-strife defend thee,</p>
<p>And choose thee the better, counsels eternal;</p>
<p>Be not over proud: life is fleeting, and its strength soon wasteth away.</p>
<p>Beware of arrogance, world-famous champion!</p>
<p>But a little-while lasts thy life-vigor’s fulness;</p>
<p>’Twill after hap early, that illness or sword-edge</p>
<p>20</p>
<p>Shall part thee from strength, or the grasp of the fire,</p>
<p>Or the wave of the current, or clutch of the edges,</p>
<p>Or flight of the war-spear, or age with its horrors,</p>
<p>Or thine eyes’ bright flashing shall fade into darkness:</p>
<p>’Twill happen full early, excellent hero,</p>
<p>Hrothgar gives an account of his reign. 25</p>
<p>That death shall subdue thee. So the Danes a half-century</p>
<p>I held under heaven, helped them in struggles</p>
<p>’Gainst many a race in middle-earth’s regions,</p>
<p>With ash-wood and edges, that enemies none</p>
<p>On earth molested me. Lo! offsetting change, now,</p>
<p>Sorrow after joy. 30</p>
<p>Came to my manor, grief after joyance,</p>
<p>When Grendel became my constant visitor,</p>
<p>Inveterate hater: I from that malice</p>
<p>Continually travailed with trouble no little.</p>
<p>Thanks be to God that I gained in my lifetime,</p>
<p>35</p>
<p>To the Lord everlasting, to look on the gory</p>
<p>Head with mine eyes, after long-lasting sorrow!</p>
<p>Go to the bench now, battle-adornèd</p>
<p>Joy in the feasting: of jewels in common</p>
<p>We’ll meet with many when morning appeareth.”</p>
<p>40</p>
<p>The Geatman was gladsome, ganged he immediately</p>
<p>To go to the bench, as the clever one bade him.</p>
<p>Then again as before were the famous-for-prowess,</p>
<p>Hall-inhabiters, handsomely banqueted,</p>
<p>Feasted anew. The night-veil fell then</p>
<p>45</p>
<p>Dark o’er the warriors. The courtiers rose then;</p>
<p>The gray-haired was anxious to go to his slumbers,</p>
<p>The hoary old Scylding. Hankered the Geatman,</p>
<p>Beowulf is fagged, and seeks rest.</p>
<p>The champion doughty, greatly, to rest him:</p>
<p>An earlman early outward did lead him,</p>
<p>50</p>
<p>Fagged from his faring, from far-country springing,</p>
<p>Who for etiquette’s sake all of a liegeman’s</p>
<p>Needs regarded, such as seamen at that time</p>
<p>Were bounden to feel. The big-hearted rested;</p>
<p>The building uptowered, spacious and gilded,</p>
<p>55</p>
<p>The guest within slumbered, till the sable-clad raven</p>
<p>Blithely foreboded the beacon of heaven.</p>
<p>Then the bright-shining sun o’er the bottoms came going;<sup>2</sup></p>
<p>The warriors hastened, the heads of the peoples</p>
<p>Were ready to go again to their peoples,</p>
<p>The Geats prepare to leave Dane-land. 60</p>
<p>The high-mooded farer would faraway thenceward</p>
<p>Look for his vessel. The valiant one bade then,<sup>3</sup></p>
<p>Unferth asks Beowulf to accept his sword as a gift. Beowulf thanks him.</p>
<p>Offspring of Ecglaf, off to bear Hrunting,</p>
<p>To take his weapon, his well-beloved iron;</p>
<p>He him thanked for the gift, saying good he accounted</p>
<p>65</p>
<p>The war-friend and mighty, nor chid he with words then</p>
<p>The blade of the brand: ’twas a brave-mooded hero.</p>
<p>When the warriors were ready, arrayed in their trappings,</p>
<p>The atheling dear to the Danemen advanced then</p>
<p>On to the dais, where the other was sitting,</p>
<p>70</p>
<p>Grim-mooded hero, greeted King Hrothgar.</p>
<p>[1] K. says ‘<i>proudly giveth</i>.’—Gr. says, ‘<i>And gives no gold-plated rings, in order to incite the recipient to boastfulness</i>.’—B. suggests ‘gyld’ for ‘gylp,’ and renders: <i>And gives no beaten rings for reward</i>.</p>
<p>[2] If S.’s emendation be accepted, v. 57 will read: <i>Then came the light, going bright after darkness: the warriors, etc</i>.</p>
<p>[3] As the passage stands in H.-So., Unferth presents Beowulf with the sword Hrunting, and B. thanks him for the gift. If, however, the suggestions of Grdtvg. and M. be accepted, the passage will read: <i>Then the brave one (</i>i.e.<i> Beowulf) commanded that Hrunting be borne to the son of Ecglaf (Unferth), bade him take his sword, his dear weapon; he (B.) thanked him (U.) for the loan, etc</i>.</p>
