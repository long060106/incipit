<p>Hrothgar laments the death of Æschere, his shoulder-companion.</p>
<p>Hrothgar rejoined, helm of the Scyldings:</p>
<p>“Ask not of joyance! Grief is renewed to</p>
<p>The folk of the Danemen. Dead is Æschere,</p>
<p>Yrmenlaf’s brother, older than he,</p>
<p>5</p>
<p>My true-hearted counsellor, trusty adviser,</p>
<p>Shoulder-companion, when fighting in battle</p>
<p>Our heads we protected, when troopers were clashing,</p>
<p>He was my ideal hero.</p>
<p>And heroes were dashing; such an earl should be ever,</p>
<p>An erst-worthy atheling, as Æschere proved him.</p>
<p>10</p>
<p>The flickering death-spirit became in Heorot</p>
<p>His hand-to-hand murderer; I can not tell whither</p>
<p>The cruel one turned in the carcass exulting,</p>
<p>This horrible creature came to avenge Grendel’s death.</p>
<p>By cramming discovered.<sup>1</sup> The quarrel she wreaked then,</p>
<p>That last night igone Grendel thou killedst</p>
<p>15</p>
<p>In grewsomest manner, with grim-holding clutches,</p>
<p>Since too long he had lessened my liege-troop and wasted</p>
<p>My folk-men so foully. He fell in the battle</p>
<p>With forfeit of life, and another has followed,</p>
<p>A mighty crime-worker, her kinsman avenging,</p>
<p>20</p>
<p>And henceforth hath ‘stablished her hatred unyielding,<sup>2</sup></p>
<p>As it well may appear to many a liegeman,</p>
<p>Who mourneth in spirit the treasure-bestower,</p>
<p>Her heavy heart-sorrow; the hand is now lifeless</p>
<p>Which<sup>3</sup> availed you in every wish that you cherished.</p>
<p>I have heard my vassals speak of these two uncanny monsters who lived in the moors. 25</p>
<p>Land-people heard I, liegemen, this saying,</p>
<p>Dwellers in halls, they had seen very often</p>
<p>A pair of such mighty march-striding creatures,</p>
<p>Far-dwelling spirits, holding the moorlands:</p>
<p>One of them wore, as well they might notice,</p>
<p>30</p>
<p>The image of woman, the other one wretched</p>
<p>In guise of a man wandered in exile,</p>
<p>Except he was huger than any of earthmen;</p>
<p>Earth-dwelling people entitled him Grendel</p>
<p>In days of yore: they know not their father,</p>
<p>35</p>
<p>Whe’r ill-going spirits any were borne him</p>
<p>The inhabit the most desolate and horrible places.</p>
<p>Ever before. They guard the wolf-coverts,</p>
<p>Lands inaccessible, wind-beaten nesses,</p>
<p>Fearfullest fen-deeps, where a flood from the mountains</p>
<p>’Neath mists of the nesses netherward rattles,</p>
<p>40</p>
<p>The stream under earth: not far is it henceward</p>
<p>Measured by mile-lengths that the mere-water standeth,</p>
<p>Which forests hang over, with frost-whiting covered,<sup>4</sup></p>
<p>A firm-rooted forest, the floods overshadow.</p>
<p>There ever at night one an ill-meaning portent</p>
<p>45</p>
<p>A fire-flood may see; ’mong children of men</p>
<p>None liveth so wise that wot of the bottom;</p>
<p>Though harassed by hounds the heath-stepper seek for,</p>
<p>Even the hounded deer will not seek refuge in these uncanny regions.</p>
<p>Fly to the forest, firm-antlered he-deer,</p>
<p>Spurred from afar, his spirit he yieldeth,</p>
<p>50</p>
<p>His life on the shore, ere in he will venture</p>
<p>To cover his head. Uncanny the place is:</p>
<p>Thence upward ascendeth the surging of waters,</p>
<p>Wan to the welkin, when the wind is stirring</p>
<p>The weathers unpleasing, till the air groweth gloomy,</p>
<p>To thee only can I look for assistance. 55</p>
<p>And the heavens lower. Now is help to be gotten</p>
<p>From thee and thee only! The abode thou know’st not,</p>
<p>The dangerous place where thou’rt able to meet with</p>
<p>The sin-laden hero: seek if thou darest!</p>
<p>For the feud I will fully fee thee with money,</p>
<p>60</p>
<p>With old-time treasure, as erstwhile I did thee,</p>
<p>With well-twisted jewels, if away thou shalt get thee.”</p>
<p>[1] For ‘gefrægnod’ (1334), K. and t.B. suggest ‘gefægnod,’ rendering ‘<i>rejoicing in her fill</i>.’ This gives a parallel to ‘æse wlanc’ (1333).</p>
<p>[2] The line ‘And … yielding,’ B. renders: <i>And she has performed a deed of blood-vengeance whose effect is far-reaching</i>.</p>
<p>[3] ‘Sé Þe’ (1345) is an instance of masc. rel. with fem. antecedent. So v. 1888, where ‘sé Þe’ refers to ‘yldo.’</p>
<p>[4] For ‘hrímge’ in the H.-So. edition, Gr. and others read ‘hrínde’ (=hrínende), and translate: <i>which rustling forests overhang</i>.</p>
