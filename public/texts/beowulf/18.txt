<p>Beowulf has no idea of letting Grendel live.</p>
<p>For no cause whatever would the earlmen’s defender</p>
<p>Leave in life-joys the loathsome newcomer,</p>
<p>He deemed his existence utterly useless</p>
<p>To men under heaven. Many a noble</p>
<p>5</p>
<p>Of Beowulf brandished his battle-sword old,</p>
<p>Would guard the life of his lord and protector,</p>
<p>The far-famous chieftain, if able to do so;</p>
<p>While waging the warfare, this wist they but little,</p>
<p>Brave battle-thanes, while his body intending</p>
<p>No weapon would harm Grendel; he bore a charmed life. 10</p>
<p>To slit into slivers, and seeking his spirit:</p>
<p>That the relentless foeman nor finest of weapons</p>
<p>Of all on the earth, nor any of war-bills</p>
<p>Was willing to injure; but weapons of victory</p>
<p>Swords and suchlike he had sworn to dispense with.</p>
<p>15</p>
<p>His death at that time must prove to be wretched,</p>
<p>And the far-away spirit widely should journey</p>
<p>Into enemies’ power. This plainly he saw then</p>
<p>Who with mirth<sup>1</sup> of mood malice no little</p>
<p>Had wrought in the past on the race of the earthmen</p>
<p>20</p>
<p>(To God he was hostile), that his body would fail him,</p>
<p>But Higelac’s hardy henchman and kinsman</p>
<p>Held him by the hand; hateful to other</p>
<p>Grendel is sorely wounded.</p>
<p>Was each one if living. A body-wound suffered</p>
<p>The direful demon, damage incurable</p>
<p>His body bursts. 25</p>
<p>Was seen on his shoulder, his sinews were shivered,</p>
<p>His body did burst. To Beowulf was given</p>
<p>Glory in battle; Grendel from thenceward</p>
<p>Must flee and hide him in the fen-cliffs and marshes,</p>
<p>Sick unto death, his dwelling must look for</p>
<p>30</p>
<p>Unwinsome and woful; he wist the more fully</p>
<p>The monster flees away to hide in the moors.</p>
<p>The end of his earthly existence was nearing,</p>
<p>His life-days’ limits. At last for the Danemen,</p>
<p>When the slaughter was over, their wish was accomplished.</p>
<p>The comer-from-far-land had cleansed then of evil,</p>
<p>35</p>
<p>Wise and valiant, the war-hall of Hrothgar,</p>
<p>Saved it from violence. He joyed in the night-work,</p>
<p>In repute for prowess; the prince of the Geatmen</p>
<p>For the East-Danish people his boast had accomplished,</p>
<p>Bettered their burdensome bale-sorrows fully,</p>
<p>40</p>
<p>The craft-begot evil they erstwhile had suffered</p>
<p>And were forced to endure from crushing oppression,</p>
<p>Their manifold misery. ’Twas a manifest token,</p>
<p>Beowulf suspends Grendel’s hand and arm in Heorot.</p>
<p>When the hero-in-battle the hand suspended,</p>
<p>The arm and the shoulder (there was all of the claw</p>
<p>45</p>
<p>Of Grendel together) ’neath great-stretching hall-roof.</p>
<p>[1] It has been proposed to translate ‘myrðe’ by <i>with sorrow</i>; but there seems no authority for such a rendering. To the present translator, the phrase ‘módes myrðe’ seems a mere padding for <i>gladly</i>; i.e., <i>he who gladly harassed mankind</i>.</p>
