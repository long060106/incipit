<p class="lede">Refusal of Hector to enter the city, though entreated by Priam and Hecuba⁠—His resolve to meet Achilles, and his flight when Achilles approaches⁠—Descent of Minerva to aid Achilles⁠—Deceit practised by her on Hector, when, assuming the form of his brother Deïphobus, she induces him to encounter Achilles⁠—His death⁠—His body dragged at the chariot-wheels of the victor⁠—Lament of Priam and Hecuba⁠—The news brought to Andromache while engaged at the loom⁠—Her sorrow and lamentation.</p>
<p class="verse">Thus were they driven within the city walls <br> Like frighted fawns, and there dispersing cooled <br> Their sweaty limbs, and quenched their eager thirst, <br> And rested on the battlements. The Greeks, <br> Bearing their shields upon their shoulders, came <br> Close to the ramparts. Hector’s adverse fate <br> Detained him still without the walls of Troy, <br> And near the Scaean gates. Meantime the god <br> Apollo to the son of Peleus said:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“O son of Peleus! Why pursue me thus <br> With thy swift feet⁠—a mortal man in chase <br> Of an immortal? That I am a god <br> Thou seest not yet, but turnest all thy rage <br> On me, and, having put the host of Troy <br> To rout, dost think of them no more. They find <br> A refuge in their town, while far astray <br> Thou wanderest hither. Thou hast not the power <br> To slay me; I am not of mortal birth.”</p>
<p class="verse">The swift Achilles angrily replied: <br> “O archer-god, thou most unjust of all <br> The immortals! Thou hast wronged me, luring me <br> Aside; since many a warrior I had forced <br> To bite the dust before they reached the gates <br> Of Ilium but for thee, who from my grasp <br> Hast snatched the glory and hast rescued them. <br> Thou didst not fear my vengeance; yet if power <br> Were given me, I would punish thee for this.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake, and with heroic purpose turned <br> Toward Ilium. As a steed that wins the race <br> Flies at his utmost speed across the plain, <br> And whirls along the chariot, with such speed <br> The son of Peleus moved his rapid feet.</p>
<p class="verse">The aged monarch Priam was the first <br> To see him as he scoured the plain, and shone <br> Like to the star which in the autumn time <br> Rises and glows among the lights of heaven <br> With eminent lustre at the dead of night⁠— <br> Orion’s Hound they call it⁠—bright indeed, <br> And yet of baleful omen, for it brings <br> Distressing heat to miserable men. <br> So shone the brass upon the warrior’s breast <br> As on he flew. The aged Priam groaned, <br> And smote his head with lifted hands, and called <br> Aloud, imploring his beloved son, <br> Who eagerly before the city gate <br> Waited his foe Achilles. Priam thus, <br> With outstretched hands, besought him piteously:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“O wait not, Hector, my beloved son, <br> To combat with Pelides, thus alone <br> And far from succor, lest thou meet thy death, <br> Slain by his hand, for he is mightier far <br> Than thou art. Would that he, the cruel one, <br> Were but as much the favorite of the gods <br> As he is mine! Then should the birds of prey <br> And dogs devour his carcass, and the grief <br> That weighs upon my spirit would depart. <br> I have been robbed by him of many sons⁠— <br> Brave youths, whom he has slain or sold as slaves <br> In distant isles; and now I see no more <br> Among our host on whom the gates are closed <br> My Polydorus and Lycaon, whom <br> The peerless dame Laothoe bore to me. <br> If yet they are within the Grecian camp, <br> I will redeem their lives with brass and gold; <br> For I have store, which Altes, the renowned <br> And aged, gave his daughter. If they live <br> No longer, but have passed to the abode <br> Of Hades, bitter will our sorrow be⁠— <br> Mine and their mother’s⁠—but the popular grief <br> Will sooner be consoled if thou fall not, <br> Slain by Achilles. Come within the walls, <br> My son, that thou mayst still be the defence <br> Of Ilium’s sons and daughters, nor increase <br> The glory of Pelides with the loss <br> Of thine own life. Have pity upon me, <br> Who only live to suffer⁠—whom the son <br> Of Saturn, on the threshold of my age, <br> Hath destined to endure a thousand griefs, <br> And then to be destroyed⁠—to see my sons <br> Slain by the sword, my daughters dragged away <br> Into captivity, their chambers made <br> A spoil, our infants dashed against the ground <br> By cruel hands, the consorts of my sons <br> Borne off by the ferocious Greeks; and last, <br> Perchance the very dogs which I have fed <br> Here in my palaces and at my board, <br> The guardians of my doors, when, by the spear <br> Or sword, some enemy shall take my life, <br> And at my threshold leave me stretched a corpse, <br> Will rend me, and, with savage greediness, <br> Will lap my blood, and in the porch lie down. <br> When one in prime of youth lies slain in war, <br> Gashed with the spear, his wounds become him well, <br> And honor him in all men’s eyes; but when <br> An aged man is slain, and his white head <br> And his white beard and limbs are foully torn <br> By ravening dogs, there is no sadder sight.”</p>
<p class="verse">So the old monarch spake, and with his hands <br> Tore his gray hair, but moved not Hector thus. <br> Then came, with lamentations and in tears, <br> The warrior’s mother forward. One hand laid <br> Her bosom bare; she pressed the other hand <br> Beneath it, sobbed, and spake these wingèd words:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Revere this bosom, Hector, and on me <br> Have pity. If when thou wert but a babe <br> I ever on this bosom stilled thy cries, <br> Think of it now, beloved child; avoid <br> That dreadful chief; withdraw within the walls, <br> Nor madly think to encounter him alone, <br> Son of my love and of my womb! If he <br> Should slay thee, I shall not lament thy death <br> Above thy bier⁠—I, nor thy noble wife⁠— <br> But far from us the greedy dogs will throng <br> To mangle thee beside the Grecian fleet.”</p>
<p class="verse">Thus, weeping bitterly, the aged pair <br> Entreated their dear son, yet moved him not. <br> He stood and waited for his mighty foe <br> Achilles, as a serpent at his den, <br> Fed on the poisons of the wild, awaits <br> The traveller, and, fierce with hate of man, <br> And glaring fearfully, lies coiled within. <br> So waited Hector with a resolute heart, <br> And kept his ground, and, leaning his bright shield <br> Against a tower that jutted from the walls, <br> Conferred with his great soul impatiently:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Ah me! If I should pass within the walls, <br> Then will Polydamas be first to cast <br> Reproach upon me; for he counselled me <br> To lead the Trojans back into the town <br> That fatal night which saw Achilles rise <br> To join the war again. I yielded not <br> To his advice; far better if I had. <br> Now, since my fatal stubbornness has brought <br> This ruin on my people, I most dread <br> The censure of the men and long-robed dames <br> Of Ilium. Men less brave than I will say, <br> ‘Foolhardy Hector in his pride has thrown <br> His people’s lives away.’ So will they speak, <br> And better were it for me to return, <br> Achilles slain, or, slain myself by him, <br> To perish for my country gloriously. <br> But should I lay aside this bossy shield <br> And this stout helm, and lean against the wall <br> This spear, and go to meet the gallant son <br> Of Peleus, with a promise to restore <br> Helen and all the treasure brought with her <br> To Troy by Paris, in his roomy ships⁠— <br> All that the war was waged for⁠—that the sons <br> Of Atreus may convey it hence, besides <br> Wealth drawn from all the hoards within the town, <br> And to be shared among the Greeks; for I <br> Would bind the Trojans by a solemn oath <br> To keep back nothing, but divide the whole⁠— <br> Whate’er of riches this fair town contains⁠— <br> Into two parts⁠—But why should I waste thought <br> On plans like these? I must not act the part <br> Of suppliant to a man who may not show <br> Regard or mercy, but may hew me down <br> Defenceless, with my armor laid aside <br> As if I were a woman. Not with him <br> May I hold parley from a tree or rock, <br> As youths and maidens with each other hold <br> Light converse. Better ’twere to rush at once <br> To combat, and the sooner learn to whom <br> Olympian Jove decrees the victory.”</p>
<p class="verse">Such were his thoughts. Achilles now drew near. <br> Like crested Mars, the warrior-god, he came. <br> On his right shoulder quivered fearfully <br> The Pelian ash, and from his burnished mail <br> There streamed a light as of a blazing fire, <br> Or of the rising sun. When Hector saw, <br> He trembled, nor could venture to remain, <br> But left the gates and fled away in fear. <br> Pelides, trusting to his rapid feet, <br> Pursued him. As, among the mountain wilds, <br> A falcon, fleetest of the birds of air, <br> Darts toward a timid dove that wheels away <br> To shun him by a sidelong flight, while he <br> Springs after her again and yet again, <br> And screaming follows, certain of his prey⁠— <br> Thus onward flew Achilles, while as fast <br> Fled Hector in dismay, with hurrying feet, <br> Beside the wall. They passed the Mount of View, <br> And the wind-beaten fig-tree, and they ran <br> Along the public way by which the wall <br> Was skirted, till they came where from the ground <br> The two fair springs of eddying Xanthus rise⁠— <br> One pouring a warm stream from which ascends <br> And spreads a vapor like a smoke from fire; <br> The other, even in summer, sending forth <br> A current cold as hail, or snow, or ice. <br> And there were broad stone basins, fairly wrought, <br> At which, in time of peace, before the Greeks <br> Had landed on the plain, the Trojan dames <br> And their fair daughters washed their sumptuous robes. <br> Past these they swept; one fled, and one pursued⁠— <br> A brave man fled, a braver followed close, <br> And swiftly both. Not for a common prize, <br> A victim from the herd, a bullock’s hide, <br> Such as reward the fleet of foot, they ran⁠— <br> The race was for the knightly Hector’s life. <br> As firm-paced coursers, that are wont to win, <br> Fly toward the goal, when some magnificent prize, <br> A tripod or a damsel, is proposed <br> In honor of some hero’s obsequies, <br> So these flew thrice on rapid feet around <br> The city of Priam. All the gods of heaven <br> Looked on, and thus the Almighty Father spake:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Alas! I see a hero dear to me <br> Pursued around the wall. My heart is grieved <br> For Hector, who has brought so many thighs <br> Of bullocks to my altar on the side <br> Of Ida ploughed with glens, or on the heights <br> Of Ilium. The renowned Achilles now <br> Is chasing him with rapid feet around <br> The city of Priam. Now bethink yourselves, <br> And answer. Shall we rescue him from death? <br> Or shall we doom him, valiant as he is, <br> To perish by the hand of Peleus’ son?”</p>
<p class="verse">Minerva, blue-eyed goddess, answered thus: <br> “O Father, who dost hurl the thunderbolt, <br> And hide the sky in clouds, what hast thou said? <br> Wouldst thou reprieve from death a mortal man, <br> Whose doom is fixed? Then do it; but know this, <br> That all the other gods will not approve.”</p>
<p class="verse">Then spake again the Cloud-compeller Jove: <br> “Tritonia, my dear child, be calm. I spake <br> Of no design. I would be kind to thee. <br> Do as thou wilt, and be there no delay.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake; and Pallas from the Olympian peaks, <br> Encouraged by his words in what her thought <br> Had planned already, downward shot to earth. <br> Still, with quick steps, the fleet Achilles pressed <br> On Hector’s flight. As when a hound has roused <br> A fawn from its retreat among the hills, <br> And chases it through glen and forest ground. <br> And to close thickets, where it skulks in fear <br> Until he overtake it, Hector thus <br> Sought vainly to elude the fleet pursuit <br> Of Peleus’ son. As often as he thought, <br> By springing toward the gates of Troy, to gain <br> Aid from the weapons of his friends who stood <br> On the tall towers, so often was the Greek <br> Before him, forcing him to turn away <br> From Ilium toward the plain. Achilles thus <br> Kept nearest to the city. As in dreams <br> The fleet pursuer cannot overtake, <br> Nor the pursued escape, so was it now; <br> One followed but in vain, the other fled <br> As fruitlessly. But how could Hector thus <br> Have put aside the imminent doom of death, <br> Had not Apollo met him once again, <br> For the last time, and given him strength and speed?</p>
<p class="verse">The great Achilles nodded to his host <br> A sign that no man should presume to strike <br> At Hector with his weapon, lest perchance <br> Another, wounding him, should bear away <br> The glory, and Pelides only wear <br> The second honors. When the twain had come <br> For the fourth time beside Scamander’s springs, <br> The All-Father raised the golden balance high, <br> And, placing in the scales two lots which bring <br> Death’s long dark sleep⁠—one lot for Peleus’ son, <br> And one for knightly Hector⁠—by the midst <br> He poised the balance. Hector’s fate sank down <br> To Hades, and Apollo left the field.</p>
<p class="verse">The blue-eyed goddess Pallas then approached <br> The son of Peleus with these wingèd words:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Renowned Achilles, dear to Jupiter! <br> Now may we, as I hope, at last return <br> To the Achaian army and the fleet <br> With glory, Hector slain, the terrible <br> In war. Escape he cannot, even though <br> The archer-god Apollo fling himself <br> With passionate entreaty at the feet <br> Of Jove the Aegis-bearer. Stay thou here <br> And breathe a moment, while I go to him <br> And lure him hither to encounter thee.”</p>
<p class="verse">She spake, and he obeyed, and gladly stood <br> Propped on the ashen stem of his keen spear; <br> While, passing on, Minerva overtook <br> The noble Hector. In the outward form, <br> And with the strong voice of Deïphobus, <br> She stood by him and spake these wingèd words:</p>
<p class="verse">“Hard pressed I find thee, brother, by the swift <br> Achilles, who, with feet that never rest, <br> Pursues thee round the walls of Priam’s town. <br> But let us make a stand and beat him back.”</p>
<p class="verse">And then the crested Hector spake in turn: <br> “Deïphobus, thou ever hast been dear <br> To me beyond my other brethren, sons <br> Of Hecuba and Priam. Now still more <br> I honor thee, since thou hast seen my plight, <br> And for my sake hast ventured forth without <br> The gates, while all the rest remain within.”</p>
<p class="verse">And then the blue-eyed Pallas spake again: <br> “Brother! ’tis true, my father, and the queen, <br> My mother, and my comrades, clasped my knees <br> In turn, and earnestly entreated me <br> That I would not go forth, such fear had fallen <br> On all of them; but I was grieved for thee. <br> Now let us combat valiantly, nor spare <br> The weapons that we bear, and we shall learn <br> Whether Achilles, having slain us both, <br> Will carry to the fleet our bloody spoil, <br> Or die himself, the victim of thy spear.”</p>
<p class="verse">The treacherous goddess spake, and led the way; <br> And when the advancing chiefs stood face to face, <br> The crested hero, Hector, thus began:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“No longer I avoid thee as of late, <br> O son of Peleus! Thrice around the walls <br> Of Priam’s mighty city have I fled, <br> Nor dared to wait thy coming. Now my heart <br> Bids me encounter thee; my time is come <br> To slay or to be slain. Now let us call <br> The gods to witness, who attest and guard <br> The covenants of men. Should Jove bestow <br> On me the victory, and I take thy life, <br> Thou shalt meet no dishonor at my hands; <br> But, stripping off the armor, I will send <br> The Greeks thy body. Do the like by me.”</p>
<p class="verse">The swift Achilles answered with a frown: <br> “Accursed Hector, never talk to me <br> Of covenants. Men and lions plight no faith, <br> Nor wolves agree with lambs, but each must plan <br> Evil against the other. So between <br> Thyself and me no compact can exist, <br> Or understood intent. First, one of us <br> Must fall and yield his life-blood to the god <br> Of battles. Summon all thy valor now. <br> A skilful spearman thou hast need to be, <br> And a bold warrior. There is no escape, <br> For now doth Pallas doom thee to be slain <br> By my good spear. Thou shalt repay to me <br> The evil thou hast done my countrymen⁠— <br> My friends whom thou hast slaughtered in thy rage.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake, and, brandishing his massive spear, <br> Hurled it at Hector, who beheld its aim <br> From where he stood. He stooped, and over him <br> The brazen weapon passed, and plunged to earth. <br> Unseen by royal Hector, Pallas went <br> And plucked it from the ground, and brought it back <br> And gave it to the hands of Peleus’ son, <br> While Hector said to his illustrious foe:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Godlike Achilles, thou hast missed thy mark; <br> Nor hast thou learned my doom from Jupiter, <br> As thou pretendest. Thou art glib of tongue. <br> And cunningly thou orderest thy speech, <br> In hope that I who hear thee may forget <br> My might and valor. Think not I shall flee, <br> That thou mayst pierce my back; for thou shalt send <br> Thy spear, if God permit thee, through my breast <br> As I rush on thee. Now avoid in turn <br> My brazen weapon. Would that it might pass <br> Clean through thee, all its length! The tasks of war <br> For us of Troy were lighter for thy death, <br> Thou pest and deadly foe of all our race!”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake, and brandishing his massive spear, <br> Hurled it, nor missed, but in the centre smote <br> The buckler of Pelides. Far away <br> It bounded from the brass, and he was vexed <br> To see that the swift weapon from his hand <br> Had flown in vain. He stood perplexed and sad; <br> No second spear had he. He called aloud <br> On the white-bucklered chief, Deïphobus, <br> To bring another; but that chief was far, <br> And Hector saw that it was so, and said:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Ah me! The gods have summoned me to die. <br> I thought my warrior-friend, Deïphobus, <br> Was by my side; but he is still in Troy, <br> And Pallas has deceived me. Now my death <br> Cannot be far⁠—is near; there is no hope <br> Of my escape, for so it pleases Jove <br> And Jove’s great archer-son, who have till now <br> Delivered me. My hour at last is come; <br> Yet not ingloriously or passively <br> I die, but first will do some valiant deed, <br> Of which mankind shall hear in after time.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake, and drew the keen-edged sword that hung, <br> Massive and finely tempered, at his side, <br> And sprang⁠—as when an eagle high in heaven, <br> Through the thick cloud, darts downward to the plain <br> To clutch some tender lamb or timid hare, <br> So Hector, brandishing that keen-edged sword, <br> Sprang forward, while Achilles opposite <br> Leaped toward him, all on fire with savage hate, <br> And holding his bright buckler, nobly wrought, <br> Before him. On his shining helmet waved <br> The fourfold crest; there tossed the golden tufts <br> With which the hand of Vulcan lavishly <br> Had decked it. As in the still hours of night <br> Hesper goes forth among the host of stars, <br> The fairest light of heaven, so brightly shone, <br> Brandished in the right hand of Peleus’ son, <br> The spear’s keen blade, as, confident to slay <br> The noble Hector, o’er his glorious form <br> His quick eye ran, exploring where to plant <br> The surest wound. The glittering mail of brass <br> Won from the slain Patroclus guarded well <br> Each part, save only where the collar-bones <br> Divide the shoulder from the neck, and there <br> Appeared the throat, the spot where life is most <br> In peril. Through that part the noble son <br> Of Peleus drave his spear; it went quite through <br> The tender neck, and yet the brazen blade <br> Cleft not the windpipe, and the power to speak <br> Remained. The Trojan fell amid the dust, <br> And thus Achilles boasted o’er his fall:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Hector, when from the slain Patroclus thou <br> Didst strip his armor, little didst thou think <br> Of danger. Thou hadst then no fear of me, <br> Who was not near thee to avenge his death. <br> Fool! There was left within the roomy ships <br> A mightier one than he, who should come forth, <br> The avenger of his blood, to take thy life. <br> Foul dogs and birds of prey shall tear thy flesh; <br> The Greeks shall honor him with funeral rites.”</p>
<p class="verse">And then the crested Hector faintly said: <br> “I pray thee by thy life, and by thy knees, <br> And by thy parents, suffer not the dogs <br> To tear me at the galleys of the Greeks. <br> Accept abundant store of brass and gold, <br> Which gladly will my father and the queen, <br> My mother, give in ransom. Send to them <br> My body, that the warriors and the dames <br> Of Troy may light for me the funeral pile.”</p>
<p class="verse">The swift Achilles answered with a frown: <br> “Nay, by my knees entreat me not, thou cur, <br> Nor by my parents. I could even wish <br> My fury prompted me to cut thy flesh <br> In fragments, and devour it, such the wrong <br> That I have had from thee. There will be none <br> To drive away the dogs about thy head, <br> Not though thy Trojan friends should bring to me <br> Tenfold and twenty-fold the offered gifts, <br> And promise others⁠—not though Priam, sprung <br> From Dardanus, should send thy weight in gold. <br> Thy mother shall not lay thee on thy bier, <br> To sorrow over thee whom she brought forth; <br> But dogs and birds of prey shall mangle thee.”</p>
<p class="verse">And then the crested Hector, dying, said: <br> “I know thee, and too clearly I foresaw <br> I should not move thee, for thou hast a heart <br> Of iron. Yet reflect that for my sake <br> The anger of the gods may fall on thee, <br> When Paris and Apollo strike thee down, <br> Strong as thou art, before the Scaean gates.” <br> Thus Hector spake, and straightway o’er him closed <br> The night of death; the soul forsook his limbs, <br> And flew to Hades, grieving for its fate⁠— <br> So soon divorced from youth and youthful might. <br> Then said the great Achilles to the dead:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Die thou; and I, whenever it shall please <br> Jove and the other gods, will meet my fate.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake, and, plucking forth his brazen lance, <br> He laid it by, and from the body stripped <br> The bloody mail. The thronging Greeks beheld <br> With wonder Hector’s tall and stately form, <br> And no one came who did not add a wound; <br> And, looking to each other, thus they said:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“How much more tamely Hector now endures <br> Our touch than when he set the fleet on fire!”</p>
<p class="verse">Such were the words of those who smote the dead; <br> But now, when swift Achilles from the corpse <br> Had stripped the armor, he stood forth among <br> The Achaian host, and spake these wingèd words:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Leaders and princes of the Grecian host! <br> Since we, my friends, by favor of the gods, <br> Have overcome the chief who wrought more harm <br> To us than all the rest, let us assault <br> The town, and learn what they of Troy intend⁠— <br> Whether their troops will leave the citadel <br> Since he is slain, or hold it with strong hand, <br> Though Hector is no more. But why give thought <br> To plans like these while yet Patroclus lies <br> A corse unwept, unburied, at the fleet? <br> I never will forget him while I live <br> And while these limbs have motion. Though below <br> In Hades they forget the dead, yet I <br> Will there remember my beloved friend. <br> Now then, ye youths of Greece, move on and chant <br> A paean, while, returning to the fleet, <br> We bring great glory with us; we have slain <br> The noble Hector, whom, throughout their town, <br> The Trojans ever worshipped like a god.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake, and, planning in his mind to treat <br> The noble Hector shamefully, he bored <br> The sinews of his feet between the heel <br> And ankle; drawing through them leathern thongs <br> He bound them to the car, but left the head <br> To trail in dust. And then he climbed the car, <br> Took in the shining mail, and lashed to speed <br> The coursers. Not unwillingly they flew. <br> Around the dead, as he was dragged along, <br> The dust arose; his dark locks swept the ground <br> That head, of late so noble in men’s eyes, <br> Lay deep amid the dust, for Jove that day <br> Suffered the foes of Hector to insult <br> His corse in his own land. His mother saw, <br> And tore her hair, and flung her lustrous veil <br> Away, and uttered piercing shrieks. No less <br> His father, who so loved him, piteously <br> Bewailed him; and in all the streets of Troy <br> The people wept aloud, with such lament <br> As if the towery Ilium were in flames <br> Even to its loftiest roofs. They scarce could keep <br> The aged king within, who, wild with grief, <br> Struggled to rush through the Dardanian gates, <br> And, rolling in the dust, entreated all <br> Who stood around him, calling them by name:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Refrain, my friends, though kind be your intent. <br> Let me go forth alone, and at the fleet <br> Of Greece will I entreat this man of blood <br> And violence. He may perchance be moved <br> With reverence for my age, and pity me <br> In my gray hairs; for such a one as I <br> Is Peleus, his own father, by whose care <br> This Greek was reared to be a scourge to Troy, <br> And, more than all, a cause of grief to me, <br> So many sons of mine in life’s fresh prime <br> Have fallen by his hand. I mourn for them, <br> But not with such keen anguish as I mourn <br> For Hector. Sorrow for his death will bring <br> My soul to Hades. Would that he had died <br> Here in my arms! This solace had been ours⁠— <br> His most unhappy mother and myself <br> Had stooped to shed these tears upon his bier.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake, and wept, and all the citizens <br> Wept with him. Hecuba among the dames <br> Took up the lamentation, and began:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Why do I live, my son, when thou art dead, <br> And I so wretched?⁠—thou who wert my boast <br> Ever, by night and day, where’er I went, <br> And whom the Trojan men and matrons called <br> Their bulwark, honoring thee as if thou wert <br> A god. They glory in thy might no more, <br> Since Fate and Death have overtaken thee.”</p>
<p class="verse">Weeping she spake. Meantime Andromache <br> Had heard no tidings of her husband yet. <br> No messenger had even come to say <br> That he was still without the gates. She sat <br> In a recess of those magnificent halls, <br> And wove a twofold web of brilliant hues, <br> On which were scattered flowers of rare device; <br> And she had given her bright-haired maidens charge <br> To place an ample cauldron on the fire, <br> That Hector, coming from the battle-field, <br> Might find the warm bath ready. Thoughtless one! <br> She knew not that the blue-eyed archer-queen, <br> Far from the bath prepared for him, had slain <br> Her husband by the hand of Peleus’ son. <br> She heard the shrieks, the wail upon the tower, <br> Trembled in every limb, and quickly dropped <br> The shuttle, saying to her bright-haired maids:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Come with me, two of you, that I may learn <br> What now has happened. ’Tis my mother’s voice <br> That I have heard. My heart leaps to my mouth; <br> My limbs fail under me. Some deadly harm <br> Hangs over Priam’s sons; far be the hour <br> When I shall hear of it. And yet I fear <br> Lest that Achilles, having got between <br> The daring Hector and the city gates, <br> May drive him to the plain alone, and quell <br> The desperate valor that was ever his; <br> For never would he keep the ranks, but ranged <br> Beyond them, and gave way to no man’s might.”</p>
<p class="verse">She spake, and from the royal mansion rushed <br> Distractedly, and with a beating heart. <br> Her maids went with her. When she reached the tower <br> And throng of men, and, standing on the wall, <br> Looked forth, she saw her husband dragged away <br> Before the city. Toward the Grecian fleet <br> The swift steeds drew him. Sudden darkness came <br> Over her eyes, and in a breathless swoon <br> She sank away and fell. The ornaments <br> Dropped from her brow⁠—the wreath, the woven band, <br> The net, the veil which golden Venus gave <br> That day when crested Hector wedded her, <br> Dowered with large gifts, and led her from her home, <br> Eëtion’s palace. Round her in a throng <br> Her sisters of the house of Priam pressed, <br> And gently raised her in that deathlike swoon. <br> But when she breathed again, and to its seat <br> The conscious mind returned, as in their arms <br> She lay, with sobs and broken speech she said:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Hector⁠—O wretched me!⁠—we both were born <br> To sorrow; thou at Troy, in Priam’s house, <br> And I at Thebé in Eëtion’s halls, <br> By woody Placos. From a little child <br> He reared me there⁠—unhappy he, and I <br> Unhappy! O that I had ne’er been born! <br> Thou goest down to Hades and the depths <br> Of earth, and leavest me in thine abode, <br> Widowed, and never to be comforted. <br> Thy son, a speechless babe, to whom we two <br> Gave being⁠—hapless parents!⁠—cannot have <br> Thy loving guardianship now thou art dead, <br> Nor be a joy to thee. Though he survive <br> The cruel warfare which the sons of Greece <br> Are waging, hard and evil yet will be <br> His lot hereafter; others will remove <br> His landmarks and will make his fields their own. <br> The day in which a boy is fatherless <br> Makes him companionless; with downcast eyes <br> He wanders, and his cheeks are stained with tears. <br> Unfed he goes where sit his father’s friends, <br> And plucks one by the cloak, and by the robe <br> Another. One who pities him shall give <br> A scanty draught, which only wets his lips, <br> But not his palate; while another boy, <br> Whose parents both are living, thrusts him thence <br> With blows and vulgar clamor: ‘Get thee gone! <br> Thy father is not with us at the feast.’ <br> Then to his widowed mother shall return <br> Astyanax in tears, who not long since <br> Was fed, while sitting in his father’s lap, <br> On marrow and the delicate fat of lambs. <br> And ever when his childish sports had tired <br> The boy, and sleep came stealing over him, <br> He slumbered, softly cushioned, on a couch <br> And in his nurse’s arms, his heart at ease <br> And satiate with delights. But now thy son <br> Astyanax⁠—whom so the Trojans name <br> Because thy valor guarded gate and tower⁠— <br> Thy care withdrawn, shall suffer many things. <br> While far from those who gave thee birth, beside <br> The roomy ships of Greece, the restless worms <br> Shall make thy flesh their banquet when the dogs <br> Have gorged themselves. Thy garments yet remain <br> Within the palace, delicately wrought <br> And graceful, woven by the women’s hands; <br> And these, since thou shalt put them on no more, <br> Nor wear them in thy death, I burn with fire <br> Before the Trojan men and dames; and all <br> Shall see how gloriously thou wert arrayed.”</p>
<p>Weeping she spake, and with her wept her maid.</p>
