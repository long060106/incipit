<p class="lede">Prowess of Hector⁠—Meeting of Minerva and Apollo near the Scaean Gates⁠—They incite Hector to challenge the Greeks to a single combat⁠—Ajax selected by lot to meet Hector⁠—The combat ended by the night⁠—Proposal of Antenor to deliver Helen to the Greeks⁠—Refusal of Paris, who offers to restore her wealth⁠—Rejection of this offer by Agamemnon⁠—A truce for burying the dead⁠—The Greek camp fortified.</p>
<p class="verse">The illustrious Hector spake, and rapidly <br> Passed through the gate, and with him issued forth <br> His brother Alexander⁠—eager, both, <br> For war and combat. As when God bestows, <br> To glad the long-expecting mariners, <br> A favorable wind while wearily <br> They beat the ocean with their polished oars, <br> Their arms all nerveless with their length of toil, <br> Such to the expecting Trojans was the sight <br> Of the two chiefs. First Alexander slew <br> Menesthius, who in Arnè had his home, <br> A son of Areïthoüs the king. <br> Large-eyed Philomedusa brought him forth <br> To the mace-bearer Areïthoüs. <br> And Hector smote Eïoneus, the spear <br> Piercing his neck beneath the brazen casque, <br> And straightway he dropped lifeless. Glaucus then <br> Son of Hippolochus, and chief among <br> The Lycians⁠—in that fiery onset slew <br> Iphinous, son of Dexius, with his spear. <br> It pierced the warrior’s shoulder as he sprang <br> To mount his rapid car, and from the place <br> He fell to earth, his limbs relaxed in death <br> Now when Minerva of the azure eyes <br> Beheld them in the furious combat thus <br> Wasting the Grecian host, she left the peaks <br> Of high Olympus, and came down in haste <br> To sacred Ilium. Straight Apollo flew <br> To meet her, for he marked from Pergamus <br> Her coming, and he greatly longed to give <br> The victory to the Trojans. As they met <br> Beside the beechen tree, the son of Jove, <br> The king Apollo, spake to Pallas thus:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Why hast thou, daughter of imperial Jove, <br> Thus left Olympus in thine eager haste? <br> Seek’st thou to turn in favor of the Greeks <br> War’s wavering chances?⁠—for I know too well <br> Thou hast no pity when the men of Troy <br> Are perishing. But, if thou wilt give ear <br> To me, I shall propose a better way. <br> Cause we the conflict for this day to cease, <br> And be it afterward renewed until <br> An end be made of Troy, since it hath pleased <br> You, goddesses, to lay the city waste.”</p>
<p class="verse">And blue-eyed Pallas answered: “Be it so, <br> O mighty Archer. With a like intent <br> I left Olympus for this battle-field <br> Of Greeks and Trojans. But by what device <br> Think’st thou to bring the combat to a pause?”</p>
<p class="verse">Then spake the king Apollo, son of Jove, <br> In turn to Pallas: “Let us seek to rouse <br> The fiery spirit of the Trojan knight <br> Hector, that he may challenge in the field <br> Some Greek to meet him, singly and alone, <br> In mortal combat. Then the well-armed Greeks, <br> Stung by the bold defiance, will send forth <br> A champion against Priam’s noble son.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake. The blue-eyed goddess gave assent <br> And straightway Helenus, beloved son <br> Of Priam, in his secret mind perceived <br> The purpose of the gods consulting thus, <br> And came and stood by Hector’s side and said:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“O Hector, son of Priam, and like Jove <br> In council, wilt thou hearken to my words <br> Who am thy brother? Cause the Trojans all <br> And all the Greeks to sit, while thou shall stand <br> Proclaiming challenge to the bravest man <br> Among the Achaians to contend with thee <br> In mortal combat. It is not thy fate <br> To fall and perish yet, for thus have said <br> The ever-living gods, whose voice I heard.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake; and Hector, hearing him, rejoiced, <br> And went between the hosts. He bore his spear, <br> Holding it in the middle, and pressed back <br> The ranks of Trojans, and they all sat down. <br> And Agamemnon caused the well-armed Greeks <br> To sit down also. Meantime Pallas sat, <br> With Phoebus of the silver bow, in shape <br> Like vultures, on the boughs of the tall beech⁠— <br> The tree of Father Jupiter who bears <br> The aegis⁠—and they looked with great delight <br> Upon the array of warriors in thick rows, <br> Horrid with shields and helm and bristling spears. <br> As when the west wind, rising fresh, breathes o’er <br> The deep, and darkens all its face with waves, <br> So seemed the Greeks and Trojans as they sat <br> In ranks upon the field, while Hector stood <br> Between the armies and bespake them thus:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Ye Trojans, and ye well-armed Greeks, give ear <br> To what my spirit bids me speak. The son <br> Of Saturn, throned on high, hath not vouchsafed <br> To ratify the treaty we have made, <br> But meditates new miseries for us both, <br> Till ye possess the towery city of Troy, <br> Or, vanquished, yield yourselves beside the barques <br> That brought you o’er the sea. With you are found <br> The bravest sons of Greece. If one of these <br> Is moved to encounter me, let him stand forth <br> And fight with noble Hector. I propose, <br> And call on Jove to witness, that if he <br> Shall slay me with the long blade of his spear, <br> My arms are his to spoil and to bestow <br> Among the hollow ships; but he must send <br> My body home, that there the sons of Troy <br> And Trojan dames may burn it on the pyre. <br> But if I take his life, and Phoebus crown <br> My combat with that glory, I will strip <br> His armor off and carry it away <br> To hallowed Ilium, there to hang it high <br> Within the temple of the archer-god <br> Apollo; but his body I will send <br> Back to the well-oared ships, that on the beach <br> The long-haired Greeks may hold his funeral rites, <br> And rear his tomb by the wide Hellespont. <br> And then, in time to come, shall someone say, <br> Sailing in his good ship the dark-blue deep, <br> ‘This is the sepulchre of one who died <br> Long since, and whom, though fighting gallantly, <br> Illustrious Hector slew.’ So shall he say <br> Hereafter, and my fame shall never die.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake; but utter silence held them all⁠— <br> Ashamed to shun the encounter, yet afraid <br> To meet it⁠—till at length, with heavy heart, <br> Rose Menelaus from his seat, and thus <br> Bespake the army with reproachful words:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“O boastful ones, no longer to be called <br> Greek warriors, but Greek women! A disgrace <br> Grievous beyond all others will be ours, <br> If none be found in all the Achaian host <br> To meet this Hector. May you, every one, <br> There where ye now are sitting, turn to earth <br> And water, craven as ye are, and lost <br> To sense of glory! I will arm myself <br> For this encounter. With the immortal gods <br> Alone it rests to give the victory.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake, and put his glorious armor on. <br> Then, Menelaus, had the Trojan’s hand <br> Ended thy life, for he was mightier far <br> Than thou, had not the Achaian kings at once <br> Uprisen to hold thee back, while Atreus’ son, <br> Wide-ruling Agamemnon, took thy hand <br> In his, and made thee listen while he spake:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Sure, noble Menelaus, thou art mad. <br> Such frenzied daring suits not with the time. <br> Restrain thyself, though thou hast cause for wrath; <br> Nor in thy pride of courage meet in arms <br> One so much mightier⁠—Hector, Priam’s son, <br> Whom every other chief regards with fear, <br> Whom even Achilles, braver far than thou, <br> Dreads to encounter in the glorious fight. <br> Withdraw, then, to thy comrades, and sit down. <br> The Greeks will send some other champion forth <br> Against him; and though fearless, and athirst <br> For combat, he, I deem, will gladly bend <br> His weary knees to rest should he escape <br> From that fierce conflict in the lists alive.”</p>
<p class="verse">With words like these the Grecian hero changed <br> The purpose of his brother, who obeyed <br> The prudent counsel; and with great delight <br> The attendants stripped the armor from his breast. <br> Then Nestor rose amid the Greeks and said:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Ye gods! A great calamity hath fallen <br> Upon Achaia. How the aged chief <br> Peleus, the illustrious counsellor and sage, <br> Who rules the Myrmidons, will now lament!⁠— <br> He who once gladly in his palace-home <br> Inquired of me the race and pedigree <br> Of the Greek warriors. Were he but to know <br> That all of them are basely cowering now <br> In Hector’s presence, how would he uplift <br> His hands and pray the gods that from his limbs <br> The parted soul might pass to the abode <br> Of Pluto! Would to Father Jupiter <br> And Pallas and Apollo that again <br> I were as young as when the Pylian host <br> And the Arcadians, mighty with the spear, <br> Fought on the banks of rapid Celadon <br> And near to Phaea and Iardan’s streams. <br> There godlike Ereuthalion stood among <br> Our foremost foes, and on his shoulders bore <br> The armor of King Areïthoüs⁠— <br> The noble Areïthoüs, whom men <br> And graceful women called the Mace-bearer; <br> For not with bow he fought, nor ponderous lance, <br> But broke the phalanxes with iron mace. <br> Lycurgus slew him, but by stratagem, <br> And not by strength; he from a narrow way, <br> Where was no room to wield the iron mace, <br> Through Areïthoüs thrust the spear: he fell <br> Backward; the victor took his arms, which Mars <br> The war-god gave, and which in after-time <br> Lycurgus wore on many a battle-field. <br> And when within his palace he grew old, <br> He gave them to be worn by one he loved⁠— <br> To Ereuthalion, who attended him <br> In battle, and who, wearing them, defied <br> The bravest of our host. All trembled; all <br> Held back in fear, nor dared encounter him. <br> But me a daring trust in my own strength <br> Impelled to meet him. I was youngest then <br> Of all the chiefs; I fought, and Pallas gave <br> The victory over him, and thus I slew <br> The hugest and most strong of men; he lay <br> Extended in vast bulk upon the ground. <br> Would I were young as then, my frame unworn <br> By years! And Hector of the beamy helm <br> Should meet an adversary soon; but now <br> No one of all the chieftains here, renowned <br> To be the bravest of the Achaian race, <br> Hastens to meet in arms the Trojan chief.”</p>
<p class="verse">Thus with upbraiding words the old man spake; <br> And straight arose nine warriors from their seats. <br> The first was Agamemnon, king of men; <br> The second, brave Tydides Diomed; <br> And then the chieftains Ajax, bold and strong; <br> And then Idomeneus, with whom arose <br> Meriones, his armor-bearer, great <br> As Mars himself in battle. After them, <br> Eurypylus, Evaemon’s valiant son, <br> And Thoas, offspring of Androemon, rose, <br> And the divine Ulysses⁠—claiming all <br> To encounter noble Hector in the lists. <br> But then spake Nestor the Gerenian knight:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Now let us cast the lot for all, and see <br> To whom it falls; for greatly will he aid <br> The nobly-armed Achaians, and as great <br> Will be his share of honor should he come <br> Alive from the hard trial of the fight.”</p>
<p class="verse">Then each one marked his lot, and all were cast <br> Into the helm of Agamemnon, son <br> Of Atreus. All the people lifted up <br> Their hands in prayer to the ever-living gods, <br> And turned their eyes to the broad heaven, and said:</p>
<p class="verse">“Grant, Father Jove, that Ajax, or the son <br> Of Tydeus, or the monarch who bears rule <br> In rich Mycenae may obtain the lot.”</p>
<p class="verse">Such was their prayer, while the Gerenian knight, <br> Old Nestor, shook the lots; and from the helm <br> Leaped forth the lot of Ajax, as they wished. <br> A herald took it, and from right to left <br> Bore it through all the assembly, showing it <br> To all the leaders of the Greeks. No one <br> Knew it, and all disclaimed it. When at last, <br> Carried through all the multitude, it came <br> To Ajax the renowned, who had inscribed <br> And laid it in the helmet, he stretched forth <br> His hand, while at his side the herald stood, <br> And took and looked upon it, knew his sign, <br> And gloried as he looked, and cast it down <br> Upon the ground before his feet, and said:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“O friends! The lot is mine, and I rejoice <br> Heartily, for I think to overcome <br> The noble Hector. Now, while I put on <br> My armor for the fight, pray ye to Jove, <br> The mighty son of Saturn, silently, <br> Unheard by them of Troy, or else aloud, <br> Since we fear no one. None by strength of arm <br> Shall vanquish me, or find me inexpert <br> In battle, nor was I to that degree <br> Ill-trained in Salamis, where I was born.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake; and they to Saturn’s monarch-son <br> Prayed, looking up to the broad heaven, and said:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“O Father Jove! Most mighty, most august! <br> Who rulest from the Idaean mount, vouchsafe <br> That Ajax bear away the victory <br> And everlasting honor; but if thou <br> Dost cherish Hector and protect his life, <br> Give equal strength to both, and equal fame.”</p>
<p class="verse">Such were their words, while Ajax armed himself <br> In glittering brass; and, when about his limbs <br> The mail was buckled, forward rushed the chief. <br> As moves the mighty Mars to war among <br> The heroes whom the son of Saturn sends <br> To struggle on the field in murderous strife, <br> So the great Ajax, bulwark of the Greeks, <br> With a grim smile came forward, and with strides <br> Firm-set and long, and shook his ponderous spear. <br> The Greeks exulted at the sight; dismay <br> Seized every Trojan: even Hector’s heart <br> Quailed in his bosom; yet he might not now <br> Withdraw through fear, nor seek to hide among <br> The throng of people, since himself had given <br> The challenge. Ajax, drawing near, upheld <br> A buckler like a rampart, bright with brass, <br> And strong with ox-hides seven. The cunning hand <br> Of Tychius, skilled beyond all other men <br> In leather-work, had wrought it at his home <br> In Hyla. He for Ajax framed the shield <br> With hides of pampered bullocks in seven folds, <br> And an eighth fold of brass⁠—the outside fold. <br> This Telamonian Ajax held before <br> His breast, as he approached, and threatening said:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Now shalt thou, Hector, singly matched with me, <br> Learn by what chiefs the Achaian host is led <br> Besides Achilles, mighty though he be to <br> To break through squadrons, and of lion-heart <br> Still in the beaked ships in which he crossed <br> The sea he cherishes his wrath against <br> The shepherd of the people⁠—Atreus’ son. <br> But we have those that dare defy thee yet, <br> And they are many. Let the fight begin.”</p>
<p class="verse">Then answered Hector of the plumèd helm:⁠— <br> “O high-born Ajax, son of Telamon, <br> And prince among thy people, think thou not <br> To treat me like a stripling weak of arm, <br> Or woman all untrained to tasks of war. <br> I know what battles are and bloody frays, <br> And how to shift to right and left the shield. <br> Of seasoned hide, and, unfatigued, maintain <br> The combat; how on foot to charge the foe <br> With steps that move to martial airs, and how <br> To leap into the chariot and pursue <br> The war with rushing steeds. Yet not by stealth <br> Seek I to smite thee, valiant as thou art, <br> But in fair open battle, if I may.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake, and, brandishing his ponderous lance, <br> Hurled it; and on the outer plate of brass, <br> Which covered the seven bullock-hides, it struck <br> The shield of Ajax. Through the brass and through <br> Six folds of hides the irresistible spear <br> Cut its swift way, and at the seventh was stopped. <br> Then high-born Ajax cast his massive spear <br> In turn, and drove it through the fair, round shield <br> Of Priam’s son. Through that bright buckler went <br> The rapid weapon, pierced the well-wrought mail, <br> And tore the linen tunic at the flank. <br> But Hector stooped and thus avoided death. <br> They took their spears again, and, coming close, <br> Like lions in their hunger, or wild boars <br> Of fearful strength, joined battle. Priam’s son <br> Sent his spear forward, striking in the midst <br> The shield of Ajax, but it broke not through <br> The brass; the metal turned the weapon’s point. <br> While Ajax, springing onward, smote the shield <br> Of Hector, drave his weapon through, and checked <br> His enemy’s swift advance, and wounded him <br> Upon the shoulder, and the black blood flowed. <br> Yet not for this did plumèd Hector cease <br> From combat, but went back, and, lifting up <br> A huge, black, craggy stone that near him lay, <br> Flung it with force against the middle boss <br> Of the broad sevenfold shield that Ajax bore. <br> The brass rang with the blow. Then Ajax raised <br> A heavier stone, and whirled it, putting forth <br> His arm’s immeasurable strength; it brake <br> Through Hector’s shield as if a millstone’s weight <br> Had fallen. His knees gave way; he fell to earth <br> Headlong; yet still he kept his shield. At once <br> Apollo raised him up; and now with swords, <br> Encountering hand to hand, they both had flown <br> To wound each other, if the heralds sent <br> As messengers from Jupiter and men <br> Had not approached⁠—Idaeus from the side <br> Of Troy, Talthybius from the Grecian host⁠— <br> Wise ancients both. Betwixt the twain they held <br> Their sceptres, and the sage Idaeus spake:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Cease to contend, dear sons, in deadly fray; <br> Ye both are loved by cloud-compelling Jove, <br> And both are great in war, as all men know. <br> The night is come; be then the night obeyed.”</p>
<p class="verse">And Telamonian Ajax answered thus:⁠— <br> “Idaeus, first let Hector speak of this, <br> For he it was who challenged to the field <br> The bravest of the Grecian host, and I <br> Shall willingly obey if he obeys.”</p>
<p class="verse">To him in turn the plumèd Hector said:⁠— <br> “Ajax, although God gave thee bulk and strength <br> And prudence, and in mastery of the spear <br> Thou dost excel the other Greeks, yet now <br> Pause we from battle and the rivalry <br> Of prowess for this day. Another time <br> We haply may renew the fight till fate <br> Shall part us and bestow the victory <br> On one of us. But now the night is here, <br> And it is good to obey the night, that thou <br> Mayst gladden at the fleet the Greeks and all <br> Thy friends and comrades, and that I in turn <br> May give the Trojan men and long-robed dames, <br> In the great city where King Priam reigns, <br> Cause to rejoice⁠—the dames who pray for me, <br> Thronging the hallowed temple. Let us now <br> Each with the other leave some noble gift, <br> That all men, Greek or Trojan, thus may say: <br> ‘They fought indeed in bitterness of heart, <br> But they were reconciled, and parted friends.’ ”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake, and gave a silver-studded sword <br> And scabbard with its fair embroidered belt; <br> And Ajax gave a girdle brightly dyed <br> With purple. Then they both departed⁠—one <br> To join the Grecian host, and one to meet <br> The Trojan people, who rejoiced to see <br> Hector alive, unwounded, and now safe <br> From the great might and irresistible arm <br> Of Ajax. Straightway to the town they led <br> Him for whose life they scarce had dared to hope. <br> And Ajax also by the well-armed Greeks, <br> Exulting in his feats of arms, was brought <br> To noble Agamemnon. When the chiefs <br> Were in his tents, the monarch sacrificed <br> A bullock of five summers to the son <br> Of Saturn, sovereign Jupiter. They flayed <br> The carcass, dressed it, carved away the limbs, <br> Divided into smaller parts the flesh, <br> Fixed them on spits, and roasted them with care, <br> And drew them from the fire. And when the task <br> Was finished, and the banquet all prepared, <br> They feasted, and there was no guest who lacked <br> His equal part in that repast. The son <br> Of Atreus, Agamemnon, brave, and lord <br> Of wide dominions, gave the chine entire <br> To Ajax as his due. Now when the calls <br> Of thirst and hunger ceased, the aged chief <br> Nestor, whose words had ever seemed most wise, <br> Opened the council with this prudent speech:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Atrides, and ye other chiefs of Greece! <br> Full many a long-haired warrior of our host <br> Hath perished. Cruel Mars hath spilt their blood <br> Beside Scamander’s gentle stream; their souls <br> Have gone to Hades. Give thou, then, command, <br> That all the Greeks tomorrow pause from war, <br> And come together at the early dawn, <br> And bring the dead in chariots drawn by mules <br> And oxen, and consume them near our fleet <br> With fire, that we, when we return from war, <br> May carry to our native land the bones, <br> And give them to the children of the slain. <br> And then will we go forth and heap from earth, <br> Upon the plain, a common tomb for all <br> Around the funeral pile, and build high towers <br> With speed beside it, which shall be alike <br> A bulwark for our navy and our host. <br> And let the entrance be a massive gate, <br> Through which shall pass an ample chariot-way. <br> And in a circle on its outer edge <br> Sink we a trench so deep that neither steeds <br> Nor men may pass, if these proud Trojans yet <br> Should, in the coming battles, press us sore.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake; the princes all approved his words. <br> Meanwhile, beside the lofty citadel <br> Of Ilium and at Priam’s palace-gates <br> In turbulence and fear the Trojans held <br> A council, and the wise Antenor spake:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Hearken, ye Trojans, Dardans, and allies, <br> To what my sober judgment bids me speak. <br> Send we the Argive Helen back with all <br> Her treasures; let the sons of Atreus lead <br> The dame away; for now we wage the war <br> After our faith is broken, and I deem <br> We cannot prosper till we make amends.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake, and sat him down. The noble chief <br> Paris, the fair-haired Helen’s husband, rose <br> To answer him, and spake this wingèd speech:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Thy words, Antenor, please me not. Thy skill <br> Could offer better counsels. If those words <br> Were gravely meant, the gods have made thee mad. <br> But let me here, amid these knights of Troy, <br> Speak openly my mind. Give up my wife <br> I never will; but all the wealth I brought <br> With her from Argos I most willingly <br> Restore, with added treasures of my own.”</p>
<p class="verse">He said, and took his seat, and in the midst <br> Dardanian Priam rose, a counsellor <br> Of godlike wisdom, and thus sagely spake:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Hear me, ye Trojans, Dardans, and allies! <br> I speak the thought that rises in my breast. <br> Take now, as ye are wont, your evening meal <br> And set a watch and keep upon your guard; <br> But let Idaeus to the hollow ships <br> Repair at morning, and to Atreus’ sons⁠— <br> To Agamemnon and his brother king⁠— <br> Make known what Paris, author of this strife, <br> Proposes, and with fairly ordered speech <br> Ask further if they will consent to pause <br> From cruel battle till we burn the dead: <br> Then be the war renewed till fate shall part <br> The hosts and give to one the victory.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake. The assembly listened and obeyed; <br> All through the camp in groups they took their meal. <br> But with the morn Idaeus visited <br> The hollow ships, and found the Achaian chiefs, <br> Followers of Mars, in council near the prow <br> Of Agamemnon’s barque; and, standing there, <br> The loud-voiced herald spake his message thus:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Ye sons of Atreus, and ye other chiefs <br> Of all the tribes of Greece, I come to you <br> From Priam and the eminent men of Troy, <br> To say, if it be pleasing to your ears, <br> What Alexander, author of the war, <br> Proposes. All the wealth which in his ships <br> He brought to Troy⁠—would he had perished first!⁠— <br> He will, with added treasures of his own, <br> Freely restore; but her who was the wife <br> Of gallant Menelaus he denies <br> To render back, though all who dwell in Troy <br> Join to demand it. I am furthermore <br> Bidden to ask if you consent to pause <br> From cruel battle till we burn our dead: <br> Then be the war renewed till fate shall part <br> The hosts and give to one the victory.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake; and all were silent for a space. <br> Then spake at length the valiant Diomed:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Let none consent to take the Trojan’s goods, <br> Nor even Helen; for a child may see <br> The utter ruin hanging over Troy.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake. The admiring Greeks confirmed with shouts <br> The words of Diomed the knight, and thus <br> King Agamemnon to Idaeus said:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Idaeus, thou thyself hast heard the Greeks <br> Pronounce their answer. What to them seems good <br> Pleases me also. For the slain, I give <br> Consent to burn them; to the dead we bear <br> No hatred; when they fall the rite of fire <br> Should soon be paid. Let Juno’s husband, Jove <br> The Thunderer, bear witness to our truce.”</p>
<p class="verse">The monarch spake, and raised to all the gods <br> His sceptre, while Idaeus took his way <br> To hallowed Ilium. There in council sat <br> Trojans and Dardans, waiting his return. <br> He came, and standing in the midst declared <br> His message. Then they all went forth in haste, <br> Some to collect the slain and some to fell <br> Trees in the forest. From their well-benched ships <br> The Achaians also issued, some to bring <br> The dead together, some to gather wood.</p>
<p class="verse">Now from the smooth deep ocean-stream the sun <br> Began to climb the heavens, and with new rays <br> Smote the surrounding fields. The Trojans met, <br> But found it hard to know their dead again. <br> They washed away the clotted blood, and laid⁠— <br> Shedding hot tears⁠—the bodies on the cars. <br> And since the mighty Priam’s word forbade <br> All wailing, silently they bore away <br> Their slaughtered friends, and heaped them on the pyre <br> With aching hearts, and, when they had consumed <br> The dead with fire, returned to hallowed Troy. <br> The nobly-armed Achaians also heaped <br> Their slaughtered warriors on the funeral pile <br> With aching hearts; and when they had consumed <br> Their dead with fire they sought their hollow ships.</p>
<p class="verse">And ere the morning came, while earth was gray <br> With twilight, by the funeral pile arose <br> A chosen band of Greeks, who, going forth, <br> Heaped round it from the earth a common tomb <br> For all, and built a wall and lofty towers <br> Near it⁠—a bulwark for the fleet and host. <br> And in the wall they fitted massive gates, <br> Through which there passed an ample chariot-way; <br> And on its outer edge they sank a trench⁠— <br> Broad, deep⁠—and planted it with pointed stakes. <br> So labored through the night the long-haired Greeks.</p>
<p class="verse">The gods who sat beside the Thunderer Jove <br> Admired the mighty labor of the Greeks; <br> But Neptune, he who shakes the earth, began:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“O Father Jove, henceforth will any one <br> Of mortal men consult the immortal gods? <br> Seest thou not how the long-haired Greeks have reared <br> A wall before their navy, and have drawn <br> A trench around it, yet have brought the gods <br> No liberal hecatombs? Now will the fame <br> Of this their work go forth wherever shines <br> The light of day, and men will quite forget <br> The wall which once we built with toiling hands⁠— <br> Phoebus Apollo and myself⁠—around <br> The city of renowned Laomedon.”</p>
<p class="verse">And cloud-compelling Jove in wrath replied:⁠— <br> “Earth-shaking power! What words are these? Some god <br> Of meaner rank and feebler arm than thou <br> Might haply dread the work the Greeks have planned. <br> But as for thee, thy glory shall be known <br> Wherever shines the day; and when at last <br> The crested Greeks, departing in their ships, <br> Shall seek their native coasts, do thou o’erthrow <br> The wall they built, and sink it in the deep, <br> And cover the great shore again with sand. <br> Thus shall their bulwark vanish from the plain.”</p>
<p class="verse">So talked they with each other while the sun <br> Was setting. But the Achaians now had brought <br> Their labors to an end; they slew their steers <br> Beside the tents and shared the evening meal, <br> While many ships had come to land with store <br> Of wine from Lemnos, which Euneüs sent⁠— <br> Euneüs whom Hypsipyle brought forth <br> To Jason, shepherd of the people. These <br> Brought wine, a thousand measures, as a gift <br> To Agamemnon and his brother king, <br> The sons of Atreus. But the long-haired Greeks <br> Bought for themselves their wines; some gave their brass, <br> And others shining steel; some bought with hides, <br> And some with steers, and some with slaves, and thus <br> Prepared an ample banquet. Through the night <br> Feasted the long-haired Greeks. The Trojan host <br> And their auxiliar warriors banqueted <br> Within the city-walls. Through all that night <br> The Great Disposer, Jove, portended woe <br> To both with fearful thunderings. All were pale <br> With terror; from their beakers all poured wine <br> Upon the ground, and no man dared to drink <br> Who had not paid to Saturn’s mighty son <br> The due libation. Then they laid them down <br> To rest, and so received the balm of sleep.</p>
