<p class="lede">Permission given by Jupiter to the gods to take part in the war⁠—The combat renewed with great violence and tumult⁠—Aeneas, encountering Achilles, to which he is encouraged by Apollo, is only preserved from death by the interposition of Neptune⁠—Slaughter of the Trojans by Achilles⁠—Hector, when in danger of being slain, snatched from the presence of Achilles by Phoebus in a cloud⁠—Havoc made by Achilles in the Trojan army.</p>
<p class="verse">Thus, O Pelides, did the sons of Greece, <br> Impatient for the battle, arm themselves, <br> By their beaked ships, around thee. Opposite, <br> Upon a height that rose amidst the plain, <br> The Trojans waited. Meantime Jupiter <br> Sent Themis from the Olympian summit, ploughed <br> With dells, to summon all the immortal ones <br> To council. Forth she went from place to place, <br> Bidding them to the palace halls of Jove. <br> Then none of all the Rivers failed to join <br> The assembly, save Oceanus, and none <br> Of all the Nymphs were absent whose abode <br> Is in the pleasant groves and river-founts <br> And grassy meadows. When they reached the halls <br> Of cloud-compelling Jove they sat them down <br> On shining thrones, divided each from each <br> By polished columns, wrought for Father Jove <br> By Vulcan’s skill. Thus all to Jove’s abode <br> Were gathered. Neptune had not disobeyed <br> The call. He left the sea, and took his seat <br> Among them, and inquired the will of Jove.</p>
<p class="verse">“Why, wielder of the lightning, dost thou call <br> The gods again to council? Do thy plans <br> Concern the Greeks and Trojans? For the war <br> Between their hosts will be rekindled soon.”</p>
<p class="verse">And thus the Cloud-compeller Jove replied: <br> “Thou who dost shake the shores, thou knowest well <br> The purpose of my mind, and for whose sake <br> I call this council. Though so soon to die, <br> They are my care. Yet will I keep my place, <br> Seated upon the Olympian mount, and look <br> Calmly upon the conflict. All of you <br> Depart, and aid the Trojans or the Greeks, <br> As it may list you. For should Peleus’ son <br> Alone do battle with the men of Troy, <br> Their squadrons could not stand before the assault <br> Of the swift-footed warrior for an hour. <br> Beforetime, at the sight of him they fled, <br> O’ercome with fear, and now, when he is roused <br> To rage by his companion’s death, I fear <br> Lest, though it be against the will of fate, <br> He level with the ground the walls of Troy.”</p>
<p class="verse">Saturnius spake, and moved the hosts to join <br> In desperate conflict. All the gods went forth <br> To mingle with the war on different sides. <br> Juno and Pallas hastened to the fleet <br> With Neptune, he who makes the earth to shake, <br> And Hermes, god of useful arts, and shrewd <br> In forecast. Vulcan also went with them, <br> Strong and stern-eyed, yet lame, his feeble legs <br> Moving with labor. To the Trojan side <br> Went crested Mars, Apollo with his locks <br> Unshorn, Diana mighty with the bow, <br> Latona, Xanthus, and the queen of smiles, <br> Venus; for while the gods remained apart <br> From men, the Achaian host was high in hope <br> Because Achilles, who so long had left <br> The war, now reappeared upon the field, <br> And terror shook the limbs of every son <br> Of Troy when he beheld the swift of foot, <br> Pelides, terrible as Mars⁠—that curse <br> Of human-kind⁠—in glittering arms again. <br> But when the dwellers of Olympus joined <br> The crowd of mortals, Discord, who makes mad <br> The nations, rose and raged; Minerva raised <br> Her war-cry from the trench without the wall, <br> And then she shouted from the sounding shore; <br> While, like a cloudy whirlwind, opposite, <br> Moved Mars, and fiercely yelled, encouraging <br> The men of Troy, as on the city heights <br> He stood, or paced with rapid steps the hill <br> Beside the Simoïs, called the Beautiful.</p>
<p class="verse">Thus, kindling hate between the hosts, the gods <br> Engaged, and hideous was the strife that rose <br> Among them. From above, with terrible crash, <br> Thundered the father of the blessed gods <br> And mortal men, while Neptune from below <br> Shook the great earth and lofty mountain peaks. <br> Then watery Ida’s heights and very roots, <br> The city of Troy, and the Greek galleys, quaked. <br> Then Pluto, ruler of the nether world, <br> Leaped from his throne in terror, lest the god <br> Who makes the earth to tremble, cleaving it <br> Above him, should lay bare to gods and men <br> His horrible abodes, the dismal haunts <br> Which even the gods abhor. Such tumult filled <br> The field of battle when the immortals joined <br> The conflict. Then against King Neptune stood <br> Phoebus Apollo, with his wingèd shafts, <br> And Pallas, goddess of the azure eyes, <br> Confronted Mars. Encountering Juno came <br> The sister of Apollo, archer-queen <br> And huntress, Dian of the golden bow. <br> The helpful Hermes, god of useful arts, <br> Opposed Latona, and the mighty stream <br> Called Xanthus by the immortals, but by men <br> Scamander, with his eddies strong and deep, <br> Stood face to face with Vulcan in the field.</p>
<p class="verse">So warred the gods with gods. Meantime the son <br> Of Peleus, ranging through the thick of fight, <br> Sought only Hector, Priam’s son, whose blood <br> He meant to pour to greedy Mars, the god <br> Of carnage. But Apollo, who impels <br> Warriors to battle, stirred Aeneas up <br> To meet Pelides. First he filled his heart <br> With resolute valor, and then took the voice <br> Of Priam’s son, Lycaon. In his shape <br> Thus spake Apollo, son of Jupiter:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Aeneas, prince of Troy, where now are all <br> The boasts which thou hast made before the chiefs <br> Of Troy at banquets, that thou yet wouldst meet <br> Pelides in the combat hand to hand?”</p>
<p class="verse">Aeneas made reply: “Priamides, <br> Why dost thou bid me, when thou knowest me <br> Unwilling, meet in combat Peleus’ son, <br> The mighty among men? It will not be <br> For the first time if I confront him now. <br> He chased me once from Ida with his spear⁠— <br> Me and my fellows, when he took our herds <br> And laid Lyrnessus waste and Pedasus. <br> But Jove, who gave me strength and nimble feet, <br> Preserved me; I had else been slain by him <br> And by Minerva, for the goddess went <br> Before him, giving him the victory <br> And moving him to slay the Leleges <br> And Trojans with the brazen spear he bore. <br> ’Tis not for mortal man to fight the son <br> Of Peleus, at whose side there ever stands <br> One of the immortal gods, averting harm. <br> And then his weapon flies right on, nor stops <br> Until it bites the flesh. Yet were the god <br> To weigh the victory in an equal scale, <br> Achilles would not vanquish me with ease, <br> Though he might boast his frame were all of brass.”</p>
<p class="verse">Then spake the king Apollo, son of Jove: <br> “Pray, warrior, to the eternal gods. They say <br> That Venus gave thee birth, who has her own <br> From Jove. His mother is of lower rank <br> Than thine. Thine is a child of Jove, but his <br> A daughter of the Ancient of the Deep. <br> Strike at him with that conquering spear of thine, <br> Nor let him scare thee with stern words and threats.”</p>
<p class="verse">He said, and breathed into the prince’s breast <br> Fresh valor, as, arrayed in glittering arms, <br> He pressed to where the foremost warriors fought; <br> Yet not unseen by Juno’s eye went forth <br> The son of old Anchises. She convened <br> The gods in council, and addressed them thus:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Neptune and Pallas, what shall now be done? <br> Consider ye. Aeneas, all arrayed <br> In glittering arms, is pressing on to meet <br> Pelides. Phoebus sends him. Let us join <br> To turn him back, or let some one of us <br> Stand near Achilles, fill his limbs with strength, <br> Nor let his heart grow faint, but let him see <br> That we, the mightiest of the immortals, look <br> On him with favor, and that those who strive <br> Amid the war and bloodshed to protect <br> The sons of Troy are empty boasters all. <br> For this we came from heaven to interpose <br> In battle, that Achilles may endure <br> No harm from Trojan hands, although, no doubt, <br> Hereafter he must suffer all that Fate <br> Spun for him when his mother brought him forth. <br> But if he hear not, from some heavenly voice, <br> Of this assurance, fear may fall on him <br> When, haply, in the battle he shall meet <br> Some god; for when revealed to human sight <br> The presence of the gods is terrible.”</p>
<p class="verse">And then did Neptune, he who shakes the earth, <br> Make answer: “Juno, it becomes thee ill <br> To be so greatly vexed. I cannot wish <br> A contest with the other gods, though we <br> In power excel them. Rather let us sit <br> Apart, where we can look upon the war, <br> And leave it to mankind. And yet if Mars <br> Or Phoebus should begin the fight, or seek <br> To thwart Achilles or restrain his arm, <br> There will be cause for us to join the strife <br> In earnest, and I deem that they full soon, <br> The contest ended, will return to join <br> The assembled gods upon the Olympian mount, <br> Forced to withdraw by our all-potent hands.”</p>
<p class="verse">So spake the dark-haired god, and led the way <br> To the high mound of godlike Hercules, <br> Raised from the earth by Trojans, with the aid <br> Of Pallas, that the hero there might find <br> A refuge when the monster of the deep <br> Should chase him from the sea-beach to the plain. <br> With other gods beside him Neptune there <br> Sat down and drew a shadow, which no sight <br> Could pierce, around their shoulders. Other gods, <br> Upon the hill called Beautiful, were grouped <br> Round thee, Apollo, archer-god, and Mars, <br> Spoiler of cities. On both sides they sat, <br> Devising plans, unwilling to begin <br> The fierce encounter, though Almighty Jove <br> From where he sat in heaven commanded it.</p>
<p class="verse">The warriors thronged into the field, which shone <br> With brazen armor and caparisons <br> Of steeds; earth trembled with the sounding tramp <br> Of marching squadrons. From the opposing ranks <br> Two chieftains, each the bravest of his host, <br> Impatient to engage⁠—Anchises’ son, <br> Aeneas, and the great Achilles⁠—came. <br> And first Aeneas, with defiant mien <br> And nodding casque, stood forth. He held his shield <br> Before him, which he wielded right and left, <br> And shook his brazen spear. On the other side, <br> Pelides hurried toward him, terrible <br> As is a lion, which the assembled hinds <br> Of a whole village chase and seek to slay, <br> While on he stalks, contemning their assault; <br> But if the arrow of some strong-armed youth <br> Have smitten him, he stands, and gathers all <br> His strength to spring, with open jaws and teeth <br> Half hid in foam, and uttering fearful growls <br> From his deep chest; he lashes with his tail <br> His sides and sinewy thighs to rouse himself <br> To combat, and then, grimly frowning, leaps <br> To slay, or by the foremost youths be slain, <br> So sprang Achilles, moved by his bold heart <br> To meet the brave Aeneas. As the twain <br> Drew near each other, the swift-footed chief, <br> The great Achilles, was the first to speak:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Why, O Aeneas, hast thou come so far <br> Through this vast crowd to seek me? Does thy heart <br> Bid thee confront me in the hope to gain <br> The place which Priam holds, and to bear rule <br> Over the knights Of Troy? Yet shouldst thou take <br> My life, think not that Priam in thy hand <br> Will place such large reward. He has his sons, <br> Nor is he fickle, but of stable mind. <br> Or will the Trojans, if thou slayest me, <br> Bestow on thee broad acres, of a soil <br> Fruitful exceedingly, and suited well <br> To vines or to the plough, which thou mayst till <br> That also, as I hope, thou wilt obtain <br> With difficulty; for, unless I err, <br> I forced thee once to flee before my spear. <br> Dost thou remember, when thou wert alone <br> Among thy beeves, I drave thee, running fast, <br> Down Ida’s steeps? Then didst thou never turn <br> To face me, but didst seek a hiding-place <br> Within Lyrnessus, which I also took <br> And wasted, with the aid of Father Jove <br> And Pallas. From the town I led away <br> The women, never to be free again. <br> Jove and the other gods protected thee <br> That day. Yet will they not protect thee now, <br> As thou dost vainly hope. Withstand me not, <br> I counsel thee, but hide thyself among <br> The crowd before thou suffer harm, for he <br> Who sees past evils only is a fool.”</p>
<p class="verse">And then Aeneas answered: “Do not think, <br> Pelides, with such words to frighten me, <br> As if I were a beardless boy. I too <br> Might use reproach and taunt; but well we know <br> Each other’s birth and lineage, through report <br> Of men, although by sight I know not thine, <br> Nor know’st thou mine. They say that thou art sprung <br> From Peleus the renowned, and from the nymph <br> Of ocean, fair-haired Thetis, while I boast <br> My birth from brave Anchises, and can claim <br> Venus as mother. Two of these today <br> Must weep the death of a beloved son, <br> For we are not to part, I think, nor end <br> The combat after a few childish words; <br> Yet let me speak, that thou mayst better know <br> Our lineage, known already far and wide. <br> Jove was the father, cloud-compelling Jove, <br> Of Dardanus, by whom Dardania first <br> Was peopled, ere our sacred Troy was built <br> On the great plain⁠—a populous town; for men <br> Dwelt still upon the roots of Ida fresh <br> With many springs. To Dardanus was born <br> King Erichthonius, richest in his day <br> Of mortal men, and in his meadows grazed <br> Three thousand mares, exulting in their brood <br> Of tender foals. Of some of this vast herd <br> Boreas became enamored as they fed. <br> He came to them in likeness of a steed <br> That wore an azure mane, and they brought forth <br> Twelve foals, which all were females, of such speed <br> That when they frolicked on the teeming earth <br> They flew along the topmost ears of wheat <br> And broke them not, and when they sported o’er <br> The mighty bosom of the deep they ran <br> Along the hoary summits of its waves. <br> To Erichthonius Tros was born, who ruled <br> The Trojans, and from Tros there sprang three sons <br> Of high renown⁠—Ilus, Assaracus, <br> And godlike Ganymede, most beautiful <br> Of men; the gods beheld and caught him up <br> To heaven, so beautiful was he, to pour <br> The wine to Jove, and ever dwell with them. <br> And Ilus had a son, Laomedon, <br> Of mighty fame, to whom five sons were born, <br> Tithonus, Priam, Lampus, Clytius, <br> And Hicetaon, trained to war by Mars. <br> Assaracus begat my ancestor, <br> Capys, to whom Anchises owes his birth. <br> Anchises is my father; Priam’s son <br> Is noble Hector. Such I claim to be <br> My lineage and my blood; but Jove at will <br> Gives in large measure, or diminishes, <br> Men’s warlike prowess; and the power of Jove <br> Is over all. But let us talk no more <br> Of things like these, as if we were but boys, <br> While here in the mid-field we stand between <br> The warring armies. Both of us might cast <br> Reproaches at each other, many and foul, <br> Such as no galley of a hundred oars <br> Could bear and float. Men’s tongues are voluble, <br> And endless are the modes of speech, and far <br> Extends from side to side the field of words. <br> Such as thou utterest it will be thy lot <br> To hear from others. But what profits it <br> For us to rail and wrangle, in high brawl, <br> Like women angered to the quick, that rush <br> Into the middle of the street and scold <br> With furious words, some true and others false, <br> As rage may prompt them? Me thou shalt not move <br> With words from my firm purpose ere thou raise <br> Thy arm against me. Let us hasten first <br> To prove the temper of our brazen spears.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake, and hurled his brazen spear to smite <br> The dreadful shield, a terror in men’s eyes; <br> That mighty buckler rang with the strong blow. <br> Achilles, as it came, held forth his shield <br> With nervous arm far from him, for he feared <br> That the long javelin of his valiant foe <br> Might pierce it. Idle fear; he had not thought <br> That the bright armor given him by the gods <br> Not easily would yield to force of man. <br> Nor could the rapid spear that left the hand <br> Of brave Aeneas pierce the shield; the gold, <br> The gift of Vulcan, stopped it. Through two folds <br> It went, but three remained; for Vulcan’s skill <br> Fenced with five folds the disk⁠—the outer two <br> Of brass, the inner two of tin; between <br> Was one of gold, and there the brazen spear <br> Was stayed. And then in turn Achilles threw <br> His ponderous spear, and struck the orbèd shield <br> Borne by Aeneas near the upper edge, <br> Where thinnest was the brass and thinnest lay <br> The bullock’s hide. The Pelian ash broke through; <br> The buckler crashed; Aeneas, stooping low, <br> Held it above him, terrified; the spear, <br> Tearing both plate and hide of that huge shield, <br> Passed over him, and, eager to go on, <br> Plunged in the earth and stood. He, when he saw <br> The massive lance which he had just escaped <br> Fixed in the earth so near him, stood awhile <br> As struck with fear, and with despairing looks. <br> Achilles drew his trenchant sword and rushed <br> With fury on Aeneas, uttering <br> A fearful shout. Aeneas lifted up <br> A stone, a mighty weight, which no two men, <br> As men are now, could raise, yet easily <br> He wielded it. Aeneas then, to save <br> His threatened life, had smitten with the stone <br> His adversary’s buckler or his helm, <br> And with his sword Pelides had laid dead <br> The Trojan, had not he who shakes the earth, <br> Neptune, beheld him in that perilous hour, <br> And instantly addressed the immortal gods:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“My heart, ye gods, is heavy for the sake <br> Of the great-souled Aeneas, who will sink <br> To Hades overcome by Peleus’ son. <br> Rash man! He listened to the archer-god <br> Apollo, who has now no power to save <br> The chief from death. But, guiltless as he is, <br> Why should he suffer evil for the wrong <br> Of others? He has always sought to please <br> With welcome offerings the gods who dwell <br> In the broad heaven. Let us withdraw him, then, <br> From this great peril, lest, if he should fall <br> Before Achilles, haply Saturn’s son <br> May be displeased. And ’tis the will of fate <br> That he escape; that so the Dardan race, <br> Beloved by Jove above all others sprung <br> From him and mortal women, may not yet <br> Perish from earth and leave no progeny. <br> For Saturn’s son already holds the house <br> Of Priam in disfavor, and will make <br> Aeneas ruler o’er the men of Troy, <br> And his sons’ sons shall rule them after him.”</p>
<p class="verse">Imperial Juno with large eyes replied: <br> “Determine, Neptune, for thyself, and save <br> Aeneas, or, all blameless as he is, <br> Abandon him to perish by the hand <br> Of Peleus’ son, Achilles. We have sworn⁠— <br> Minerva and myself⁠—that never we <br> Would aid in aught the Trojans to escape <br> Their day of ruin, though the town of Troy <br> Sink to the dust in the destroying flames⁠— <br> Flames kindled by the warlike sons of Greece.”</p>
<p class="verse">And then did Neptune, shaker of the shores, <br> Go forth into the battle and amidst <br> The clash of spears, and come where stood the chiefs, <br> Aeneas and his mighty foe, the son <br> Of Peleus. Instantly he caused to rise <br> A darkness round the eyes of Peleus’ son, <br> And from the buckler of Aeneas drew <br> The spear with ashen stem and brazen blade, <br> And laid it at Achilles’ feet, and next <br> He lifted high Aeneas from the ground <br> And bore him thence. O’er many a warrior’s head, <br> And many a harnessed steed, Aeneas flew, <br> Hurled by the god, until he reached the rear <br> Of that fierce battle, where the Caucons stood <br> Arrayed for war. The shaker of the shores <br> Drew near, and said to him in wingèd words:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“What god, Aeneas, moved thee to defy <br> Madly the son of Peleus, who in might <br> Excels thee, and is dearer to the gods? <br> Whenever he encounters thee in arms <br> Give way, lest thou, against the will of fate, <br> Pass down to Hades. When he shall have met <br> His fate and perished, thou mayst boldly dare <br> To face the foremost of the enemy; <br> No other of the Greeks shall take thy life.</p>
<p class="verse">He spake, and having thus admonished him <br> He left Aeneas there, and suddenly <br> Swept off the darkness that so thickly rose <br> Around Achilles, who, with sight now clear, <br> Looked forth, and, sighing, said to his great soul:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“How strange is this! My eyes have seen today <br> A mighty marvel. Here the spear I flung <br> Is lying on the earth, and him at whom <br> I cast it, in the hope to take his life, <br> I see no longer. Well beloved, no doubt, <br> Is this Aeneas by the immortal gods. <br> Yet that, I thought, was but an empty boast <br> Of his. Well, let him go; I cannot think <br> That he who gladly fled from death will find <br> The courage to encounter me again. <br> And now will I exhort the Greeks to fight <br> This battle bravely, while I go to prove <br> The prowess of the other chiefs of Troy.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake, and, cheering on the soldiery, <br> He sprang into the ranks: “Ye noble Greeks, <br> Avoid no more the Trojans; press right on. <br> “Let each man single out his man, and fight <br> With eager heart. ’Tis hard for me to chase, <br> With all my warlike might, so many men, <br> And fight with all. Not even Mars, the god, <br> Although immortal, nor Minerva’s self, <br> Could combat with so vast a multitude <br> Unwearied; yet whatever I can do, <br> With hands and feet and strength, I give my word <br> Not to decline, or be remiss in aught. <br> I go to range the Trojan files, where none, <br> I think, will gladly stand to meet my spear.”</p>
<p class="verse">Such stirring words he uttered, while aloud <br> Illustrious Hector called, encouraging <br> The men of Troy, and promising to meet <br> Achilles: “Valiant Trojans, do not quail <br> Before Pelides. In the strife of words <br> I too might bear my part against the gods; <br> But harder were the combat with the spear, <br> For greater is their might than ours. The son <br> Of Peleus cannot make his threatenings good. <br> A part will he perform and part will leave <br> Undone. I go to wait him; I would go <br> Although his hands were like consuming flame⁠— <br> His hands like flame, his strength the strength of steel.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake: the Trojans at his stirring word <br> Lifted their lances, and the adverse hosts <br> Joined battle with a fearful din. Then came <br> Apollo and admonished Hector thus:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Hector, encounter not Achilles here <br> Before the armies, but amidst the throng <br> And tumult of the battle, lest perchance <br> He strike thee with the javelin or the sword.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake: the Trojan chief, dismayed to hear <br> The warning of the god, withdrew among <br> The crowded ranks. Meantime Achilles sprang <br> Upon the Trojans with a terrible cry, <br> And slew a leader of the host, the brave <br> Iphition, whom a Naiad, at the foot <br> Of snowy Tmolus, in the opulent vale <br> Of Hyda, bore to the great conqueror <br> Of towns, Otrynteus. As he came in haste, <br> The noble son of Peleus with his spear <br> Smote him upon the forehead in the midst, <br> And cleft the head in two. He fell; his arms <br> Clashed, and Achilles boasted o’er him thus:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Son of Otrynteus, terrible in arms, <br> Thou art brought low; thou meetest here thy death, <br> Though thou wert born by the Gygaean lake <br> Where lie, by fishy Hyllus and the stream <br> Of eddying Hermus, thy paternal fields.”</p>
<p class="verse">Thus boastfully he spake, while darkness came <br> Over Iphition’s eyes, and underneath <br> The chariots of the Greeks who foremost fought <br> His corse was mangled. Next Achilles smote <br> Antenor’s son, Demoleon, gallantly <br> Breasting the onset of the Greeks. He pierced <br> His temple through the helmet’s brazen cheek; <br> The brass stayed not the blow; the eager spear <br> Brake through the bone, and crushed the brain within, <br> And the brave youth lay dead. Achilles next <br> Struck down Hippodamas; he pierced his back <br> As, leaping from his car, the Phrygian fled <br> Before him. With a moan he breathed away <br> His life, as moans a bull when dragged around <br> The altar of the Heliconian king <br> By youths on whom the god that shakes the earth <br> Looks down well pleased. With such a moaning sound <br> The fiery spirit left the Phrygian’s frame.</p>
<p class="verse">Then sprang Achilles with his spear to slay <br> The godlike Polydorus, Priam’s son, <br> Whose father bade him not to join the war, <br> For he was younger than the other sons, <br> And dearest of them all. In speed of foot <br> He had no peer. Yet, with a boyish pride <br> To show his swiftness, in the foremost ranks <br> He ranged the field, until he lost his life. <br> Him with a javelin the swift-footed son <br> Of Peleus smote as he was hurrying by. <br> The weapon pierced the middle of his back, <br> Where, by its golden rings, the belt was clasped <br> Above the double corselet; the keen blade <br> Came forth in front; the Trojan with a cry <br> Fell forward on his knees, and, bending, clasped <br> His bowels in his hands. When Hector saw <br> His brother thus upon the earth, there came <br> A darkness o’er his eyes, nor could he bear <br> Longer to stand aloof, but, brandishing <br> His spear, came forward like a rushing flame <br> To meet the son of Peleus, who beheld <br> And bounded toward him, saying boastfully: <br> “So, he is near whose hand hath given my heart <br> Its deepest wound, who slew my dearest friend. <br> No more are we to shun each other now, <br> Timidly stealing through the paths of war.”</p>
<p class="verse">And then he said to Hector with a frown: <br> “Draw nearer, that thou mayst the sooner die.”</p>
<p class="verse">The crested Hector, undismayed, replied: <br> “Pelides, do not hope with empty words <br> To frighten me, as if I were a boy. <br> Insults and taunts I could with ease return. <br> I know that thou art brave; I know that I <br> In might am not thy equal; but the event <br> Rests in the laps of the great gods, and they <br> May, though I lack the prowess, give thy life <br> Into my hands when I shall cast my spear. <br> The weapon that I bear is keen like thine.”</p>
<p class="verse">Thus having spoken, brandishing his spear, <br> He sent it forth; but with a gentle breath <br> Minerva turned it from the glorious Greek, <br> And laid it at the noble Hector’s feet. <br> Then did Achilles, resolute to slay <br> His enemy, rush against him with a shout <br> Of fury; but Apollo, with such power <br> As gods put forth, withdrew him thence, and spread <br> A darkness round him. Thrice the swift of foot, <br> Achilles, rushed against him with his spear, <br> And thrice he smote the cloud. But when once more, <br> In godlike might, he made the assault, he spake <br> These wingèd words of menace and reproach:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Hound as thou art, thou hast once more escaped <br> Thy death; for it was near. Again the hand <br> Of Phoebus rescues thee; to him thy vows <br> Are made ere thou dost trust thyself amidst <br> The clash of javelins. I shall meet thee yet <br> And end thee utterly, if any god <br> Favor me also. I will now pursue <br> And strike the other Trojan warriors down.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake, and in the middle of the neck <br> Smote Dryops with his spear. The Phrygian fell <br> Before him at his feet. He left him there, <br> And wounding with his spear Philetor’s son, <br> Demuchus, tall and valiant, in the knee, <br> Stayed him until he slew him with his sword. <br> Then from their chariot to the ground he cast <br> Laogonus and Dardanus, the sons <br> Of Bias, piercing with a javelin one, <br> And cutting down the other with his sword.</p>
<p class="verse">And Tros, Alastor’s son, who came to him <br> And clasped his knees, in hope that he would spare <br> A captive⁠—spare his life, nor slay a youth <br> Of his own age⁠—vain hope! He little knew <br> That not by prayers Achilles could be moved, <br> Nor was he pitiful, nor mild of mood, <br> But hard of heart⁠—while Tros embraced his knees <br> And passionately sued, Pelides thrust <br> His sword into his side; the liver came <br> Forth at the wound; the dark blood gushing filled <br> The Phrygian’s bosom; o’er his eyes there crept <br> A darkness, and his life was at an end.</p>
<p class="verse">Approaching Mulius next, Achilles smote <br> The warrior at the ear; the brazen point <br> Passed through the other ear; and then he slew <br> Agenor’s son, Echeclus, letting fall <br> His heavy-hilted sword upon his head <br> Just in the midst; the blade grew warm with blood, <br> And gloomy death and unrelenting fate <br> Darkened the victim’s eyes. Achilles next <br> Wounded Deucalion, thrusting through his arm <br> The brazen javelin, where the sinews met <br> That strung the elbow. While with powerless arm <br> The wounded Trojan stood awaiting death, <br> Achilles drave his falchion through his neck. <br> Far flew the head and helm, the marrow flowed <br> From out the spine, and stretched upon the ground <br> Deucalion lay. Pelides still went on, <br> O’ertaking Rigmus, the renowned son <br> Of Peireus, from the fruitful fields of Thrace, <br> And smote him in the stomach with his lance. <br> There hung the weapon fixed; the wounded man <br> Fell from the car. At Areïthoüs <br> The charioteer, who turned his steeds to flee, <br> Achilles sent his murderous lance, and pierced <br> His back, and dashed him from the car, and left <br> His horses wild with fright. As when, among <br> The deep dells of an arid mountain-side, <br> A great fire burns its way, and the thick wood <br> Before it is consumed, and shifting winds <br> Hither and thither sweep the flames, so ranged <br> Achilles in his fury through the field <br> From side to side, and everywhere o’ertook <br> His victims, and the earth ran dark with blood.</p>
<p class="verse">As when a yeoman underneath the yoke <br> Brings his broad-fronted oxen to tread out <br> White barley on the level threshing-floor, <br> The sheaves are quickly trodden small beneath <br> The heavy footsteps of the bellowing beasts, <br> So did the firm-paced coursers, which the son <br> Of Peleus guided, trample with their feet <br> Bucklers and corpses, while beneath the car <br> Blood steeped the axle, and the chariot-seat <br> Dripped on its rim with blood, that from below <br> Was splashed upon them by the horses’ hoofs <br> And by the chariot-wheels. Such havoc made <br> Pelides in his ardor for renown, <br> Till his invincible hands were foul with blood.</p>
