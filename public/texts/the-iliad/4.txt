<p class="lede">A council of the gods, who decide that the war shall go on⁠—Minerva sent down to cause the breaking of the truce⁠—Pandarus persuaded by her to aim an arrow at Menelaus, who is wounded by it, and healed by Machaon⁠—Exhortations of Agamemnon addressed to the Greek chiefs⁠—A furious battle, and great slaughter on both sides.</p>
<p class="verse">Meantime the immortal gods with Jupiter <br> Upon his golden pavement sat and held <br> A council. Hebe, honored of them all, <br> Ministered nectar, and from cups of gold <br> They pledged each other, looking down on Troy. <br> When, purposely to kindle Juno’s mood <br> To anger, Saturn’s son, with biting words <br> That well betrayed his covert meaning, spake:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Two goddesses⁠—the Argive Juno one, <br> The other Pallas, her invincible friend⁠— <br> Take part with Menelaus, yet they sit <br> Aloof, content with looking on, while still <br> Venus, the laughter-loving one, protects <br> Her Paris, ever near him, warding off <br> The stroke of fate. Just now she rescued him <br> When he was near his death. The victory <br> Belongs to Menelaus, loved of Mars. <br> Now let us all consider what shall be <br> The issue⁠—whether we allow the war, <br> With all its waste of life, to be renewed, <br> Or cause the warring nations to sit down <br> In amity. If haply it shall be <br> The pleasure and the will of all the gods, <br> Let Priam’s city keep its dwellers still, <br> And Menelaus lead his Helen home.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake, but Juno and Minerva sat, <br> And with closed lips repined, for secretly <br> They plotted evil for the Trojan race. <br> Minerva held her peace in bitterness <br> Of heart and sore displeased with Father Jove. <br> But Juno could not curb her wrath, and spake:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“What words, austere Saturnius, hast thou said! <br> Wilt thou then render vain the toils I bear, <br> And all my sweat? My very steeds even now <br> Are weary with the mustering of the host <br> That threaten woe to Priam and his sons. <br> Yet do thy will; but be at least assured <br> That all the other gods approve it not.”</p>
<p class="verse">The cloud-compelling Jupiter replied <br> In anger: “Pestilent one! What grievous wrong <br> Hath Priam done to thee, or Priam’s sons, <br> That thou shouldst persevere to overthrow <br> His noble city? Shouldst thou through the gates <br> Of Ilium make thy way, and there devour, <br> Within the ramparts, Priam and his sons <br> And all the men of Troy alive, thy rage <br> Haply might be appeased. Do as thou wilt, <br> So that this difference breed no lasting strife <br> Between us. Yet I tell thee this⁠—and thou <br> Bear what I say in mind: In time to come, <br> Should I design to level in the dust <br> Some city where men dear to thee are born, <br> Seek not to thwart my vengeance, but submit. <br> For now I fully yield me to thy wish, <br> Though with unwilling mind. Wherever dwell <br> The race of humankind beneath the sun <br> And starry heaven, of all their cities Troy <br> Has been by me most honored⁠—sacred Troy⁠— <br> And Priam, and the people who obey <br> Priam, the wielder of the ashen spear; <br> For there my altars never lacked their rites⁠— <br> Feasts, incense, and libations duly paid.”</p>
<p class="verse">Then Juno, the majestic, with large eyes, <br> Rejoined: “The cities most beloved by me <br> Are three⁠—Mycenae, with her spacious streets, <br> Argos, and Sparta. Raze them to the ground, <br> If they be hateful to thee. I shall ne’er <br> Contend to save them, nor repine to see <br> Their fall; for, earnestly as I might seek <br> To rescue them from ruin, all my aid <br> Would not avail, so much the mightier thou. <br> Yet doth it ill become thee thus to make <br> My efforts vain. I am a goddess, sprung <br> From the same stock with thee; I am the child <br> Of crafty Saturn, and am twice revered⁠— <br> Both for my birth and that I am the spouse <br> Of thee who rulest over all the gods. <br> Now let us each yield somewhat⁠—I to thee <br> And thou to me; the other deathless gods <br> Will follow us. Let Pallas be despatched <br> To that dread battle-field on which are ranged <br> The Trojans and Achaians, and stir up <br> The Trojan warriors first to lift their hands <br> Against the elated Greeks and break the league,”</p>
<p class="verse">She ended, and the Father of the gods <br> And mortals instantly complied, and called <br> Minerva, and in wingèd accents said:⁠— <br> “Haste to the battle-field, and there, among <br> The Trojan and Achaian armies, cause <br> The Trojan warriors first to lift their hands <br> Against the elated Greeks and break the league.”</p>
<p class="verse">So saying, Jupiter to Pallas gave <br> The charge she wished already. She in haste <br> Shot from the Olympian summits, like a star <br> Sent by the crafty Saturn’s son to warn <br> The seamen or some mighty host in arms⁠— <br> A radiant meteor scattering sparkles round. <br> So came and lighted Pallas on the earth <br> Amidst the armies. All who saw were seized <br> With wonder⁠—Trojan knights and well-armed Greeks; <br> And many a one addressed his comrade thus:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Sure we shall have the wasting war again, <br> And stubborn combats; or, it may be, Jove, <br> The arbiter of wars among mankind, <br> Decrees that the two nations dwell in peace.”</p>
<p class="verse">So Greeks and Trojans said. The goddess went <br> Among the Trojan multitude disguised; <br> She seemed Laodocus, Antenor’s son, <br> A valiant warrior, seeking through the ranks <br> For godlike Pandarus. At length she found <br> Lycaon’s gallant and illustrious son, <br> Standing with bucklered warriors ranged around, <br> Who followed him from where Aesepus flows; <br> And, standing near, she spake these wingèd words:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Son of Lycaon! Wilt thou hear my words, <br> Brave as thou art? Then wilt thou aim a shaft <br> At Menelaus; thus wilt thou have earned <br> Great thanks and praise from all the men of Troy, <br> And chiefly from Prince Paris, who will fill, <br> Foremost of all, thy hands with lavish gifts, <br> When he shall look on Menelaus slain⁠— <br> The warlike son of Atreus⁠—by thy hand, <br> And laid upon his lofty funeral pile. <br> Aim now at Menelaus the renowned <br> An arrow, while thou offerest a vow <br> To Lycian Phoebus, mighty with the bow, <br> That thou wilt bring to him a hecatomb <br> Of firstling lambs, when thou again shalt come <br> Within thine own Zeleia’s sacred walls.”</p>
<p class="verse">So spake Minerva, and her words o’ercame <br> The weak one’s purpose. He uncovered straight <br> His polished bow, made of the elastic horns <br> Of a wild goat, which, from his lurking-place, <br> As once it left its cavern lair, he smote, <br> And pierced its breast, and stretched it on the rock. <br> Full sixteen palms in length the horns had grown <br> From the goat’s forehead. These an artisan <br> Had smoothed, and, aptly fitting each to each, <br> Polished the whole and tipped the work with gold. <br> To bend that bow, the warrior lowered it <br> And pressed an end against the earth. His friends <br> Held up, meanwhile, their shields before his face, <br> Lest the brave sons of Greece should lift their spears <br> Against him ere the champion of their host, <br> The warlike Menelaus, should have felt <br> The arrow. Then the Lycian drew aside <br> The cover from his quiver, taking out <br> A well-fledged arrow that had never flown⁠— <br> A cause of future sorrows. On the string <br> He laid that fatal arrow, while he made <br> To Lycian Phoebus, mighty with the bow, <br> A vow to sacrifice before his shrine <br> A noble hecatomb of firstling lambs <br> When he should come again to his abode <br> Within his own Zeleia’s sacred walls. <br> Grasping the bowstring and the arrow’s notch, <br> He drew them back, and forced the string to meet <br> His breast, the arrow-head to meet the bow, <br> Till the bow formed a circle. Then it twanged. <br> The cord gave out a shrilly sound; the shaft <br> Leaped forth in eager haste to reach the host.</p>
<p class="verse">Yet, Menelaus, then the blessed gods, <br> The deathless ones, forgot thee not; and first, <br> Jove’s daughter, gatherer of spoil, who stood <br> Before thee, turned aside the deadly shaft. <br> As when a mother, while her child is wrapped <br> In a sweet slumber, scares away the fly, <br> So Pallas turned the weapon from thy breast, <br> And guided it to where the golden clasps <br> Made fast the belt, and where the corselet’s mail <br> Was doubled. There the bitter arrow struck <br> The belt, and through its close contexture passed, <br> And fixed within the well-wrought corselet stood, <br> Yet reached the plated quilt which next his skin <br> The hero wore⁠—his surest guard against <br> The weapon’s force⁠—and broke through that alike; <br> And there the arrow gashed the part below, <br> And the dark blood came gushing from the wound. <br> As when some Carian or Maeonian dame <br> Tinges with purple the white ivory, <br> To form a trapping for the cheeks of steeds⁠— <br> And many a horseman covets it, yet still <br> It lies within her chamber, to become <br> The ornament of some great monarch’s steed <br> And make its rider proud⁠—thy shapely thighs, <br> Thy legs, and thy fair ankles thus were stained, <br> O Menelaus! with thy purple blood.</p>
<p class="verse">When Agamemnon, king of men, beheld <br> The dark blood flowing from his brother’s wound, <br> He shuddered. Menelaus, great in war, <br> Felt the like horror; yet, when he perceived <br> That still the arrow, neck and barb, remained <br> Without the mail, the courage rose again <br> That filled his bosom. Agamemnon, then, <br> The monarch, sighing deeply, took the hand <br> Of Menelaus⁠—while his comrades round <br> Like him lamented⁠—sighing as he spake:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Dear brother, when I sent thee forth alone <br> To combat with the Trojans for the Greeks, <br> I ratified a treaty for thy death, <br> Since now the Trojans smite and under foot <br> Trample the league. Yet not in vain shall be <br> The treaty, nor the blood of lambs, nor wine <br> Poured to the gods, nor right hands firmly pledged; <br> For though it please not now Olympian Jove <br> To make the treaty good, he will in time <br> Cause it to be fulfilled, and they shall pay <br> Dearly with their own heads and with their wives <br> And children for this wrong. And this I know <br> In my undoubting mind⁠—a day will come <br> When sacred Troy and Priam and the race <br> Governed by Priam, mighty with the spear, <br> Shall perish all. Saturnian Jove, who sits <br> On high, a dweller of the upper air, <br> Shall shake his dreadful aegis in the sight <br> Of all, indignant at this treachery. <br> Such the event will be; but I shall grieve <br> Bitterly, Menelaus, if thou die, <br> Thy term of life cut short. I shall go back <br> To my dear Argos with a brand of shame <br> Upon me. For the Greeks will soon again <br> Bethink them of their country; we shall then <br> Leave Argive Helen to remain the boast <br> Of Priam and the Trojans⁠—while thy bones <br> Shall moulder, mingling with the earth of Troy⁠— <br> Our great design abandoned. Then shall say <br> Some haughty Trojan, leaping on the tomb <br> Of Menelaus: ‘So in time to come <br> May Agamemnon wreak his wrath, as here <br> He wreaked it, whither he had vainly led <br> An army, and now hastens to his home <br> And his own land, with ships that bear no spoil, <br> And the brave Menelaus left behind.’ <br> So shall some Trojan say; but, ere that time, <br> May the earth open to receive my bones!”</p>
<p class="verse">The fair-haired Menelaus cheerfully <br> Replied: “Grieve not, nor be the Greeks alarmed <br> For me, since this sharp arrow has not found <br> A vital part, but, ere it reached so far, <br> The embroidered belt, the quilt beneath, and plate <br> Wrought by the armorer’s cunning, broke its force.”</p>
<p class="verse">King Agamemnon took the word and said:⁠— <br> “Dear Menelaus! Would that it were so, <br> Yet the physician must explore thy wound, <br> And with his balsams soothe the bitter pain.” <br> Then turning to Talthybius, he addressed <br> The sacred herald: “Hasten with all speed, <br> Talthybius; call Machaon, warrior-son <br> Of Aesculapius, that much-honored leech, <br> And bring him to the Achaian general, <br> The warlike Menelaus, whom some hand <br> Of Trojan or of Lycian, skilled to bend <br> The bow, hath wounded with his shaft⁠—a deed <br> For him to exult in, but a grief to us.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake; nor failed the herald to obey, <br> But hastened at the word and passed among <br> The squadrons of Achaia, mailed in brass, <br> In search of great Machaon. Him he found <br> As midst the valiant ranks of bucklered men <br> He stood⁠—the troops who followed him to war <br> From Triccae, nurse of steeds. Then, drawing near, <br> The herald spake to him in wingèd words:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“O son of Aesculapius, come in haste. <br> King Agamemnon calls thee to the aid <br> Of warlike Menelaus, whom some hand <br> Of Trojan or of Lycian, skilled to bend <br> The bow, hath wounded with his shaft⁠—a deed <br> For him to exult in, but a grief to us.”</p>
<p class="verse">Machaon’s heart was touched, and forth they went <br> Through the great throng, the army of the Greeks. <br> And when they came where Atreus’ warlike son <br> Was wounded, they perceived the godlike man <br> Standing amid a circle of the chiefs, <br> The bravest of the Achaians, who at once <br> Had gathered round. Without delay he drew <br> The arrow from the fairly-fitted belt. <br> The barbs were bent in drawing. Then he loosed <br> The embroidered belt, the quilted vest beneath, <br> And plate⁠—the armorer’s work⁠—and carefully <br> O’erlooked the wound where fell the bitter shaft, <br> Cleansed it from blood, and sprinkled over it <br> With skill the soothing balsams which of yore <br> The friendly Chiron to his father gave.</p>
<p class="verse">While round the warlike Menelaus thus <br> The chiefs were busy, all the Trojans moved <br> Into array of battle; they put on <br> Their armor, and were eager for the fight. <br> Then wouldst thou not have seen, hadst thou been there, <br> King Agamemnon slumbering, or in fear, <br> And skulking from the combat, but alert, <br> Preparing for the glorious tasks of war. <br> His horses, and his chariot bright with brass, <br> He left, and bade Eurymedon, his groom, <br> The son of Ptolemy Piraides, <br> Hold them apart still panting, yet with charge <br> To keep them near their master, till the hour <br> When he should need them, weary with the toil <br> Of such a vast command. Meantime he went <br> On foot among his files of soldiery, <br> And whomsoe’er he found with fiery steeds <br> Hasting to battle, thus he cheered them on:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“O Argives! Let not your hot courage cool, <br> For Father Jove will never take the part <br> Of treachery. Whosoe’er have been the first <br> To break the league, upon their lifeless limbs <br> Shall vultures feast; and doubt not we shall bear <br> Away in our good ships the wives they love <br> And their young children, when we take their town.”</p>
<p class="verse">But whomsoe’er he saw that kept afar <br> From the dread field, he angrily rebuked:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“O Argives! Who with arrows only fight, <br> Base as ye are, have ye no sense of shame? <br> Why stand ye stupefied, like fawns, that, tired <br> With coursing the wide pastures, stop at last, <br> Their strength exhausted! Thus ye stand amazed, <br> Nor think of combat. Wait ye for the hour <br> When to your ships, with their fair-sculptured prows, <br> Moored on the borders of the hoary deep, <br> The Trojans come, that haply ye may see <br> If the great hand of Jove will shield you then?”</p>
<p class="verse">Thus Agamemnon, as supreme in power, <br> Threaded the warrior-files, until he came <br> Where stood the Cretans. All in arms they stood <br> Around Idomeneus, the great in war. <br> Like a wild boar in strength, he led the van, <br> And, in the rear, Meriones urged on <br> His phalanxes. The king of men rejoiced, <br> And blandly thus bespake Idomeneus:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Idomeneus! I honor thee above <br> The other knights of Greece, as well in war <br> As in all other labors, and no less <br> In banquets, when the Achaian nobles charge <br> Their goblets with the dark-red mingled wine <br> In sign of honor. All the other Greeks <br> Drink by a certain measure, but thy cup <br> Stands ever full, like mine, that thou mayst drink <br> When thou desirest. Hasten to the war <br> With all the valor thou dost glory in.”</p>
<p class="verse">The Cretan chief, Idomeneus, replied:⁠— <br> “Atrides, I remain thy true ally, <br> As I have pledged my faith. But thou exhort <br> The other long-haired Greeks, and bid them rush <br> To combat, since the Trojans break their oath. <br> For woe and death must be the lot of those <br> Who broke the peace they vowed so solemnly.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake. The son of Atreus, glad at heart, <br> Passed on among the squadrons, till he came <br> To where the warriors Ajax formed their ranks <br> For battle, with a cloud of infantry. <br> As when some goatherd from the hill-top sees <br> A cloud that traverses the deep before <br> A strong west wind⁠—beholding it afar, <br> Pitch-black it seems, and bringing o’er the waves <br> A whirlwind with it; he is seized with fear, <br> And drives his flock to shelter in a cave⁠— <br> So with the warriors Ajax to the war <br> Moved, dense and dark, the phalanxes of youths <br> Trained for the combat, and their serried files <br> Bristling with spears and shields. The king of men <br> Saw with delight, and spake these wingèd words:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“O warriors Ajax, leaders of the Greeks <br> In brazen armor, I enjoin you not <br> To rouse the courage of your soldiery. <br> Such word would ill become me, for yourselves <br> Have made your followers eager to engage <br> In manful combat. Would to Jupiter, <br> To Pallas, and Apollo, that there dwelt <br> In every bosom such a soul as yours! <br> Then would the city of King Priam fall <br> At once, o’erthrown and levelled by our hands.”</p>
<p class="verse">Thus having said, he left them and went on <br> To others. There he found the smooth of speech, <br> Nestor, the Pylian orator, employed <br> In marshalling his squadrons. Near to him <br> Alastor and the large-limbed Pelagon, <br> Chromius, and Haemon, prince among his tribe, <br> And Bias, shepherd of the people, stood. <br> The cavalry with steeds and cars he placed <br> In front. A vast and valiant multitude <br> Of infantry he stationed in the rear, <br> To be the bulwark of the war. Between <br> He made the faint of spirit take their place, <br> That, though unwillingly, they might be forced <br> To combat with the rest. And first he gave <br> His orders to the horsemen, bidding them <br> To keep their coursers reined, nor let them range <br> At random through the tumult of the crowd:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“And let no man, too vain of horsemanship, <br> And trusting in his valor, dare advance <br> Beyond the rest to attack the men of Troy, <br> Nor let him fall behind the rest, to make <br> Our ranks the weaker. Whoso from his car <br> Can reach an enemy’s, let him stand and strike <br> With his long spear, for ’tis the shrewder way. <br> By rules like these, which their brave hearts obeyed, <br> The men of yore laid level towns and towers.”</p>
<p class="verse">The aged man, long versed in tasks of war, <br> Counselled them thus. King Agamemnon heard, <br> Delighted, and in wingèd words he said:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“O aged man, would that thy knees were firm <br> As is thy purpose, and thy strength as great! <br> But age, the common fate of all, has worn <br> Thy frame: would that some others had thy age, <br> And thou wert of the number of our youths!”</p>
<p class="verse">Then answered Nestor, the Gerenian knight:⁠— <br> “O son of Atreus, I myself could wish <br> That I were now as when of yore I struck <br> The high-born Ereuthalion down. The gods <br> Bestow not all their gifts on man at once. <br> If I were then a youth, old age in turn <br> Is creeping o’er me. Still I keep among <br> The knights, and counsel and admonish them⁠— <br> The office of the aged. Younger men, <br> They who can trust their strength, must wield the spear.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake. The son of Atreus passed him by, <br> Pleased with his words, and, moving onward, came <br> Where⁠—with the Athenians, ever prompt to raise <br> The war-cry, grouped around him⁠—stood the knight <br> Menestheus, son of Peteus. Near to these <br> Was wise Ulysses, with his sturdy band <br> Of Cephalonians. None of these had heard <br> The clamor of the battle, for the hosts <br> Of Trojan knights and Greeks had just begun <br> To move, and there they waited for the advance <br> Of other squadrons marching on to charge <br> The Trojans and begin the war anew. <br> The king of men, Atrides, was displeased, <br> And spake, and chid them thus with wingèd words:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“O son of Peteus, foster-child of Jove, <br> And thou, the man of craft and evil wiles! <br> Why stand ye here aloof, irresolute, <br> And wait for others? Ye should be the first <br> To meet the foe and stem the battle’s rage. <br> I bid you first to banquets which the Greeks <br> Give to their leaders, where ye feast at will <br> On roasted meats and bowls of pleasant wine. <br> Now, ere ye move, ye willingly would see <br> Ten Grecian squadrons join the deadly strife.”</p>
<p class="verse">The man of many arts, Ulysses, spake, <br> And frowned: “O Atreus’ son! What words are those <br> Which pass thy lips? How canst thou say that we <br> Avoid the battle? Ever when the Greeks <br> Seek bloody conflict with the Trojan knights, <br> Thou, if thou wilt, and if thou givest heed <br> To things like these, shalt with thine eyes behold <br> The father of Telemachus engaged <br> In combat with the foremost knights that form <br> The Trojan van. Thou utterest empty words.”</p>
<p class="verse">King Agamemnon, when he saw the chief <br> Offended, changed his tone, and, smiling, said:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Son of Laertes, nobly-born and wise <br> Ulysses! It is not for me to chide <br> Nor to exhort thee, for thy heart, I know, <br> Counsels thee kindly toward me, and thy thought <br> Agrees with mine. We will discuss all this <br> Hereafter. If just now too harsh a word <br> Was uttered, may the immortals make it vain!”</p>
<p class="verse">So saying, he departed, and went on <br> To others. By his steeds and by his car, <br> That shone with fastenings of brass, he found <br> The son of Tydeus, large-souled Diomed, <br> And Sthenelus, the son of Capaneus, <br> Standing beside him. Looking at them both, <br> King Agamemnon to Tydides spake <br> In wingèd words, and thus reproved the chief:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“O son of Tydeus, that undaunted knight! <br> What is there to appall thee? Why look through <br> The spaces that divide the warlike ranks? <br> Not thus did Tydeus feel the touch of fear, <br> But ever foremost of his warriors fought. <br> So they declare who saw his deeds, for I <br> Was never with him, nor have ever seen <br> The hero. Yet they say that he excelled <br> All others. Certain is it that he once <br> Entered Mycenae as a friendly guest, <br> With no array of soldiery, but came <br> With godlike Polynices. ’Twas the time <br> When warrior-bands were gathered to besiege <br> The sacred walls of Thebes, and earnestly <br> They prayed that from Mycenae they might lead <br> Renowned auxiliars to the war, and we <br> Would willingly have given the aid they asked⁠— <br> For we approved the prayer⁠—but Jove, with signs <br> Of angry omen, changed our purposes. <br> The chiefs departed, journeying on to where <br> Asopus flows through reeds and grass, and thence <br> The Achaians sent an embassy to Thebes <br> By Tydeus. There he met the many sons <br> Of Cadmus at the banquets in the hall <br> Of valiant Eteocles. Though alone <br> Among so many, and a stranger guest, <br> The hero feared them not, but challenged them <br> To vie with him in games; and easily <br> He won the victory, such aid was given <br> By Pallas. Then the sons of Cadmus, skilled <br> In horsemanship, were wroth, and privily <br> Sent fifty armed youths to lie in wait <br> For his return. Two leaders had the band⁠— <br> Maion, the son of Harmon, like a god <br> In form, and Lycophontes, brave in war, <br> Son of Autophonos. A bloody death <br> Did Tydeus give the youths. He slew them all <br> Save Maion, whom he suffered to return, <br> Obedient to an omen from the gods. <br> Such was Aetolian Tydeus; but his son, <br> A better speaker, is less brave in war.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake; and valiant Diomed, who heard <br> The king’s reproof with reverence, answered not. <br> Then spake the son of honored Capaneus:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Atrides, speak not falsely, when thou know’st <br> The truth so well. Assuredly we claim <br> To be far braver than our fathers were. <br> We took seven-gated Thebes with fewer troops <br> Than theirs, when, trusting in the omens sent <br> From heaven, and in the aid of Jupiter, so <br> We led our men beneath the city walls <br> Sacred to Mars. Our fathers perished there <br> Through their own folly. Therefore never seek <br> To place them in the same degree with us.”</p>
<p class="verse">The brave Tydides with a frown replied:⁠— <br> “Nay, hold thy peace, my friend, and heed my words. <br> Of Agamemnon I will not complain⁠— <br> The shepherd of the people; it is his <br> To exhort the well-armed Greeks to gallant deeds. <br> Great glory will attend him if the Greeks <br> Shall overcome the Trojans, and shall take <br> The sacred Ilium; but his grief will be <br> Bitter if we shall fail and be destroyed. <br> Hence think we only of the furious charge!”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake, and from his chariot leaped to earth <br> All armed; the mail upon the monarch’s breast <br> Rang terribly as he marched swiftly on. <br> The boldest might have heard that sound with fear.</p>
<p class="verse">As when the ocean-billows, surge on surge, <br> Are pushed along to the resounding shore <br> Before the western wind, and first a wave <br> Uplifts itself, and then against the land <br> Dashes and roars, and round the headland peaks <br> Tosses on high and spouts its spray afar, <br> So moved the serried phalanxes of Greece <br> To battle, rank succeeding rank, each chief <br> Giving command to his own troops; the rest <br> Marched noiselessly: you might have thought no voice <br> Was in the breasts of all that mighty throng, <br> So silently they all obeyed their chiefs, <br> Their showy armor glittering as they moved <br> In firm array. But, as the numerous flock <br> Of some rich man, while the white milk is drawn <br> Within his sheepfold, hear the plaintive call <br> Of their own lambs, and bleat incessantly⁠— <br> Such clamors from the mighty Trojan host <br> Arose; nor was the war-cry one, nor one <br> The voice, but words of mingled languages, <br> For they were called from many different climes. <br> These Mars encouraged to the fight; but those <br> The blue-eyed Pallas. Terror too was there, <br> And Fright, and Strife that rages unappeased⁠— <br> Sister and comrade of man-slaying Mars⁠— <br> Who rises small at first, but grows, and lifts <br> Her head to heaven and walks upon the earth. <br> She, striding through the crowd and heightening <br> The mutual rancor, flung into the midst <br> Contention, source of bale to all alike.</p>
<p class="verse">And now, when met the armies in the field, <br> The ox-hide shields encountered, and the spears, <br> And might of warriors mailed in brass; then clashed <br> The bossy bucklers, and the battle-din <br> Was loud; then rose the mingled shouts and groans <br> Of those who slew and those who fell; the earth <br> Ran with their blood. As when the winter streams <br> Rush down the mountain-sides, and fill, below, <br> With their swift waters, poured from gushing springs, <br> Some hollow vale, the shepherd on the heights <br> Hears the far roar⁠—such was the mingled din <br> That rose from the great armies when they met.</p>
<p class="verse">Then first Antilochus, advancing, struck <br> The Trojan champion Echepolus down, <br> Son of Thalysius, fighting in the van. <br> He smote him on the helmet’s cone, where streamed <br> The horse-hair plume. The brazen javelin stood <br> Fixed in his forehead, piercing through the bone, <br> And darkness gathered o’er his eyes. He fell <br> As falls a tower before some stubborn siege. <br> Then Elephenor, son of Chalcodon, <br> Prince of the brave Abrantes, by the foot <br> Seized the slain chieftain, dragging him beyond <br> The reach of darts, to strip him of his arms; <br> Yet dropped him soon, for brave Agenor saw, <br> And, as he stooped to drag the body, hurled <br> His brazen spear and pierced the uncovered side <br> Seen underneath the shield. At once his limbs <br> Relaxed their hold, and straight the spirit fled. <br> Then furious was the struggle of the Greeks <br> And Trojans o’er the slain; they sprang like wolves <br> Upon each other, and man slaughtered man.</p>
<p class="verse">Then by the hand of Ajax Telamon <br> Fell Simoisius, in the bloom of youth, <br> Anthemion’s son. His mother once came down <br> From Ida, with her parents, to their flocks <br> Beside the Simoïs; there she brought him forth <br> Upon its banks, and gave her boy the name <br> Of Simoisius. Unrequited now <br> Was all the care with which his parents nursed <br> His early years, and short his term of life⁠— <br> Slain by the hand of Ajax, large of soul. <br> For, when he saw him coming, Ajax smote <br> Near the right pap the Trojan’s breast; the blade <br> Passed through, and out upon the further side. <br> He fell among the dust of earth, as falls <br> A poplar growing in the watery soil <br> Of some wide marsh⁠—a fair, smooth bole, with boughs <br> Only on high, which with his gleaming axe <br> Some artisan has felled to bend its trunk <br> Into the circle of some chariot-wheel; <br> Withering it lies upon the river’s bank. <br> So did the high-born Ajax spoil the corpse <br> Of Simoisius, Anthemion’s son. <br> But Antiphus, the son of Priam, clad <br> In shining armor, saw, and, taking aim, <br> Cast his sharp spear at Ajax through the crowd. <br> The weapon struck him not, but pierced the groin <br> Of one who was Ulysses’ faithful friend⁠— <br> Leucus⁠—as from the spot he dragged the dead; <br> He fell, the body dropping from his hold. <br> Ulysses, stung with fury at his fall, <br> Rushed to the van, arrayed in shining brass, <br> Drew near the foe, and, casting a quick glance <br> Around him, hurled his glittering spear. The host <br> Of Trojans, as it left his hand, shrank back <br> Upon each other. Not in vain it flew, <br> But struck Democoön, the spurious son <br> Of Priam, who, to join the war, had left <br> Abydos, where he tended the swift mares. <br> Ulysses, to revenge his comrade’s death, <br> Smote him upon the temple with his spear. <br> Through both the temples passed the brazen point, <br> And darkness gathered o’er his eyes; he fell, <br> His armor clashing round him with his fall. <br> Then did the foremost bands, and Hector’s self, <br> Fall back. The Argives shouted, dragging off <br> The slain, and rushing to the ground they won. <br> Then was Apollo angered, looking down <br> From Pergamus, and thus he called aloud:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Rally, ye Trojans! Tamers of fleet steeds! <br> Yield not the battle to the Greeks. Their limbs <br> Are not of stone or iron, to withstand <br> The trenchant steel ye wield. Nor does the son <br> Of fair-haired Thetis now, Achilles, take <br> Part in the battle, but sits, brooding o’er <br> The choler that devours him, in his ships.” <br> Thus from the city spake the terrible god. <br> Meantime Tritonian Pallas, glorious child <br> Of Jupiter, went through the Grecian ranks <br> Where’er they wavered, and revived their zeal.</p>
<p class="verse">Diores, son of Amarynceus, then <br> Met his hard fate. The fragment of a rock <br> Was thrown by hand at his right leg, and struck <br> The ankle. Piroüs, son of Imbrasus, <br> Who came from Aenus, leading to the war <br> His Thracian soldiers, flung it; and it crushed <br> Tendons and bones, and down the warrior fell <br> In dust, and toward his comrades stretched his hands, <br> And gasped for breath. But he who gave the wound, <br> Piroüs, came up and pierced him with his spear. <br> Forth gushed the entrails, and the eyes grew dark.</p>
<p class="verse">But Piroüs by Aetolian Thoas fell, <br> Who met him with his spear and pierced his breast <br> Above the pap. The brazen weapon stood <br> Fixed in the lungs. Then Thoas came and plucked <br> The massive spear away, and drew his sword, <br> And thrusting through him the sharp blade, he took <br> His life away. Yet could he not despoil <br> The slain man of his armor, for around <br> His comrades thronged, the Thracians, with their tufts <br> Of streaming hair, and, wielding their long spears, <br> Drove him away. And he, though huge of limb, <br> And valiant and renowned, was forced to yield <br> To numbers pressing on him, and withdrew. <br> Thus near each other stretched upon the ground <br> Piroüs, the leader of the Thracian band, <br> And he who led the Epeans, brazen-mailed <br> Diores, lay with many others slain.</p>
<p class="verse">Then could no man, who near at hand beheld <br> The battle of that day, see cause of blame <br> In aught, although, unwounded and unbruised <br> By weapons, Pallas led him by the hand <br> In safety through the midst, and turned aside <br> The violence of javelins; for that day <br> Saw many a Trojan slain, and many a Greek, <br> Stretched side by side upon the bloody field.</p>
