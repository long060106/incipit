<p class="lede">The visit of Chryses, Priest of Apollo, to Agamemnon, asking the ransom of his daughter⁠—Refusal of Agamemnon⁠—A pestilence sent by Apollo upon the Greek army⁠—A Council called by Achilles⁠—The cause of the pestilence declared by the Seer Calchas⁠—Dispute between Agamemnon and Achilles, which ends with the taking away of Briseis from Achilles⁠—The daughter of Chryses restored to him⁠—Visit of Thetis to Jupiter, who promises to avenge Achilles⁠—Mutual chiding of Jupiter and Juno.</p>
<p class="verse">O Goddess! Sing the wrath of Peleus’ son, <br> Achilles; sing the deadly wrath that brought <br> Woes numberless upon the Greeks, and swept <br> To Hades many a valiant soul, and gave <br> Their limbs a prey to dogs and birds of air⁠— <br> For so had Jove appointed⁠—from the time <br> When the two chiefs, Atrides, king of men, <br> And great Achilles, parted first as foes.</p>
<p class="verse">Which of the gods put strife between the chiefs, <br> That they should thus contend? Latona’s son <br> And Jove’s. Incensed against the king, he bade <br> A deadly pestilence appear among <br> The army, and the men were perishing. <br> For Atreus’ son with insult had received <br> Chryses the priest, who to the Grecian fleet <br> Came to redeem his daughter, offering <br> Uncounted ransom. In his hand he bore <br> The fillets of Apollo, archer-god, <br> Upon the golden sceptre, and he sued <br> To all the Greeks, but chiefly to the sons <br> Of Atreus, the two leaders of the host:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Ye sons of Atreus, and ye other chiefs, <br> Well-greaved Achaians, may the gods who dwell <br> Upon Olympus give you to o’erthrow <br> The city of Priam, and in safety reach <br> Your homes; but give me my beloved child, <br> And take her ransom, honoring him who sends <br> His arrows far, Apollo, son of Jove.”</p>
<p class="verse">Then all the other Greeks, applauding, bade <br> Revere the priest and take the liberal gifts <br> He offered, but the counsel did not please <br> Atrides Agamemnon; he dismissed <br> The priest with scorn, and added threatening words:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Old man, let me not find thee loitering here, <br> Beside the roomy ships, or coming back <br> Hereafter, lest the fillet thou dost bear <br> And sceptre of thy god protect thee not. <br> This maiden I release not till old age <br> Shall overtake her in my Argive home, <br> Far from her native country, where her hand <br> Shall throw the shuttle and shall dress my couch. <br> Go, chafe me not, if thou wouldst safely go.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake; the aged man in fear obeyed <br> The mandate, and in silence walked apart, <br> Along the many-sounding ocean-side, <br> And fervently he prayed the monarch-god, <br> Apollo, golden-haired Latona’s son:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Hear me, thou bearer of the silver bow, <br> Who guardest Chrysa, and the holy isle <br> Of Cilia, and art lord in Tenedos, <br> O Smintheus! If I ever helped to deck <br> Thy glorious temple, if I ever burned <br> Upon thy altar the fat thighs of goats <br> And bullocks, grant my prayer, and let thy shafts <br> Avenge upon the Greeks the tears I shed.”</p>
<p class="verse">So spake he supplicating, and to him <br> Phoebus Apollo hearkened. Down he came, <br> Down from the summit of the Olympian mount, <br> Wrathful in heart; his shoulders bore the bow <br> And hollow quiver; there the arrows rang <br> Upon the shoulders of the angry god, <br> As on he moved. He came as comes the night, <br> And, seated from the ships aloof, sent forth <br> An arrow; terrible was heard the clang <br> Of that resplendent bow. At first he smote <br> The mules and the swift dogs, and then on man <br> He turned the deadly arrow. All around <br> Glared evermore the frequent funeral piles. <br> Nine days already had his shafts been showered <br> Among the host, and now, upon the tenth, <br> Achilles called the people of the camp <br> To council. Juno, of the snow-white arms, <br> Had moved his mind to this, for she beheld <br> With sorrow that the men were perishing. <br> And when the assembly met and now was full, <br> Stood swift Achilles in the midst and said:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“To me it seems, Atrides, that ’twere well, <br> Since now our aim is baffled, to return <br> Homeward, if death o’ertake us not; for war <br> And pestilence at once destroy the Greeks. <br> But let us first consult some seer or priest, <br> Or dream-interpreter⁠—for even dreams <br> Are sent by Jove⁠—and ask him by what cause <br> Phoebus Apollo has been angered thus; <br> If by neglected vows or hecatombs, <br> And whether savor of fat bulls and goats <br> May move the god to stay the pestilence.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake, and took again his seat; and next <br> Rose Calchas, son of Thestor, and the chief <br> Of augurs, one to whom were known things past <br> And present and to come. He, through the art <br> Of divination, which Apollo gave, <br> Had guided Iliumward the ships of Greece. <br> With words well ordered courteously he spake:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Achilles, loved of Jove, thou biddest me <br> Explain the wrath of Phoebus, monarch-god, <br> Who sends afar his arrows. Willingly <br> Will I make known the cause; but covenant thou, <br> And swear to stand prepared, by word and hand, <br> To bring me succor. For my mind misgives <br> That he who rules the Argives, and to whom <br> The Achaian race are subject, will be wroth. <br> A sovereign is too strong for humbler men, <br> And though he keep his choler down awhile, <br> It rankles, till he sate it, in his heart. <br> And now consider: wilt thou hold me safe?”</p>
<p class="verse">Achilles, the swift-footed, answered thus:⁠— <br> “Fear nothing, but speak boldly out whate’er <br> Thou knowest, and declare the will of Heaven. <br> For by Apollo, dear to Jove, whom thou, <br> Calchas, dost pray to, when thou givest forth <br> The sacred oracles to men of Greece, <br> No man, while yet I live, and see the light <br> Of day, shall lay a violent hand on thee <br> Among our roomy ships; no man of all <br> The Grecian armies, though thou name the name <br> Of Agamemnon, whose high boast it is <br> To stand in power and rank above them all.”</p>
<p class="verse">Encouraged thus, the blameless seer went on:⁠— <br> “ ’Tis not neglected vows or hecatombs <br> That move him, but the insult shown his priest, <br> Whom Agamemnon spurned, when he refused <br> To set his daughter free, and to receive <br> Her ransom. Therefore sends the archer-god <br> These woes, and still will send them on the Greeks, <br> Nor ever will withdraw his heavy hand <br> From our destruction, till the dark-eyed maid <br> Freely, and without ransom, be restored <br> To her beloved father, and with her <br> A sacred hecatomb to Chrysa sent. <br> So may we haply pacify the god.”</p>
<p class="verse">Thus having said, the augur took his seat. <br> And then the hero-son of Atreus rose, <br> Wide-ruling Agamemnon, greatly chafed. <br> His gloomy heart was full of wrath, his eyes <br> Sparkled like fire; he fixed a menacing look <br> Full on the augur Calchas, and began:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Prophet of evil! Never hadst thou yet <br> A cheerful word for me. To mark the signs <br> Of coming mischief is thy great delight. <br> Good dost thou ne’er foretell nor bring to pass. <br> And now thou pratest, in thine auguries, <br> Before the Greeks, how that the archer-god <br> Afflicts us thus, because I would not take <br> The costly ransom offered to redeem <br> The virgin child of Chryses. ’Twas my choice <br> To keep her with me, for I prize her more <br> Than Clytemnestra, bride of my young years, <br> And deem her not less nobly graced than she, <br> In form and feature, mind and pleasing arts. <br> Yet will I give her back, if that be best; <br> For gladly would I see my people saved <br> From this destruction. Let meet recompense, <br> Meantime, be ready, that I be not left, <br> Alone of all the Greeks, without my prize. <br> That were not seemly. All of you perceive <br> That now my share of spoil has passed from me.”</p>
<p class="verse">To him the great Achilles, swift of foot, <br> Replied: “Renowned Atrides, greediest <br> Of men, where wilt thou that our noble Greeks <br> Find other spoil for thee, since none is set <br> Apart, a common store? The trophies brought <br> From towns which we have sacked have all been shared <br> Among us, and we could not without shame <br> Bid every warrior bring his portion back. <br> Yield, then, the maiden to the god, and we, <br> The Achaians, freely will appoint for thee <br> Threefold and fourfold recompense, should Jove <br> Give up to sack this well-defended Troy.”</p>
<p class="verse">Then the king Agamemnon answered thus:⁠— <br> “Nay, use no craft, all valiant as thou art, <br> Godlike Achilles; thou hast not the power <br> To circumvent nor to persuade me thus. <br> Think’st thou that, while thou keepest safe thy prize, <br> I shall sit idly down, deprived of mine? <br> Thou bid’st me give the maiden back. ’Tis well, <br> If to my hands the noble Greeks shall bring <br> The worth of what I lose, and in a shape <br> That pleases me. Else will I come myself, <br> And seize and bear away thy prize, or that <br> Of Ajax or Ulysses, leaving him <br> From whom I take his share with cause for rage. <br> Another time we will confer of this. <br> Now come, and forth into the great salt sea <br> Launch a black ship, and muster on the deck <br> Men skilled to row, and put a hecatomb <br> On board, and let the fair-cheeked maid embark, <br> Chryseis. Send a prince to bear command⁠— <br> Ajax, Idomeneus, or the divine <br> Ulysses;⁠—or thyself, Pelides, thou <br> Most terrible of men, that with due rites <br> Thou soothe the anger of the archer-god.”</p>
<p class="verse">Achilles the swift-footed, with stern look, <br> Thus answered: “Ha, thou mailed in impudence <br> And bent on lucre! Who of all the Greeks <br> Can willingly obey thee, on the march, <br> Or bravely battling with the enemy? <br> I came not to this war because of wrong <br> Done to me by the valiant sons of Troy. <br> No feud had I with them; they never took <br> My beeves or horses, nor, in Phthia’s realm, <br> Deep-soiled and populous, spoiled my harvest fields. <br> For many a shadowy mount between us lies, <br> And waters of the wide-resounding sea. <br> Man unabashed! We follow thee that thou <br> Mayst glory in avenging upon Troy <br> The grudge of Menelaus and thy own, <br> Thou shameless one! And yet thou hast for this <br> Nor thanks nor care. Thou threatenest now to take <br> From me the prize for which I bore long toils <br> In battle; and the Greeks decreed it mine. <br> I never take an equal share with thee <br> Of booty when the Grecian host has sacked <br> Some populous Trojan town. My hands perform <br> The harder labors of the field in all <br> The tumult of the fight; but when the spoil <br> Is shared, the largest share of all is thine, <br> While I, content with little, seek my ships, <br> Weary with combat. I shall now go home <br> To Phthia; better were it to return <br> With my beaked ships; but here, where I am held <br> In little honor, thou wilt fail, I think, <br> To gather, in large measure, spoil and wealth.”</p>
<p class="verse">Him answered Agamemnon, king of men:⁠— <br> “Desert, then, if thou wilt; I ask thee not <br> To stay for me; there will be others left <br> To do me honor yet, and, best of all, <br> The all-providing Jove is with me still. <br> Thee I detest the most of all the men <br> Ordained by him to govern; thy delight <br> Is in contention, war, and bloody frays. <br> If thou art brave, some deity, no doubt, <br> Hath thus endowed thee. Hence, then, to thy home, <br> With all thy ships and men! There domineer <br> Over thy Myrmidons; I heed thee not, <br> Nor care I for thy fury. Thus, in turn, <br> I threaten thee; since Phoebus takes away <br> Chryseis, I will send her in my ship <br> And with my friends, and, coming to thy tent, <br> Will bear away the fair-cheeked maid, thy prize, <br> Briseis, that thou learn how far I stand <br> Above thee, and that other chiefs may fear <br> To measure strength with me, and brave my power.”</p>
<p class="verse">The rage of Peleus’ son, as thus he spake, <br> Grew fiercer; in that shaggy breast his heart <br> Took counsel, whether from his thigh to draw <br> The trenchant sword, and, thrusting back the rest, <br> Smite down Atrides, or subdue his wrath <br> And master his own spirit. While he thus <br> Debated with himself, and half unsheathed <br> The ponderous blade, Pallas Athene came, <br> Sent from on high by Juno, the white-armèd, <br> Who loved both warriors and made both her care. <br> She came behind him, seen by him alone, <br> And plucked his yellow hair. The hero turned <br> In wonder, and at once he knew the look <br> Of Pallas and the awful-gleaming eye, <br> And thus accosted her with wingèd words:⁠— <br> “Why com’st thou hither, daughter of the god <br> Who bears the aegis? Art thou here to see <br> The insolence of Agamemnon, son <br> Of Atreus? Let me tell thee what I deem <br> Will be the event. That man may lose his life, <br> And quickly too, for arrogance like this.”</p>
<p class="verse">Then thus the goddess, blue-eyed Pallas, spake:⁠— <br> “I came from heaven to pacify thy wrath, <br> If thou wilt heed my counsel. I am sent <br> By Juno the white-armed, to whom ye both <br> Are dear, who ever watches o’er you both. <br> Refrain from violence; let not thy hand <br> Unsheathe the sword, but utter with thy tongue <br> Reproaches, as occasion may arise, <br> For I declare what time shall bring to pass; <br> Threefold amends shall yet be offered thee, <br> In gifts of princely cost, for this day’s wrong. <br> Now calm thy angry spirit, and obey.”</p>
<p class="verse">Achilles, the swift-footed, answered thus:⁠— <br> “O goddess, be the word thou bring’st obeyed, <br> However fierce my anger; for to him <br> Who hearkens to the gods, the gods give ear.”</p>
<p class="verse">So speaking, on the silver hilt he stayed <br> His strong right hand, and back into its sheath <br> Thrust his good sword, obeying. She, meantime, <br> Returned to heaven, where aegis-bearing Jove <br> Dwells with the other gods. And now again <br> Pelides, with opprobrious words, bespake <br> The son of Atreus, venting thus his wrath:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Wine-bibber, with the forehead of a dog <br> And a deer’s heart! Thou never yet hast dared <br> To arm thyself for battle with the rest, <br> Nor join the other chiefs prepared to lie <br> In ambush⁠—such thy craven fear of death. <br> Better it suits thee, midst the mighty host <br> Of Greeks, to rob some warrior of his prize <br> Who dares withstand thee. King thou art, and yet <br> Devourer of thy people. Thou dost rule <br> A spiritless race, else this day’s insolence, <br> Atrides, were thy last. And now I say, <br> And bind my saying with a mighty oath: <br> By this my sceptre, which can never bear <br> A leaf or twig, since first it left its stem <br> Among the mountains⁠—for the steel has pared <br> Its boughs and bark away, to sprout no more⁠— <br> And now the Achaian judges bear it⁠—they <br> Who guard the laws received from Jupiter, <br> Such is my oath⁠—the time shall come when all <br> The Greeks shall long to see Achilles back, <br> While multitudes are perishing by the hand <br> Of Hector, the man-queller; thou, meanwhile, <br> Though thou lament, shalt have no power to help, <br> And thou shalt rage against thyself to think <br> That thou hast scorned the bravest of the Greeks.</p>
<p class="verse">As thus he spake, Pelides to the ground <br> Flung the gold-studded wand, and took his seat. <br> Fiercely Atrides raged; but now uprose <br> Nestor, the master of persuasive speech, <br> The clear-toned Pylian orator, whose tongue <br> Dropped words more sweet than honey. He had seen <br> Two generations that grew up and lived <br> With him on sacred Pylos pass away, <br> And now he ruled the third. With prudent words <br> He thus addressed the assembly of the chiefs:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Ye gods! What new misfortunes threaten Greece! <br> How Priam would exult and Priam’s sons, <br> And how would all the Trojan race rejoice, <br> Were they to know how furiously ye strive⁠— <br> Ye who in council and in fight surpass <br> The other Greeks. Now hearken to my words⁠— <br> Ye who are younger than myself⁠—for I <br> Have lived with braver men than you, and yet <br> They held me not in light esteem. Such men <br> I never saw, nor shall I see again⁠— <br> Men like Pirithoüs and like Druas, lord <br> Of nations, Caeneus and Exadius, <br> And the great Polypheme, and Theseus, son <br> Of Aegeus, likest to the immortal gods. <br> Strongest of all the earth-born race they fought⁠— <br> The strongest with the strongest of their time⁠— <br> With Centaurs, the wild dwellers of the hills, <br> And fearfully destroyed them. With these men <br> Did I hold converse, coming to their camp <br> From Pylos in a distant land. They sent <br> To bid me join the war, and by their side <br> I fought my best, but no man living now <br> On the wide earth would dare to fight with them. <br> Great as they were, they listened to my words <br> And took my counsel. Hearken also ye, <br> And let my words persuade you for the best. <br> Thou, powerful as thou art, take not from him <br> The maiden; suffer him to keep the prize <br> Decreed him by the sons of Greece; and thou, <br> Pelides, strive no longer with the king, <br> Since never Jove on sceptred prince bestowed <br> Like eminence to his. Though braver thou, <br> And goddess-born, yet hath he greater power <br> And wider sway. Atrides, calm thy wrath⁠— <br> ’Tis I who ask⁠—against the chief who stands <br> The bulwark of the Greeks in this fierce war.”</p>
<p class="verse">To him the sovereign Agamemnon said:⁠— <br> “The things which thou hast uttered, aged chief, <br> Are fitly spoken; but this man would stand <br> Above all others; he aspires to be <br> The master, over all to domineer, <br> And to direct in all things; yet, I think, <br> There may be one who will not suffer this. <br> For if by favor of the immortal gods <br> He was made brave, have they for such a cause <br> Given him the liberty of insolent speech?”</p>
<p class="verse">Hereat the great Achilles, breaking in, <br> Answered: “Yea, well might I deserve the name <br> Of coward and of wretch, should I submit <br> In all things to thy bidding. Such commands <br> Lay thou on others, not on me; nor think <br> I shall obey thee longer. This I say⁠— <br> And bear it well in mind⁠—I shall not lift <br> My hand to keep the maiden whom ye gave <br> And now take from me; but whatever else <br> May be on board that swift black ship of mine, <br> Beware thou carry not away the least <br> Without my leave. Come, make the trial now, <br> That these may see thy black blood bathe my spear.</p>
<p class="verse">Then, rising from that strife of words, the twain <br> Dissolved the assembly at the Grecian fleet. <br> Pelides to his tents and well manned ships <br> Went with Patroclus and his warrior friends, <br> While Agamemnon bade upon the sea <br> Launch a swift barque with twenty chosen men <br> To ply the oar, and put a hecatomb <br> Upon it for the god. He thither led <br> The fair-cheeked maid Chryseis; the command <br> He gave to wise Ulysses; forth they went, <br> Leader and crew, upon their watery path. <br> Meanwhile, he bade the camp be purified; <br> And straight the warriors purified the camp, <br> And, casting the pollutions to the waves, <br> They burned to Phoebus chosen hecatombs <br> Of bulls and goats beside the barren main, <br> From which the savor rose in smoke to heaven.</p>
<p class="verse">So was the host employed. But not the less <br> Did Agamemnon persevere to urge <br> His quarrel with Pelides; and he thus <br> Addressed Talthybius and Eurybates. <br> His heralds and his faithful ministers:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Go ye to where Achilles holds his tent, <br> And take the fair Briseis by the hand, <br> And bring her hither. If he yield her not, <br> I shall come forth to claim her with a band <br> Of warriors, and it shall be worse for him.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake, and sent them forth with added words <br> Of menace. With unwilling steps they went <br> Beside the barren deep, until they reached <br> The tents and vessels of the Myrmidons, <br> And found Achilles seated by his tent <br> And his black ship; their coming pleased him not. <br> They, moved by fear and reverence of the king, <br> Stopped, and bespake him not, nor signified <br> Their errand; he perceived their thought and said:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Hail, heralds, messengers of Jove and men! <br> Draw near; I blame you not. I only blame <br> Atrides, who hath sent you for the maid. <br> Noble Patroclus! Bring the damsel forth, <br> And let them lead her hence. My witnesses <br> Are ye, before the blessed deities, <br> And mortal men, and this remorseless king, <br> If ever he shall need me to avert <br> The doom of utter ruin from his host. <br> Most sure it is, he madly yields himself <br> To fatal counsels, thoughtless of the past <br> And of the future, nor forecasting how <br> The Greeks may fight, unvanquished, by their fleet.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake. Meantime Patroclus had obeyed <br> The word of his beloved friend. He brought <br> The fair-cheeked maid Briseis from the tent, <br> And she was led away. The messengers <br> Returned to where their barques were moored, and she <br> Unwillingly went with them. Then in tears <br> Achilles, from his friends withdrawing, sat <br> Beside the hoary ocean-marge, and gazed <br> On the black deep beyond, and stretched his hands, <br> And prayed to his dear mother, earnestly:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Mother! Since thou didst bring me forth to dwell <br> Brief space on earth, Olympian Jupiter, <br> Who thunders in the highest, should have filled <br> That space with honors, but he grants them not. <br> Wide-ruling Agamemnon takes and holds <br> The prize I won, and thus dishonors me.”</p>
<p class="verse">Thus, shedding tears, he spake. His mother heard, <br> Sitting within the ocean deeps, beside <br> Her aged father. Swiftly from the waves <br> Of the gray deep emerging like a cloud, <br> She sat before him as he wept, and smoothed <br> His brow with her soft hand, and kindly said:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“My child, why weepest thou? What grief is this? <br> Speak, and hide nothing, so that both may know.”</p>
<p class="verse">Achilles, swift of foot, sighed heavily, <br> And said: “Thou know’st already. Why relate <br> These things to thee, who art apprised of all?</p>
<p class="verse">“To Thebé, to Eëtion’s sacred town, <br> We marched, and plundered it, and hither brought <br> The booty, which was fairly shared among <br> The sons of Greece, and Agamemnon took <br> The fair-cheeked maid Chryseis as his prize. <br> But Chryses, priest of Phoebus, to the fleet <br> Of the Achaian warriors, brazen-mailed, <br> Came, to redeem his daughter, offering <br> Ransom uncounted. In his hand he bore <br> The fillets of Apollo, archer-god, <br> Upon the golden sceptre, and he sued <br> To all the Greeks, but chiefly to the sons <br> Of Atreus, the two leaders of the host. <br> Then all the other chiefs, applauding, bade <br> Revere the priest and take the liberal gifts <br> He offered; but the counsel did not please <br> Atrides Agamemnon: he dismissed <br> The priest with scorn, and added threatening words. <br> The aged man indignantly withdrew; <br> And Phoebus⁠—for the priest was dear to him⁠— <br> Granted his prayer and sent among the Greeks <br> A deadly shaft. The people of the camp <br> Were perishing in heaps. His arrows flew <br> Among the Grecian army, far and wide. <br> A seer expert in oracles revealed <br> The will of Phoebus, and I was the first <br> To counsel that the god should be appeased. <br> But Agamemnon rose in sudden wrath, <br> Uttering a threat, which he has since fulfilled. <br> And now the dark-eyed Greeks are taking back <br> His child to Chryses, and with her they bear <br> Gifts to the monarch-god; while to my tent <br> Heralds have come, and borne away the maid <br> Briseis, given me by the sons of Greece. <br> But succor thou thy son, if thou hast power; <br> Ascend to heaven and bring thy prayer to Jove, <br> If e’er by word or act thou gav’st him aid. <br> For I remember, in my father’s halls <br> I often heard thee, glorying, tell how thou, <br> Alone of all the gods, didst interpose <br> To save the cloud-compeller, Saturn’s son, <br> From shameful overthrow, when all the rest <br> Who dwell upon Olympus had conspired <br> To bind him⁠—Juno, Neptune, and with them <br> Pallas Athene. Thou didst come and loose <br> His bonds, and call up to the Olympian heights <br> The hundred-handed, whom the immortal gods <br> Have named Briareus, but the sons of men <br> Aegeon, mightier than his sire in strength; <br> And he, rejoicing in the honor, took <br> His seat by Jove, and all the immortals shrank <br> Aghast before him, and let fall the chains. <br> Remind him of all this, and, sitting down, <br> Embrace his knees, and pray him to befriend <br> The Trojans, that the Greeks, hemmed in and slain <br> Beside their ships and by the shore, may learn <br> To glory in their king, and even he, <br> Wide-ruling Agamemnon, may perceive <br> How grievous was his folly when he dared <br> To treat with scorn the bravest of the Greeks.”</p>
<p class="verse">And Thetis answered, weeping as she spake:⁠— <br> “Alas, my son, why did I rear thee, born <br> To sorrow as thou wert? O would that thou <br> Unwronged, and with no cause for tears, couldst dwell <br> Beside thy ships, since thou must die so soon. <br> I brought thee forth in an unhappy hour, <br> Short-lived and wronged beyond all other men. <br> Yet will I climb the Olympian height among <br> Its snows and make my suit to Jupiter <br> The Thunderer, if haply he may yield <br> To my entreaties. Thou, meanwhile, abide <br> By thy swift ships, incensed against the Greeks, <br> And take no part in all their battles more. <br> But yesterday did Jove depart to hold <br> A banquet far in Ocean’s realm, among <br> The blameless Ethiopians, and with him <br> Went all the train of gods. Twelve days must pass <br> Ere he return to heaven, and I will then <br> Enter his brazen palace, clasp his knees, <br> And hope to move his purpose by my prayers.”</p>
<p class="verse">So saying, she departed, leaving him <br> In anger for the shapely damsel’s sake, <br> Whom forcibly they took away. Meantime <br> Ulysses, with the sacred hecatomb, <br> Arrived at Chrysa. Entering the deep port, <br> They folded up the sails and laid them down <br> In the black ship, and lowering the mast, <br> With all its shrouds, they brought it to its place, <br> Then to the shore they urged the barque with oars, <br> And cast the anchors and secured the prow <br> With fastenings. Next, they disembarked and stood <br> Upon the beach and placed the hecatomb <br> In sight of Phoebus, the great archer. Last, <br> Chryseis left the deck, and, leading her <br> Up to the altar, wise Ulysses gave <br> The maid to her dear father, speaking thus:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“O Chryses! Agamemnon, king of men, <br> Sends me in haste to bring this maid to thee <br> And offer up this hallowed hecatomb <br> To Phoebus, for the Greeks: that so the god, <br> Whose wrath afflicts us sore, may be appeased.</p>
<p class="verse">So speaking, to her father’s hands he gave <br> The maiden; joyfully the priest received <br> The child he loved. Then did the Greeks array <br> The noble hecatomb in order round <br> The sculptured altar, and with washen hands <br> They took the salted meal, while Chryses stood <br> And spread abroad his hands and prayed aloud:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Hear me, thou bearer of the glittering bow, <br> Who guardest Chrysa and the pleasant isle <br> Of Cilia and art lord in Tenedos! <br> Already hast thou listened to my prayer <br> And honored me, and terribly hast scourged <br> The Achaian people. Hear me yet again, <br> And cause the plague that wastes the Greeks to cease.”</p>
<p class="verse">So spake he, supplicating, and to him <br> Phoebus Apollo hearkened. When the prayers <br> Were ended, and the salted meal was flung, <br> Backward they turned the necks of the fat beeves, <br> And cut their throats, and flayed the carcasses, <br> And hewed away the thighs, and covered them <br> With caul in double folds; and over this <br> They laid raw fragments of the other parts. <br> O’er all the aged priest poured dark red wine, <br> And burned them on dry wood. A band of youths <br> With five-pronged spits, beside him, thrust these through <br> The entrails, which they laid among the flames. <br> And when the thighs were all consumed, and next <br> The entrails tasted, all the rest was carved <br> Into small portions and transfixed with spits <br> And roasted with nice care and then withdrawn <br> From the hot coals. This task performed, they made <br> The banquet ready. All became its guests <br> And all were welcome to the equal feast. <br> And when their thirst and hunger were allayed, <br> Boys crowned the ample urns with wreaths, and served <br> The wine to all, and poured libations forth. Meantime the <br> Argive youths, that whole day long, <br> Sang to appease the god; they chanted forth <br> High anthems to the archer of the skies. <br> He listened to the strain, and his stern mood <br> Was softened. When, at length, the sun went down <br> And darkness fell, they gave themselves to sleep <br> Beside the fastenings of their ships, and when <br> Appeared the rosy-fingered Dawn, the child <br> Of Morning, they returned to the great host <br> Of the Achaians. Phoebus deigned to send <br> A favoring breeze; at once they reared the mast <br> And opened the white sails; the canvas swelled <br> Before the wind, and hoarsely round the keel <br> The dark waves murmured as the ship flew on. <br> So ran she, cutting through the sea her way. <br> But when they reached the great Achaian host, <br> They drew their vessel high upon the shore <br> Among the sands, and underneath its sides <br> They laid long beams to prop the keel, and straight <br> Dispersed themselves among the tents and ships.</p>
<p class="verse">The goddess-born Achilles, swift of foot, <br> Beside his ships still brooded o’er his wrath, <br> Nor came to council with the illustrious chiefs, <br> Nor to the war, but suffered idleness <br> To eat his heart away; for well he loved <br> Clamor and combat. But when now, at length, <br> The twelfth day came, the ever-living gods <br> Returned together to the Olympian mount <br> With Jove, their leader. Thetis kept in mind <br> Her son’s desire, and, with the early morn, <br> Emerging from the depths of ocean, climbed <br> To the great heaven and the high mount, and found <br> All-seeing Jove, who, from the rest apart, <br> Was seated on the loftiest pinnacle <br> Of many-peaked Olympus. She sat down <br> Before the son of Saturn, clasped his knees <br> With her left arm, and lifted up her right <br> In supplication to the Sovereign One:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“O Jupiter, my father, if among <br> The immortals I have ever given thee aid <br> By word or act, deny not my request. <br> Honor my son, whose life is doomed to end <br> So soon; for Agamemnon, king of men, <br> Hath done him shameful wrong: he takes from him <br> And keeps the prize he won in war. But thou, <br> Olympian Jupiter, supremely wise, <br> Honor him thou, and give the Trojan host <br> The victory, until the humbled Greeks <br> Heap large increase of honors on my son.”</p>
<p class="verse">She spake, but cloud-compelling Jupiter <br> Answered her not; in silence long he sat. <br> But Thetis, who had clasped his knees at first, <br> Clung to them still, and prayed him yet again:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“O promise me, and grant my suit; or else <br> Deny it⁠—for thou need’st not fear⁠—and I <br> Shall know how far below the other gods <br> Thou holdest me in honor.” As she spake, <br> The Cloud-compeller, sighing heavily, <br> Answered her thus: “Hard things dost thou require, <br> And thou wilt force me into new disputes <br> With Juno, who will anger me again <br> With contumelious words; forever thus, <br> In presence of the immortals, doth she seek <br> Cause of contention, charging that I aid <br> The Trojans in their battles. Now depart, <br> And let her not perceive thee. Leave the rest <br> To be by me accomplished; and that thou <br> Mayst be assured, behold, I give the nod; <br> For this, with me, the immortals know, portends <br> The highest certainty: no word of mine <br> Which once my nod confirms can be revoked, <br> Or prove untrue, or fail to be fulfilled.”</p>
<p class="verse">As thus he spake, the son of Saturn gave <br> The nod with his dark brows. The ambrosial curls <br> Upon the Sovereign One’s immortal head <br> Were shaken, and with them the mighty mount <br> Olympus trembled. Then they parted, she <br> Plunging from bright Olympus to the deep, <br> And Jove returning to his palace home; <br> Where all the gods, uprising from their thrones, <br> At sight of the Great Father, waited not <br> For his approach, but met him as he came.</p>
<p class="verse">And now upon his throne the Godhead took <br> His seat, but Juno knew⁠—for she had seen⁠— <br> That Thetis of the silver feet, and child <br> Of the gray Ancient of the Deep, had held <br> Close council with her consort. Therefore she <br> Bespake the son of Saturn harshly, thus:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“O crafty one, with whom, among the gods, <br> Plottest thou now? Thus hath it ever been <br> Thy pleasure to devise, apart from me, <br> Thy plans in secret; never willingly <br> Dost thou reveal to me thy purposes.”</p>
<p class="verse">Then thus replied the Father of the gods <br> And mortals: “Juno, do not think to know <br> All my designs, for thou wilt find the task <br> Too hard for thee, although thou be my spouse. <br> What fitting is to be revealed, no one <br> Of all the immortals or of men shall know <br> Sooner than thou; but when I form designs <br> Apart from all the gods, presume thou not <br> To question me or pry into my plans.”</p>
<p class="verse">Juno, the large-eyed and august, rejoined:⁠— <br> “What words, stern son of Saturn, hast thou said! <br> It never was my wont to question thee <br> Or pry into thy plans, and thou art left <br> To form them as thou wilt; yet now I fear <br> The silver-footed Thetis has contrived⁠— <br> That daughter of the Ancient of the Deep⁠— <br> To o’erpersuade thee, for, at early prime, <br> She sat before thee and embraced thy knees; <br> And thou hast promised her, I cannot doubt, <br> To give Achilles honor and to cause <br> Myriads of Greeks to perish by their fleet.”</p>
<p class="verse">Then Jove, the cloud-compeller, spake again:⁠— <br> “Harsh-tongued! thou ever dost suspect me thus, <br> Nor can I act unwatched; and yet all this <br> Profits thee nothing, for it only serves <br> To breed dislike, and is the worse for thee. <br> But were it as thou deemest, ’tis enough <br> That such has been my pleasure. Sit thou down <br> In silence, and obey, lest all the gods <br> Upon Olympus, when I come and lay <br> These potent hands on thee, protect thee not.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake, and Juno, large-eyed and august, <br> O’erawed, and curbing her high spirit, sat <br> In silence; meanwhile all the gods of heaven <br> Within the halls of Jove were inly grieved. <br> But Vulcan, the renowned artificer, <br> Sought to console his mother in her grief⁠— <br> The white-armed Juno⁠—and thus interposed:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Great will the evil be and hard to bear, <br> If, for the sake of mortals, ye are moved <br> To such contention and the assembled gods <br> Disturbed with discord. Even the pleasant feast <br> Will lose its flavor when embittered thus. <br> And let me warn my mother while I speak, <br> Wise as she is, that she defer to Jove, <br> Lest the All-Father angrily again <br> Reply, and spoil the banquet of the day. <br> The Thunderer of Olympus, if he choose <br> To make a wreck of all things, wields a power <br> Far greater than we all. Accost him thou <br> With gentle speeches, and the Lord of heaven <br> Will then regard us in a kindly mood.”</p>
<p class="verse">As thus he spake, he gave into the hands <br> Of his beloved mother the round cup <br> Of double form, and thus he spake again:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Mother, be patient and submit, although <br> In sadness, lest these eyes behold thee yet <br> Beaten with stripes, and though I hold thee dear <br> And grieve for thee, I cannot bring thee help; <br> For hard it is to strive with Jupiter. <br> Already once, when I took part with thee, <br> He seized me by the foot and flung me o’er <br> The battlements of heaven. All day I fell, <br> And with the setting sun I struck the earth <br> In Lemnos. Little life was left in me, <br> What time the Sintians took me from the ground.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake, and Juno, the white-shouldered, smiled, <br> And smiling took the cup her son had brought; <br> And next he poured to all the other gods <br> Sweet nectar from the jar, beginning first <br> With those at the right hand. As they beheld <br> Lame Vulcan laboring o’er the palace-floor, <br> An inextinguishable laughter broke <br> From all the blessed gods. So feasted they <br> All day till sunset. From that equal feast <br> None stood aloof, nor from the pleasant sound <br> Of harp, which Phoebus touched, nor from the voice <br> Of Muses singing sweetly in their turn.</p>
<p class="verse">But when the sun’s all-glorious light was down, <br> Each to his sleeping-place betook himself; <br> For Vulcan, the lame god, with marvellous art, <br> Had framed for each the chamber of his rest.</p>
<p class="verse">And Jupiter, the Olympian Thunderer, <br> Went also to his couch, where ’twas his wont, <br> When slumber overtook him, to recline. <br> And there, beside him, slept the white-armed queen <br> Juno, the mistress of the golden throne.</p>
