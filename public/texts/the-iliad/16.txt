<p class="lede">Patroclus permitted by Achilles to take part in the war, on condition that he will return after repulsing the Trojans from the fleet⁠—His preparations for the battle, putting on the armor of Achilles, and summoning the Myrmidons to follow him⁠—Alarm of the Trojans on seeing him, supposing him to be Achilles⁠—His exploits⁠—The Trojans driven back from the fleet⁠—Death of Sarpedon⁠—The Trojans pursued by Patroclus, contrary to the command of Achilles, to the walls of Troy⁠—Patroclus disarmed by Apollo, wounded by Euphorbus, and slain by Hector.</p>
<p class="verse">Such was the struggle for that gallant barque. <br> Meanwhile Patroclus stood beside his friend <br> The shepherd of the people, Peleus’ son, <br> And shed hot tears, as when a fountain sheds <br> Dark waters streaming down a precipice. <br> The great Achilles, swift of foot, beheld <br> And pitied him, and spake these wingèd words:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Why weepest thou, Patroclus, like a girl⁠— <br> A little girl that by her mother’s side <br> Runs, importuning to be taken up, <br> And plucks her by the robe, and stops her way, <br> And looks at her, and cries, until at last <br> She rests within her arms? Thou art like her, <br> Patroclus, with thy tears. Dost thou then bring <br> Sad tidings to the Myrmidons or me? <br> Or hast thou news from Phthia? It is said <br> That still Menoetius, son of Actor, lives, <br> And Peleus also, son of Aeacus, <br> Among the Myrmidons. Full bitterly <br> Should we lament to hear that either died. <br> Or mournest thou because the Achaians fall <br> Through their own folly by the roomy ships? <br> Speak, and hide nothing, for I too would know.”</p>
<p class="verse">And thou, O knight Patroclus, with a sigh <br> Deep drawn, didst answer thus: “Be not displeased, <br> Achilles, son of Peleus, bravest far <br> Of all the Achaian army! For the Greeks <br> Endure a bitter lot. The chiefs who late <br> Were deemed their mightiest are within the ships, <br> Wounded or stricken down. There Diomed, <br> The gallant son of Tydeus, lies, and there <br> Ulysses, the great spearman, wounded both; <br> And Agamemnon; and Eurypylus, <br> Driven from the field, an arrow in his thigh. <br> Round them the healers, skilled in remedies, <br> Attend and dress their painful wounds, while thou, <br> Achilles, sittest here implacable. <br> O, never be such fierce resentments mine <br> As thou dost cherish, who art only brave <br> For mischief! Whom wilt thou hereafter aid, <br> If now thou rescue not the perishing Greeks? <br> O merciless! It cannot surely be <br> That Peleus was thy father, or the queen <br> Thetis thy mother; the green sea instead <br> And rugged precipices brought thee forth, <br> For savage is thy heart. But if thou heed <br> The warning of some god, if thou hast heard <br> Aught which thy goddess-mother has received <br> From Jove, send me at least into the war, <br> And let me lead thy Myrmidons, that thus <br> The Greeks may have some gleam of hope. And give <br> The armor from thy shoulders. I will wear <br> Thy mail, and then the Trojans, at the sight, <br> May think I am Achilles, and may pause <br> From fighting, and the warlike sons of Greece, <br> Tired as they are, may breathe once more, and gain <br> A respite from the conflict. Our fresh troops <br> May easily drive back upon their town <br> The weary Trojans from our tents and fleet.”</p>
<p class="verse">So spake he, sighing; rash and blind, he asked <br> Death for himself and evil destiny. <br> Achilles the swift-footed also drew <br> A heavy sigh, and thus in turn he spake:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“What, O divine Patroclus, hast thou said? <br> I fear no omen yet revealed to me; <br> Nor has my goddess-mother told me aught <br> From Jove; but ever in my heart and soul <br> Rankles the painful sense of injury done <br> By one who, having greater power, deprives <br> An equal of his right, and takes away <br> The prize he won. This is my wrong, and this <br> The cause of all my bitterness of heart. <br> Her whom the sons of Greece bestowed on me <br> As my reward, a trophy of my spear, <br> After the sack of a fenced city⁠—her <br> Did Agamemnon, son of Atreus, take <br> Out of my hands, as if I were a wretch, <br> A worthless outcast. But let that affront <br> Be with the things that were. It is not well <br> To bear a grudge forever. I have said <br> My anger should not cease to burn until <br> The clamor of the battle and the assault <br> Should reach the fleet. But go thou and put on <br> My well-known armor; lead into the field <br> My Myrmidons, men that rejoice in war, <br> Since like a lowering cloud the men of Troy <br> Surround the fleet, and the Achaians stand <br> In narrow space close pressed beside the sea, <br> And all the city of Ilium flings itself <br> Against them, confident of victory, <br> Now that the glitter of my helm no more <br> Flashes upon their eyes. Yet very soon <br> Their flying host would fill the trenches here <br> With corpses, had but Agamemnon dealt <br> Gently with me; and now their squadrons close <br> Around our army. Now no more the spear <br> Is wielded by Tydides Diomed <br> In rescue of the Greeks; no more the shout <br> Of Agamemnon’s hated throat is heard; <br> But the man-queller Hector, lifting up <br> His voice, exhorts the Trojans, who, in throngs, <br> Raising the war-cry, fill the plain, and drive <br> The Greeks before them. Gallantly lead on <br> The charge, Patroclus; rescue our good ships; <br> Let not the enemy give them to the flames, <br> And cut us off from our desired return. <br> Follow my counsel; bear my words in mind; <br> So shalt thou win for me among the Greeks <br> Great honor and renown, and they shall bring <br> The beautiful maiden back with princely gifts. <br> When thou hast driven the assailants from the fleet, <br> Return thou hither. If the Thunderer, <br> Husband of Juno, suffer thee to gain <br> That victory, seek no further to prolong <br> The combat with the warlike sons of Troy, <br> Apart from me, lest I be brought to shame, <br> Nor, glorying in the battle and pursuit, <br> Slaying the Trojans as thou goest, lead <br> Thy men to Troy, lest from the Olympian mount <br> One of the ever-living gods descend <br> Against thee: Phoebus loves the Trojans well. <br> But come as soon as thou shalt see the ships <br> In safety; leave the foes upon the plain <br> Contending with each other. Would to Jove <br> The All-Father, and to Pallas, and the god <br> Who bears the bow, Apollo, that of all <br> The Trojans, many as they are, and all <br> The Greeks, not one might be reprieved from death, <br> While thou and I alone were left alive <br> To overthrow the sacred walls of Troy.”</p>
<p class="verse">So talked they with each other. Ajax, whelmed <br> Beneath a storm of darts, meantime but ill <br> Endured the struggle, for the will of Jove <br> And the fierce foe prevailed. His shining helm <br> Rang fearfully, as on his temples fell, <br> Stroke following after stroke, the weapons hurled <br> Against its polished studs. The buckler borne <br> Firmly on his left arm, and shifted oft <br> From side to side, had wearied it, and yet <br> The Trojans, pressing round him, could not drive, <br> With all their darts, the hero from his place. <br> Heavily heaved his panting chest; his limbs <br> Streamed with warm sweat; there was no breathing time; <br> On danger danger followed, toil on toil.</p>
<p class="verse">Now, Muses, dwellers of Olympus, tell <br> How first the galleys of the Greeks were fired.</p>
<p class="verse">Hector drew near, and smote with his huge sword <br> The ashen spear of Ajax just below <br> The socket of the blade, and cut the stem <br> In two. The son of Telamon in vain <br> Brandished the severed weapon, while afar <br> The brazen blade flew off, and ringing fell <br> To earth. Then Ajax in his mighty mind <br> Acknowledged that the gods were in the war, <br> And shuddered, knowing that the Thunderer <br> Was thwarting all his warlike purposes, <br> And willed the victory to Troy. The chief <br> Withdrew beyond the reach of spears, while fast <br> The eager enemy hurled the blazing brands <br> At the swift ship, and wrapped the stern in flames <br> Unquenchable. Achilles saw, and smote <br> His thigh, and spake: “Patroclus, noble friend <br> And knight, make haste: already I behold <br> The flames that rage with fury at the fleet. <br> Now, lest the enemy seize our ships and we <br> Be barred of our return, put quickly on <br> Thy armor; be my task to call the troops.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake: Patroclus then in glittering brass <br> Arrayed himself; and first around his thighs <br> He put the beautiful greaves, and fastened them <br> With silver clasps; around his chest he bound <br> The breastplate of the swift Aeacides, <br> With star-like points, and richly chased; he hung <br> The sword with silver studs and blade of brass <br> Upon his shoulders, and with it the shield <br> Solid and vast; upon his gallant head <br> He placed the glorious helm with horse-hair plume, <br> That grandly waved on high. Two massive spears <br> He took, that fitted well his grasp, but left <br> The spear which great Achilles only bore, <br> Heavy and huge and strong, and which no arm <br> Among the Greeks save his could poise; his strength <br> Alone sufficed to wield it. ’Twas an ash <br> Which Chiron felled in Pelion’s top, and gave <br> To Peleus, that it yet might be the death <br> Of heroes. Then he called, to yoke with speed <br> The steeds, Automedon, whom he esteemed <br> Next to Achilles, that great scatterer <br> Of armies; for he found him ever firm <br> In battle, breasting faithfully its shock. <br> Automedon led forth to take the yoke <br> Xanthus and Balius, coursers that in speed <br> Were like the wind. Podargè brought them forth <br> To Zephyrus, while she, the Harpy, grazed <br> By ocean’s streams. Upon the outer side <br> He joined to them the noble Pedasus, <br> Brought by Achilles from the captured town <br> Where ruled Eëtion. Though of mortal stock, <br> Well might he match with those immortal steeds.</p>
<p class="verse">Meanwhile Achilles armed the Myrmidons, <br> Passing from tent to tent. Like ravening wolves, <br> Terribly strong, that, having slain among <br> The hills an antlered stag of mighty size, <br> Tear and devour it, while their jaws are stained <br> With its red blood, then gather in a herd <br> About some darkly flowing stream, and lap <br> The sullen water with their slender tongues, <br> And drop the clots of blood from their grim mouths <br> And, although gorged, are fierce and fearless still⁠— <br> So came the leaders of the Myrmidons, <br> In rushing crowds, about the valiant friend <br> Of swift Aeacides. Among them stood <br> Achilles, great in war, encouraging <br> The charioteers and warriors armed with shields.</p>
<p class="verse">Achilles, dear to Jupiter, had led <br> Fifty swift barques to Ilium, and in each <br> Were fifty men, companions at the oar. <br> O’er these he gave command to five; himself, <br> Supreme in power, was ruler over all. <br> One band the nobly armed Menestheus led, <br> Son of Spercheius. To that river-god, <br> Beautiful Polydora brought him forth, <br> Daughter of Peleus; she, a mortal maid, <br> Met an immortal’s love. Yet Borus, son <br> Of Periëres, owned the boy and took <br> The mother for his bride, with princely dower <br> Eudorus led the second band, a youth <br> Of warlike mould, whom Polymela bore, <br> Daughter of Phylas, graceful in the dance. <br> In secrecy she brought him forth, for once <br> The mighty Argus-queller saw the maid <br> Among the choir of those who danced and sang <br> At Dian’s festival, the huntress-queen, <br> Who bears the golden shafts; he saw and loved <br> And, climbing to her chamber, met by stealth <br> The damsel, and she bore a gallant son, <br> Eudorus, swift of foot and brave in war. <br> When Ilithyia, midwife goddess, gave <br> The boy to see the pleasant light of day, <br> The stout Echecleus, son of Actor, brought <br> The mother to his house, with liberal dower. <br> The aged Phylas reared the child she left <br> Tenderly as a son, and loved him well. <br> Pisander, warlike son of Msemalus, <br> Commanded the third squadron; none like him <br> Among the Myrmidons could wield the spear <br> Except Pelides. Phoenix, aged knight, <br> Led the fourth squadron. With the fifth and last <br> There came Alcimedon, Laerceus’ son, <br> As leader. When their ranks were duly formed, <br> Achilles spake to them in earnest words:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Now, Myrmidons, forget no single word <br> Of all the threats ye uttered against Troy <br> Since first my wrath began. Ye blame me much, <br> And say: ‘Hard-hearted son of Peleus, sure <br> Thy mother must have suckled thee on gall; <br> For sternly thou dost keep us in the ships, <br> Unwilling as we are. We might, at least, <br> Crossing the sea, return in our good ships, <br> If thus thine anger is to last.’ These words <br> Ye utter oft when our assemblies meet, <br> And now the great occasion is at hand <br> Which ye have longed for; now let him whose heart <br> Is fearless meet the Trojans valiantly.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake, and roused their courage and their might, <br> And as they heard their king they brought their rank <br> To closer order. As an architect <br> Builds up, with closely fitting stones, the wall <br> Of some tall mansion, proof against the blast, <br> So close were now the helms and bossy shields, <br> Shield leaned on shield, and helm on helm, and man <br> On man, and on the glittering helmet-cones <br> The horse-hair plumes with every motion touched <br> Each other, so compact the squadrons stood. <br> Two heroes, nobly armed, were at their head, to <br> Patroclus and Automedon, and both <br> Had but one thought⁠—to combat in the van.</p>
<p class="verse">Entering his tent, Achilles raised the lid <br> Of a fair coffer, beautifully wrought, <br> Which silver-footed Thetis placed on board <br> His barque, and filled with tunics, cloaks well lined, <br> And fleecy carpets. There he also kept <br> A goblet richly chased, from which no lip <br> Of man, save his, might drink the dark red wine, <br> Nor wine be poured to any god save Jove, <br> The mighty Father. This he took in hand <br> And purified with sulphur first, and then <br> Rinsed with clear water. Next, with washen hands, <br> He drew the dark red wine, and stood without, <br> In the open space, and, pouring out the wine, <br> Prayed with his eyes turned heavenward, not unheard <br> By Jupiter, who wields the thunderbolt.</p>
<p class="verse">“Dodonian Jove, Pelasgian, sovereign King, <br> Whose dwelling is afar, and who dost rule <br> Dodona winter-bound, where dwell thy priests, <br> The Selli, with unwashen feet, who sleep <br> Upon the ground! Thou once hast heard my prayer, <br> And thou hast honored me, and terribly <br> Avenged me on the Greeks. Accomplish yet <br> This one request of mine. I shall remain <br> Among the rows of ships, but in my stead <br> I send my comrade, who will lead to war <br> My vast array of Myrmidons. With him, <br> O God of Thunders, send the victory. <br> Make his heart bold; let even Hector learn <br> Whether my follower, though alone, can wage <br> Successful war, or conquer only then <br> When I go forth with him into the field <br> Of slaughter. When he shall have beaten back <br> The assailants from the fleet, let him return <br> Unharmed to my good galleys and to me. <br> With all his arms and all his valiant men.”</p>
<p class="verse">So spake he, offering prayer, and Jupiter, <br> The Great Disposer, hearkened. Half the prayer <br> The All-Father granted him, and half denied: <br> To drive the storm of battle from the fleet <br> He granted, but denied his friend’s return <br> In safety. When the warrior thus had prayed, <br> And poured the wine to Father Jove, he went <br> Into his tent again, and there replaced <br> The goblet in the coffer. Coming forth, <br> He stood before the entrance to behold <br> The terrible encounter of the hosts.</p>
<p class="verse">The newly armed, led by their gallant chief, <br> Patroclus, marched in warlike order forth, <br> And in high hope, to fall upon the foe. <br> As wasps, that by the wayside build their cells, <br> Angered from time to time by thoughtless boys⁠— <br> Whence mischief comes to many⁠—if by chance <br> Some passing traveller should unwittingly <br> Disturb them, all at once are on the wing, <br> And all attack him, to defend their young <br> So fearless and so fierce the Myrmidons <br> Poured from their fleet, and mighty was the din. <br> Patroclus with loud voice exhorted them:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“O Myrmidons, companions of the son <br> Of Peleus, bear in mind, my friends, your fame <br> For valor, and be men, that we who serve <br> Achilles, we who combat hand to hand, <br> May honor him by our exploits, and teach <br> Wide-ruling Agamemnon how he erred <br> Slighting the bravest warrior of the Greeks.”</p>
<p class="verse">These words awoke the courage and the might <br> Of all who heard them, and in close array <br> They fell upon the Trojans. Fearfully <br> The fleet around them echoed to the sound <br> Of Argives shouting. When the Trojans saw, <br> In glittering arms, Menoetius’ gallant son <br> And his attendant, every heart grew faint <br> With fear; the close ranks wavered; for they thought <br> That the swift son of Peleus at the fleet <br> Had laid aside his wrath, and was again <br> The friend of Agamemnon. Eagerly <br> They looked around for an escape from death.</p>
<p class="verse">Then first Patroclus cast his shining displeasure <br> Into the crowd before him, where they fought <br> Most fiercely round the stern of the good ship <br> Of brave Protesilaüs. There it smote <br> Pyraechmes, who had led from Amydon, <br> On the broad Axius, his Paeonian knights. <br> Through his right shoulder went the blade; he fell, <br> Heavily groaning, to the earth. His band <br> Of warriors from Paeonia, panic-struck, <br> Fled from Patroclus as they saw their chief <br> Cut off, their bravest in the battle-field. <br> So from the ship he drave the foe, and quenched <br> The blazing fire. There lay the half-burnt barque, <br> While with a mighty uproar fled the host <br> Of Troy, and from between the beaked ships <br> Poured after them with tumult infinite <br> The Greeks. As when from some high mountaintop <br> The God of Lightnings, Jupiter, sweeps off <br> The overshadowing cloud, at once appear <br> The watch-lowers and the headland heights and lawns <br> All in full light, and all the unmeasured depth <br> Of ether opens, so the Greeks, when thus <br> Their fleet was rescued from the hostile flame, <br> Breathed for a space; and yet they might not cease <br> From battle, for not everywhere alike <br> Were chased the Trojans from the dark-hulled ships <br> Before the Greeks, but struggled still to keep <br> The mastery, and yielded but to force.</p>
<p class="verse">Then in that scattered conflict of the chiefs <br> Each Argive slew a warrior. With his spear <br> The brave son of Menoetius made a thrust <br> At Areilochus, and pierced his thigh, <br> Just as he turned away, and through the part <br> Forced the keen weapon, splintering as it went <br> The bone, and brought the Trojan to the ground; <br> And warlike Menelaus pierced the breast <br> Of Thoas where the buckler left it bare, <br> And took his life. The son of Phyleus saw <br> Amphiclus rushing on, and with his spear <br> Met him and pierced his leg below the knee, <br> Where brawniest is the limb. The blade cut through <br> The sinews, and his eyes were closed in night. <br> There fought the sons of Nestor. One of these, <br> Antilochus, transfixed with his good spear <br> Atymnius through the flank, and brought him down <br> At his own feet. With sorrow Maris saw <br> His brother fall, and toward Antilochus <br> Flew to defend the corpse; but ere he strook, <br> The godlike Thrasymedes, with a blow <br> That missed not, smote his shoulder, tearing off <br> With the spear’s blade upon the upper arm <br> The muscles from the bone. With ringing arms <br> He fell, and darkness gathered o’er his eyes. <br> Thus were two brothers by two brothers slain, <br> And sent to Erebus; two valiant friends <br> Were they of King Sarpedon, and the sons <br> Of Amisodarus, who reared and fed <br> Chimera, the destroyer of mankind.</p>
<p class="verse">Oilean Ajax, springing forward, seized <br> On Cleobulus, for the struggling crowd <br> Hindered his flight. He took the Trojan’s life, <br> Smiting the neck with his huge-handled sword; <br> The blade grew warm with blood, and cruel fate <br> Brought darkness o’er the dying warrior’s eyes. <br> Peneleus fought with Lycon; each had cast <br> His spear and missed his aim, and now with swords <br> The twain encountered. Lycon dealt a stroke <br> Upon the crested helmet of his foe, <br> And the blade failed him, breaking at the hilt. <br> Meantime Peneleus smote beneath the ear <br> The neck of Lycon: deep the weapon went; <br> The severed head, held only by the skin, <br> Dropped to one side, and life forsook the limbs. <br> Meriones, o’ertaking Acamas, <br> In rapid flight, discharged a mighty blow <br> On his left shoulder as he climbed his car; <br> He fell, and darkness gathered o’er his eyes. <br> Then plunged Idomeneus the cruel spear <br> Into the mouth of Erymas. The blade <br> Passed on beneath the brain, and pierced the neck, <br> And there divided the white bones. It dashed <br> The teeth out; both the eyes were filled with blood, <br> Which gushed from mouth and nostrils as he breathed; <br> And the black cloud of death came over him. <br> Thus every Grecian leader slew his man.</p>
<p class="verse">As ravening wolves that spring on lambs and kids, <br> And seize them, wandering wide among the hills <br> Beyond the keeper’s care, and bear them off, <br> And rend with cruel fangs their helpless prey, <br> So fiercely did the Achaians fling themselves <br> Upon the men of Troy, who only thought <br> Of flight from that tumultuous strife, and quite <br> Forgot their wonted valor. All the while <br> The greater Ajax sought to hurl his spear <br> At Hector, clad in brazen mail, who yet, <br> Expert in battle, kept his ample chest <br> Hid by his bull’s-hide shield, and, though he heard <br> The hiss of darts and clash of spears, and saw <br> The fortune of the field deserting him, <br> Lingered to rescue his beloved friends.</p>
<p class="verse">As from the summit of Olympus spreads <br> A cloud into the sky that late was clear, <br> When Jove brings on the tempest, with such speed <br> In clamorous flight the Trojans left the fleet, <br> Yet passed they not the trench in seemly plight. <br> The rapid steeds of Hector bore him safe <br> Across with all his arms, while, left between <br> The high banks of the trench, the Trojan host <br> Struggled despairingly. The fiery steeds, <br> Harnessed to many a chariot, left it there <br> With broken pole. Patroclus followed close, <br> With mighty voice encouraging the Greeks, <br> And meditating vengeance on the foe, <br> That noisily ran on, and right and left <br> Were scattered, filling all the ways. The dust <br> Rose thick and high, and spread, and reached the clouds, <br> As with swift feet the Trojan coursers held <br> Their way to Ilium from the tents and ships. <br> Patroclus where he saw the wildest rout <br> Drave thither, shouting threats. Full many a chief <br> Fell under his own axle from his car, <br> And chariots with a crash were overthrown. <br> The swift, immortal horses which the gods <br> Bestowed on Peleus leaped the trench at once, <br> Eager to reach the plain. As eagerly <br> Patroclus longed to overtake and smite <br> Hector, whose steeds were hurrying him away.</p>
<p class="verse">As when, in autumn time, the dark-brown earth <br> Is whelmed with water from the stormy clouds, <br> When Jupiter pours down his heaviest rains, <br> Offended at men’s crimes who override <br> The laws by violence, and drive justice forth <br> From the tribunals, heedless of the gods <br> And their displeasure⁠—all the running streams <br> Are swelled to floods⁠—the furious torrents tear <br> The mountain slopes, and, plunging from the heights <br> With mighty roar, lay waste the works of men, <br> And fling themselves into the dark-blue sea⁠— <br> Thus with loud tumult fled the Trojan horse.</p>
<p class="verse">Patroclus, having cut the nearest bands <br> Of Troy in pieces, made his warriors turn <br> Back to the fleet, and, eager as they were, <br> Stopped the pursuit that led them toward the town. <br> Then, in the area bounded by the sea, <br> River, and lofty wall, he chased and smote <br> And took full vengeance. With his glittering spear <br> He wounded Pronoüs where the buckler left <br> The breast exposed; the Trojan with a clash <br> Fell to the earth, and life forsook his limbs. <br> Advancing in his might, Patroclus smote <br> Thestor, the son of Enops, as he sat <br> Cowering upon his sumptuous seat, o’ercome <br> With fear, and dropped the reins. Through his right cheek <br> Among the teeth Patroclus thrust his spear, <br> And o’er the chariot’s border drew him forth <br> With the spear’s stem. As when an angler sits <br> Upon a jutting rock, and from the sea <br> Draws a huge fish with line and gleaming hook, <br> So did Patroclus, with his shining spear, <br> Draw forth the panting Trojan from his car, <br> And shook him clear: he fell to earth and died.</p>
<p class="verse">As Eryalus then came swiftly on, <br> Patroclus flung a stone, and on the brow <br> Smote him; the Trojan’s head, beneath the blow, <br> Parted in two within the helm; he fell <br> Headlong to earth, a prey to ghastly death. <br> Then slew he Erymas, Amphoterus, <br> Epaltes, Pyris, Ipheus, Echius, <br> Tlepolemus, Damastor’s son, and next <br> Euippus; nor was Polymelus spared, <br> The son of Argias⁠—smitten all, and thrown, <br> Slain upon slain, along their mother earth.</p>
<p class="verse">And now Sarpedon, as he saw his friends, <br> The unbelted Lycians, falling by the hand <br> Of Menoetiades, exhorted thus <br> The gallant Lycians: “Shame upon you all, <br> My Lycians! Whither do you flee? Be bold! <br> For I myself will meet this man, and learn <br> Who walks the field in triumph thus, and makes <br> Such havoc in our squadrons; for his hand <br> Has laid full many a gallant warrior low.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake, and from his car with all his arms <br> Sprang to the ground, while on the other side <br> Patroclus, as he saw him come, leaped down <br> And left his chariot. As on some tall rock <br> Two vultures, with curved talons and hooked beaks, <br> Fight screaming, so these two with furious cries <br> Advanced against each other. When the son <br> Of crafty Saturn saw them meet, his heart <br> Was touched with pity, and he thus bespake <br> His spouse and sister Juno: “Woe is me! <br> Sarpedon, most beloved of men, is doomed <br> To die, o’ercome by Menoetiades. <br> And now I halt between two purposes⁠— <br> Whether to bear him from this fatal fight, <br> Alive and safe, to Lycia’s fertile fields, <br> Or let him perish by his enemy’s hand.”</p>
<p class="verse">Imperial, large-eyed Juno answered thus:⁠— <br> “What words, dread son of Saturn, hast thou said! <br> Wouldst thou deliver from the common lot <br> Of death a mortal doomed long since by fate? <br> Do as thou wilt, but be thou sure of this⁠— <br> The other gods will not approve. And bear <br> In mind these words of mine. If thou shouldst send <br> Sarpedon home to Lycia safe, reflect <br> Some other god may claim the right, like thee, <br> To rescue his beloved son from death <br> In battle; for we know that in the war <br> Round Priam’s noble city are many sons <br> Of gods, who will with vehement anger see <br> Thy interposing hand. Yet if he be <br> So dear to thee, and thou dost pity him, <br> Let him in mortal combat be o’ercome <br> By Menoetiades, and when the breath <br> Of life has left his frame, give thou command <br> To Death and gentle Sleep to bear him hence <br> To the broad realm of Lycia. There his friends <br> And brethren shall perform the funeral rites; <br> There shall they build him up a tomb, and rear <br> A column⁠—honors that become the dead.”</p>
<p class="verse">She ceased, nor did the All-Father disregard <br> Her words. He caused a bloody dew to fall <br> Upon the earth in sorrow for the son <br> Whom well he loved, and whom Patroclus soon <br> Should slay upon the fertile plain of Troy, <br> Far from the pleasant land that saw his birth.</p>
<p class="verse">The warriors now drew near. Patroclus slew <br> The noble Thrasymelus, who had been <br> Sarpedon’s valiant comrade in the war. <br> Below the belt he smote him, and he fell <br> Lifeless. Sarpedon threw his shining lance; <br> It missed, but struck the courser Pedasus <br> In the right shoulder. With a groan he fell <br> In dust, and, moaning, breathed his life away. <br> Then the two living horses sprang apart, <br> And the yoke creaked, and the entangled reins <br> Were useless, fastened to the fallen horse. <br> Automedon, the mighty spearman, saw <br> The remedy, and from his brawny thigh <br> He drew his sword, and cut the outside horse <br> Loose from his fellows. They again were brought <br> Together, and obeyed the reins once more; <br> And the two chiefs renewed the mortal fight.</p>
<p class="verse">And now, again, Sarpedon’s shining spear <br> Was vainly flung; the point, in passing o’er <br> Patroclus’s left shoulder, gave no wound. <br> In turn, Patroclus, hurling not in vain <br> His weapon, smote him where the midriff’s web <br> Holds the tough heart. He fell as falls an oak <br> Or poplar or tall pine, which workmen hew <br> Among the mountains with their sharpened steel <br> To frame a ship. So he before his steeds <br> And chariot fell upon the bloody dust, <br> And grasped it with his hands, and gnashed his teeth. <br> As when a lion coming on a herd <br> Seizes, amid the crowd of stamping beeves, <br> A tawny and high-mettled bull, that dies <br> Bellowing in fury in the lion’s jaws⁠— <br> Like him, indignant to be overcome, <br> The leader of the bucklered Lycian host, <br> Laid prostrate by Patroclus, called by name <br> His dear companion, and addressed him thus:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Beloved Glaucus, mighty among men! <br> Now prove thyself a hero, now be bold. <br> Now, if thou have a warrior’s spirit, think <br> Of nought but battle. Go from rank to rank, <br> Exhorting all the Lycian chiefs to fight <br> Around Sarpedon. Combat thou for me <br> With thy good spear, for I shall be to thee <br> A shame and a reproach through all thy days, <br> If here the Greeks, beside whose ships I fall, <br> Bear off my armor. Stand thou firm, and stir <br> Thy people up to combat valiantly.”</p>
<p class="verse">While he was speaking, death crept o’er his sight <br> And stopped his breath. Patroclus set his heel <br> Against his bosom, and plucked out the spear; <br> The midriff followed it, and thus he drew <br> The life and weapon forth at once. Meantime <br> The Myrmidons held fast the snorting steeds, <br> That, loosened from the Lycian’s car, were bent <br> On flight. The grief of Glaucus as he heard <br> His comrade’s voice was bitter, and his heart <br> Ached at the thought that he could bring no aid. <br> He seized his arm and pressed it in his grasp, <br> For there the wound which Teucer’s arrow left, <br> When Glaucus stormed the wall and Teucer’s shafts <br> Defended it, still pained him grievously, <br> And thus he prayed to Phoebus, archer-god:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Give ear, O king! wherever thou abide, <br> In the opulent realm of Lycia, or in Troy; <br> For everywhere thou nearest those who cry <br> To thee in sorrow, and great sorrow now <br> Is on me. Grievous is the wound I bear; <br> Sharp are the pains that pierce my hand; the blood <br> Cannot be stanched; my very arm becomes <br> A burden; I can wield the spear no more <br> With a firm grasp, nor combat with the foe. <br> A mighty chief⁠—Sarpedon, son of Jove⁠— <br> Has perished, and the father came not nigh <br> To aid his son. Yet come thou to my aid, <br> O monarch-god! and heal this painful wound, <br> And give me strength to rally to the fight <br> The Lycian warriors, and myself contend <br> Valiantly for the rescue of the dead.”</p>
<p class="verse">So prayed he: Phoebus hearkened, and at once <br> Assuaged the pain, and stanched the purple blood <br> In the deep wound, and filled his frame with strength. <br> The warrior felt the change, rejoiced to know <br> That with such friendly speed the mighty god <br> Granted his prayer. And first he went among <br> The Lycian chiefs, exhorting them to wage <br> Fierce battle for Sarpedon. Then he sought, <br> Walking with rapid strides, the Trojan chiefs, <br> Agenor, nobly born, Polydamas, <br> The son of Panthoüs, Aeneas next, <br> And Hector mailed in brass. By him he stood, <br> And thus accosted him with wingèd words:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“O Hector, thou art careless of the fate <br> Of thine allies, who for thy sake, afar <br> From those they love, and from their native land, <br> Pour out their lives; thou bringest them no aid. <br> Sarpedon lies in death, the chief who led <br> The bucklered Lycians, who with justice swayed <br> The realm of Lycia, and defended it <br> With valor. Him hath brazen Mars beneath <br> The weapon of Patroclus smitten down. <br> Come then, my friends, repulse we gallantly <br> These Myrmidons; else will they bear away <br> His armor and insult his corpse, to avenge <br> The havoc we have made among the Greeks <br> Who perished by our weapons at the fleet.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake, and grief immitigable seized <br> The Trojans; for the slain, though stranger-born, <br> Had been a pillar of the realm of Troy, <br> And many were the troops that followed him, <br> And he was bravest of them all in war.</p>
<p class="verse">Then rushed the Trojans fiercely on the Greeks, <br> With Hector, sorrowing for Sarpedon’s fall, <br> Leading them on, while the bold-hearted chief, <br> Patroclus Menoetiades, aroused <br> The courage of the Greeks. He thus addressed <br> The warriors Ajax, eager like himself <br> For combat: “Be it now your welcome task, <br> O warriors Ajax, to drive back the foe; <br> He who first sprang across the Grecian wall, <br> Sarpedon, lies a corpse, and we must now <br> Dishonor the dead chief, and strip from him <br> His armor, and strike down with our good spears <br> Whoever of his comrades shall resist.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake, and all were resolute to beat <br> The enemy back; and when, on either side, <br> Trojans and Lycians, Myrmidons and Greeks, <br> Had put their phalanxes in firm array, <br> They closed, with dreadful shouts and horrid clash <br> Of arms, in fight around the dead, while Jove <br> Drew o’er that deadly fray an awful veil <br> Of darkness, that the struggle for the corpse <br> Of his dear son might rage more furiously. <br> The Trojans first drave back the dark-eyed Greeks, <br> For one was in the onset smitten down, <br> Not the least valiant of the Myrmidons⁠— <br> The son of brave Agacles, nobly born <br> Epeigeus, who aforetime, when he ruled <br> The populous Budeium, having slain <br> A noble kinsman, fled a suppliant <br> To Peleus and the silver-footed queen, <br> Thetis, his consort, and by them was sent, <br> With terrible Achilles, to the coast <br> Of courser-breeding Ilium and the siege <br> Of Troy. As now he stooped to seize the dead, <br> Illustrious Hector smote him with a stone <br> Upon the forehead, cleaving it in two <br> In the strong helmet; headlong on the corse <br> He fell, and cruel death crept over him. <br> With grief Patroclus saw his comrade slain, <br> And broke his way among the foremost ranks. <br> As a swift hawk that chases through the air <br> Starlings and daws, so didst thou dart among <br> Trojans and Lycians, for thy wrath was roused, <br> O knight Patroclus! by thy comrade’s death. <br> And now his hand struck Sthenelaüs down, <br> The dear son of Ithaemenes; he flung <br> A stone that crushed the sinews of the neck. <br> Back drew illustrious Hector, and with him <br> The warriors who were fighting in the van. <br> As far as one can send a javelin, <br> When men contend in martial games, or meet <br> Their deadly enemies in war, so far <br> Withdrew the Trojans, and the Greeks pursued. <br> The leader of the bucklered Lycian host, <br> Glaucus, was first to turn against his foes. <br> He slew the brave Bathycles, the dear son <br> Of Chalcon, who in Hellas had his home, <br> And was the richest of the Myrmidons. <br> The Lycian, turning on him suddenly <br> As he drew near pursuing, sent his spear <br> Right through his breast, and with a clash he fell. <br> Great was the sorrow of the Greeks to see <br> That valiant warrior fall; the men of Troy <br> Exulted, and pressed round him in a crowd. <br> Nor lacking was the valor of the Greeks, <br> Who met them manfully. Meriones <br> Struck down a Trojan chief, Laogonus, <br> Onetor’s valiant son. His father stood <br> Priest at the altar of Idaean Jove, <br> And like a god was honored by the realm. <br> Below the jaw and ear Meriones <br> Smote him, and instantly the life forsook <br> His limbs, and fearful darkness shrouded him. <br> Straight at Meriones Aeneas aimed <br> His brazen spear to smite him, as he came, <br> Beneath his buckler; but the Greek beheld <br> The weapon in the air, and, stooping low, <br> Escaped it; over him it passed, and stood <br> Fixed in the earth behind him, where its stem <br> Trembled, for now the rapid steel had spent <br> Its force. As thus it quivered in the ground, <br> Aeneas, who perceived that it had left <br> His powerful hand in vain, was vexed, and said: <br> “Had I but struck thee, dancer as thou art, <br> Meriones, my spear had suddenly <br> Ended thy dancing.” Then Meriones, <br> The skilful spearman, answered: “Thou art brave, <br> But thou wilt find it hard to overcome <br> The might of all who gather to repulse <br> Thy onset. Thou art mortal, and if I, <br> Aiming at thee with my good spear, should pierce <br> Thy bosom, valiant as thou art and proud <br> Of thy strong arm, thy death would bring me praise, <br> And send thy soul where gloomy Pluto dwells.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake; the brave Patroclus heard, and thus <br> Rebuked him: “Why wilt thou, Meriones, <br> With all thy valor, stand to make a speech? <br> The foe, my friend, will not be forced to leave <br> The corpse by insults; some of them must die. <br> In deeds the issue of a battle lies; <br> Words are for counsel. Now is not the time <br> To utter swelling phrases, but to fight.”</p>
<p class="verse">He ended, and went on; the godlike man <br> Followed his steps. As when from mountain dells <br> Rises, and far is heard, a crashing sound <br> Where woodmen fell the trees, such was the noise <br> From those who fought on that wide plain⁠—the din <br> Of brass, of leather, and of tough bull’s-hide <br> Smitten with swords and two-edged spears. No eye, <br> Although of keenest sight, would then have known <br> Noble Sarpedon, covered as he lay, <br> From head to foot, with weapons, blood, and dust; <br> And still the warriors thronged around the dead. <br> As when in spring-time at the cattle-stalls <br> Flies gather, humming, when the milk is drawn, <br> Round the full pails, so swarmed around the corpse <br> The combatants; nor once did Jove withdraw <br> His bright eyes from the stubborn fray, but still <br> Gazed, planning how Patroclus should be slain. <br> Uncertain whether, in the desperate strife <br> Over the great Sarpedon, to permit <br> Illustrious Hector with his spear to lay <br> The hero dead, and make his arms a spoil, <br> Or spare him yet a while, to make the war <br> More bloody. As he pondered, this seemed best: <br> That the brave comrade of Achilles first <br> Should put to flight the Trojans and their chief, <br> Hector the brazen-mailed, pursuing them <br> Toward Troy with slaughter. To this end he sent <br> Into the heart of Hector panic fear, <br> Who climbed his car and fled, and bade the rest <br> Flee also, for he saw how Jove had weighed <br> The fortunes of the day. Now none remained, <br> Not even the gallant Lycians, when they saw <br> Their monarch lying wounded to the heart <br> Among a heap of slain; for Saturn’s son <br> In that day’s strife had caused a multitude <br> To fall in death. Now when the Greeks had stripped <br> Sarpedon of the glittering brazen mail, <br> The brave son of Menoetius bade his friends <br> Convey it to the hollow ships. Meanwhile <br> The Cloud-compeller spake to Phoebus thus:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Go now, beloved Phoebus, and withdraw <br> Sarpedon from the weapons of the foe; <br> Cleanse him from the dark blood, and bear him thence, <br> And lave him in the river-stream, and shed <br> Ambrosia o’er him. Clothe him then in robes <br> Of heaven, consigning him to Sleep and Death, <br> Twin brothers, and swift bearers of the dead, <br> And they shall lay him down in Lycia’s fields, <br> That broad and opulent realm. There shall his friends <br> And kinsmen give him burial, and shall rear <br> His tomb and column⁠—honors due the dead.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake: Apollo instantly obeyed <br> His father, leaving Ida’s mountain height, <br> And sought the field of battle, and bore off <br> Noble Sarpedon from the enemy’s spears, <br> And laved him in the river-stream, and shed <br> Ambrosia o’er him. Then in robes of heaven <br> He clothed him, giving him to Sleep and Death, <br> Twin brothers, and swift bearers of the dead, <br> And they, with speed conveying it, laid down <br> The corpse in Lycia’s broad and opulent realm.</p>
<p class="verse">Meantime Patroclus, urging on his steeds <br> And charioteer, pursued, to his own hurt, <br> Trojans and Lycians. Madman! Had he then <br> Obeyed the counsel which Pelides gave, <br> The bitter doom of death had not been his. <br> But stronger than the purposes of men <br> Are those of Jove, who puts to flight the brave, <br> And takes from them the victory, though he <br> Impelled them to the battle; and he now <br> Urged on Patroclus to prolong the fight.</p>
<p class="verse">Who first, when thus the gods decreed thy death, <br> Fell by thy hand, Patroclus, and who last? <br> Adrastus first, Autonoüs next, and then <br> Echeclus; then died Perimus, the son <br> Of Meges; then with Melanippus fell <br> Epistor; next was Elasus o’ercome, <br> And Mulius, and Pylartes. These he slew, <br> While all the rest betook themselves to flight.</p>
<p class="verse">Then had the Greeks possessed themselves of Troy, <br> With all its lofty portals, by the hand <br> And valor of Patroclus, for his rage <br> Was terrible beyond the rage of all <br> Who bore the spear, had not Apollo stood <br> On a strong tower to menace him with ill, <br> And aid the Trojans. Thrice Patroclus climbed <br> A shoulder of the lofty wall, and thrice <br> Apollo, striking his immortal hands <br> Against the glittering buckler, thrust him down; <br> And when, for the fourth time, the godlike man <br> Essayed to mount the wall, the archer-god, <br> Phoebus, encountered him with fearful threats: <br> “Noble Patroclus, hold thy hand, nor deem <br> The city of the warlike Trojans doomed <br> To fall beneath thy spear, nor by the arm <br> Of Peleus’ son, though mightier far than thou.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake; Patroclus, fearful of the wrath <br> Of the archer-god, withdrew, and stood afar, <br> While Hector, at the Scaean gates, restrained <br> His coursers, doubtful whether to renew <br> The fight by mingling with the crowd again, <br> Or gather all his host within the walls <br> By a loud summons. As he pondered thus, <br> Apollo stood beside him in the form <br> Of Asius, a young warrior and a brave, <br> Uncle of Hector, the great horse-tamer, <br> And brother of Queen Hecuba, and son <br> Of Dymas, who in Phrygia dwelt beside <br> The streams of the Sangarius. Putting on <br> His shape and aspect, thus Apollo said:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Why, Hector, dost thou pause from battle thus? <br> Nay, it becomes thee not. Were I in might <br> Greater than thou, as I am less, full soon <br> Wouldst thou repent this shrinking from the war. <br> Come boldly on, and urge thy firm-paced steeds <br> Against Patroclus; slay him on the field, <br> And Phoebus will requite thee with renown.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake, and mingled in the hard-fought fray, <br> While noble Hector bade his charioteer, <br> The brave Cebriones, ply well the lash, <br> And join the battle. Phoebus went before, <br> Entering the crowd, and spread dismay among <br> The Greeks, and gave the glory of the hour <br> To Hector and the Trojans. Little heed <br> Paid Hector to the rest, nor raised his arm <br> To slay them, but urged on his firm-paced steeds <br> To meet Patroclus, who, beholding him, <br> Leaped from his car. In his left hand he held <br> A spear, and with the other lifting up <br> A white, rough stone, the largest he could grasp, <br> Flung it with all its force. It flew not wide, <br> Nor flew in vain, but smote Cebriones, <br> The warlike chief who guided Hector’s steeds, <br> A spurious son of Priam the renowned. <br> The sharp stone smote his forehead as he held <br> The reins, and crushed both eyebrows in; the bone <br> Resisted not the blow; the warrior’s eyes <br> Fell in the dust before his very feet. <br> Down from the sumptuous seat he plunged, as dives <br> A swimmer, and the life forsook his limbs. <br> And this, Patroclus, was thy cruel jest:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Truly a nimble man is this who dives <br> With such expertness. Were this, now, the sea, <br> Where fish are bred, and he were searching it <br> For oysters, he might get an ample store <br> For many men, in leaping from a ship, <br> Though in a storm, so skilfully he dives <br> Even from the chariot to the plain. No doubt <br> There must be divers in the town of Troy.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake, and sprang upon Cebriones. <br> With all a lion’s fury, which attacks <br> The stables and is wounded in the breast, <br> And perishes through his own daring; thus, <br> Patroclus, didst thou fall upon the slain, <br> While Hector, hastening also, left his steeds, <br> And both contended for Cebriones. <br> As lions for the carcass of a deer <br> Fight on a mountain summit, hungry both, <br> And both unyielding, thus two mighty men <br> Of war, Patroclus Menoetiades <br> And glorious Hector, eager each to smite <br> His adversary with the cruel spear, <br> Fought for Cebriones. The slain man’s head <br> Was seized by Hector’s powerful hand, whose grasp <br> Relaxed not, while Patroclus held the foot; <br> And, thronging to the spot, the other Greeks <br> And Trojans mingled in the desperate strife.</p>
<p class="verse">As when the east wind and the south contend <br> In the open mountain grounds, and furiously <br> Assail the deep old woods of beech and ash <br> And barky cornel, flinging their long boughs <br> Against each other with a mighty roar, <br> And crash of those that break, so did the Greeks <br> And Trojans meet with mutual blows, and slay <br> Each other; nor had either host a thought <br> Of shameful flight. Full many a trenchant spear <br> Went to its mark beside Cebriones, <br> And many a wingèd arrow that had left <br> The bowstring; many a massive stone was hurled <br> Against the ringing bucklers, as they fought <br> Around the dead, while he, the mighty, lay <br> Stretched on the ground amid the eddying dust, <br> Forgetful of his art of horsemanship.</p>
<p class="verse">While yet the sun was climbing to his place <br> In middle heaven, the men of either host <br> Were smitten by the weapons, and in both <br> The people fell; but when he stooped to the west <br> The Greeks prevailed, and from that storm of darts <br> And tumult of the Trojans they drew forth <br> Cebriones, and stripped him of his arms. <br> Still rushed Patroclus onward, bent to wreak <br> His fury on the Trojans. Fierce as Mars, <br> He charged their squadrons thrice with fearful shouts, <br> And thrice he laid nine warriors in the dust. <br> But as with godlike energy he made <br> The fourth assault, then clearly was it seen, <br> Patroclus, that thy life was near its end, <br> For Phoebus terribly in that fierce strife <br> Encountered thee. Patroclus saw him not <br> Advancing in the tumult, for he moved <br> Unseen in darkness. Coming close behind, <br> He smote, with open palm, the hero’s back <br> Between the ample shoulders, and his eyes <br> Reeled with the blow, while Phoebus from his head <br> Struck the tall helm, that, clanking, rolled away <br> Under the horses’ feet; its crest was soiled <br> With blood and dust, though never till that hour <br> Had dust defiled its horse-hair plume; for once <br> That helmet guarded an illustrious head, <br> The glorious brows of Peleus’ son, and now <br> Jove destined it for Hector, to be worn <br> In battle; and his death was also near. <br> The spear Patroclus wielded, edged with brass, <br> Long, tough, and huge, was broken in his hands, <br> And his broad buckler, dropping with its band, <br> Lay on the ground, while Phoebus, son of Jove, <br> Undid the fastenings of his mail. With mind <br> Bewildered, and with powerless limbs, he stood <br> As thunderstruck. Then a Dardanian named <br> Euphorbus, son of Panthoüs, who excelled <br> His comrades in the wielding of the spear, <br> The race, and horsemanship, approaching, smote <br> Patroclus in the back with his keen spear, <br> Between the shoulder-blades. Already he <br> Had dashed down twenty warriors from their cars, <br> Guiding his own, a learner in the art <br> Of war. The first was he who threw a lance <br> At thee, Patroclus, yet o’ercome thee not; <br> For, plucking from thy back its ashen stem, <br> He fled, and mingled with the crowd, nor dared <br> Await thy coming, though thou wert unarmed, <br> While, weakened by that wound and by the blow <br> Given by the god, Patroclus turned and sought <br> Shelter from danger in the Grecian ranks; <br> But Hector, when he saw the gallant Greek <br> Thus wounded and retreating, left his place <br> Among the squadrons, and, advancing, pierced <br> Patroclus with his spear, below the belt, <br> Driving the weapon deep. The hero fell <br> With clashing mail, and all the Greeks beheld <br> His fall with grief. As when a lion bears <br> A stubborn boar to earth, what time the twain <br> Fight on the mountains for a slender spring, <br> Both thirsty and both fierce, the lion’s strength <br> Lays prone his panting foe, so Priam’s son <br> Slew, fighting hand to hand, the valiant Greek, <br> Son of Menoetius, who himself had slain <br> So many. Hector gloried over him <br> With wingèd words: “Patroclus, thou didst think <br> To lay our city waste, and carry off <br> Our women captive in thy ships to Greece. <br> Madman! In their defence the fiery steeds <br> Of Hector sweep the battle-field, and I, <br> Mightiest of all the Trojans, with the spear <br> Will guard them from the doom of slavery. <br> Now vultures shall devour thee, wretched youth! <br> Achilles, mighty though he be, has brought <br> No help to thee, though doubtless when he sent <br> Thee forth to battle, and remained within, <br> He charged thee thus: ‘Patroclus, flower of knights, <br> Return not to the fleet until thy hand <br> Hath torn the bloody armor from the corpse <br> Of the man-queller Hector.’ So he spake, <br> And filled with idle hopes thy foolish heart.”</p>
<p class="verse">Then thou, Patroclus, with a faltering voice, <br> Didst answer thus: “Now, Hector, while thou mayst, <br> Utter thy boast in swelling words, since Jove <br> And Phoebus gave the victory to thee. <br> Easily have they vanquished me; ’twas they <br> Who stripped the armor from my limbs, for else, <br> If twenty such as thou had met me, all <br> Had perished by my spear. A cruel fate <br> O’ertakes me, aided by Latona’s son, <br> The god, and by Euphorbus among men. <br> Thou who shalt take my spoil art but the third; <br> Yet hear my words, and keep them in thy thought. <br> Not long shalt thou remain alive; thy death <br> By violence is at hand, and thou must fall, <br> Slain by the hand of great Aeacides.”</p>
<p class="verse">While he was speaking, death stole over him <br> And veiled his senses, while the soul forsook <br> His limbs and flew to Hades, sorrowing <br> For its sad lot, to part from life in youth <br> And prime of strength. Illustrious Hector thus <br> Answered the dying man: “Why threaten me, <br> Patroclus, with an early death? Who knows <br> That he, thy friend, whom fair-haired Thetis bore, <br> Achilles, may not sooner lose his life, <br> Slain by my spear?” He spake, and set his heel <br> Upon the slain, and from the wound drew forth <br> His brazen spear and pushed the corpse aside, <br> And with the weapon hurried on to smite <br> Godlike Automedon, the charioteer <br> Of swift Aeacides; but him the steeds <br> Fleet-footed and immortal, which the gods <br> Bestowed on Peleus, swiftly bore away.</p>
