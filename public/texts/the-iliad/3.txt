<p class="lede">Proposal of Hector to end the war by a duel between Menelaus and Paris, the victor to possess Helen and her wealth⁠—Priam and Helen behold the combat⁠—Description of the principal Greek princes and chiefs, given by Helen to Priam⁠—Paris snatched away from the combat by Venus, as he was in danger of being slain, and conveyed to the bedchamber of Helen.</p>
<p class="verse">Now when both armies were arrayed for war, <br> Each with its chiefs, the Trojan host moved on <br> With shouts and clang of arms, as when the cry <br> Of cranes is in the air, that, flying south <br> From winter and its mighty breadth of rain, <br> Wing their way over ocean, and at dawn <br> Bring fearful battle to the pygmy race, <br> Bloodshed and death. But silently the Greeks <br> Went forward, breathing valor, mindful still <br> To aid each other in the coming fray.</p>
<p class="verse">As when the south wind shrouds a mountaintop <br> In vapors that awake the shepherd’s fear⁠— <br> A surer covert for the thief than night⁠— <br> And round him one can only see as far <br> As one can hurl a stone⁠—such was the cloud <br> Of dust that from the warriors’ trampling feet <br> Rose round their rapid march and filled the air.</p>
<p class="verse">Now drew they near each other, face to face, <br> And Paris in the Trojan van pressed on, <br> In presence like a god. A leopard’s hide <br> Was thrown across his shoulders, and he bore <br> A crooked bow and falchion. Brandishing <br> Two brazen-pointed javelins, he defied <br> To mortal fight the bravest of the Greeks.</p>
<p class="verse">Him, Menelaus, loved of Mars, beheld <br> Advancing with large strides before the rest; <br> And as a hungry lion who has made <br> A prey of some large beast⁠—a horned stag <br> Or mountain goat⁠—rejoices, and with speed <br> Devours it, though swift hounds and sturdy youth <br> Press on his flank, so Menelaus felt <br> Great joy when Paris, of the godlike form, <br> Appeared in sight, for now he thought to wreak <br> His vengeance on the guilty one, and straight <br> Sprang from his car to earth with all his arms.</p>
<p class="verse">But when the graceful Paris saw the chief <br> Come toward him from the foremost ranks, his heart <br> Was troubled, and he turned and passed among <br> His fellow-warriors and avoided death. <br> As one, who meets within a mountain glade <br> A serpent, starts aside with sudden fright, <br> And takes the backward way with trembling limbs <br> And cheeks all white⁠—the graceful Paris thus <br> Before the son of Atreus shrank in fear, <br> And mingled with the high-souled sons of Troy. <br> Hector beheld and thus upbraided him <br> Harshly: “O luckless Paris, nobly formed, <br> Yet woman-follower and seducer! Thou <br> Shouldst never have been born, or else at best <br> Have died unwedded; better were it far, <br> Than thus to be a scandal and a scorn <br> To all who look on thee. The long-haired Greeks, <br> How they will laugh, who for thy gallant looks <br> Deemed thee a hero, when there dwells in thee <br> No spirit and no courage? Wast thou such <br> When, crossing the great deep in thy stanch ships <br> With chosen comrades, thou didst make thy way <br> Among a stranger-people and bear off <br> A beautiful woman from that distant land, <br> Allied by marriage-ties to warrior-men⁠— <br> A mischief to thy father and to us <br> And all the people, to our foes a joy, <br> And a disgrace to thee? Why couldst thou not <br> Await Atrides? Then hadst thou been taught <br> From what a valiant warrior thou didst take <br> His blooming spouse. Thy harp will not avail, <br> Nor all the gifts of Venus, nor thy locks, <br> Nor thy fair form, when thou art laid in dust. <br> Surely the sons of Troy are faint of heart, <br> Else hadst thou, for the evil thou hast wrought, <br> Been laid beneath a coverlet of stone.”</p>
<p class="verse">Then Paris, of the godlike presence, spake <br> In answer: “Hector, thy rebuke is just; <br> Thou dost not wrong me. Dauntless is thy heart; <br> ’Tis like an axe when, wielded by the hand <br> That hews the shipwright’s plank, it cuts right through, <br> Doubling the wielder’s force. Such tameless heart <br> Dwells in thy bosom. Yet reproach me not <br> With the fair gifts which golden Venus gave. <br> Whatever in their grace the gods bestow <br> Is not to be rejected: ’tis not ours <br> To choose what they shall give us. But if thou <br> Desirest to behold my prowess shown <br> In combat, cause the Trojans and the Greeks <br> To pause from battle, while, between the hosts, as <br> I and the warlike Menelaus strive <br> In single fight for Helen and her wealth. <br> Whoever shall prevail and prove himself <br> The better warrior, let him take with him <br> The treasure and the woman, and depart; <br> While all the other Trojans, having made <br> A faithful league of amity, shall dwell <br> On Ilium’s fertile plain, and all the Greeks <br> Return to Argos, famed for noble steeds, <br> And to Achaia, famed for lovely dames.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake, and Hector, hearing him, rejoiced, <br> And went between the hosts, and with his spear, <br> Held by the middle, pressed the phalanxes <br> Of Trojans back, and made them all sit down. <br> The long-haired Greeks meanwhile, with bended bows, <br> Took aim against him, just about to send <br> Arrows and stones; but Agamemnon, king <br> Of men, beheld, and thus he cried aloud:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Restrain yourselves, ye Argives; let not fly <br> Your arrows, ye Achaians; Hector asks⁠— <br> He of the beamy helmet asks to speak.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake, and they refrained, and all, at once, <br> Were silent. Hector then stood forth and said:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Hearken, ye Trojans and ye nobly-armed <br> Achaians, to what Paris says by me. <br> He bids the Trojans and the Greeks lay down <br> Their shining arms upon the teeming earth, <br> And he and Menelaus, loved of Mars, <br> Will strive in single combat, on the ground <br> Between the hosts, for Helen and her wealth; <br> And he who shall o’ercome, and prove himself <br> The better warrior, to his home shall bear <br> The treasure and the woman, while the rest <br> Shall frame a solemn covenant of peace.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake, and both the hosts in silence heard. <br> Then Menelaus, great in battle, said:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Now hear me also⁠—me whose spirit feels <br> The wrong most keenly. I propose that now <br> The Greeks and Trojans separate reconciled, <br> For greatly have ye suffered for the sake <br> Of this my quarrel, and the original fault <br> Of Paris. Whomsoever fate ordains <br> To perish, let him die; but let the rest <br> Be from this moment reconciled, and part. <br> And bring an offering of two lambs⁠—one white, <br> The other black⁠—to Earth and to the Sun, <br> And we ourselves will offer one to Jove. <br> And be the mighty Priam here, that he <br> May sanction this our compact⁠—for his sons <br> Are arrogant and faithless⁠—lest some hand <br> Wickedly break the covenant of Jove. <br> The younger men are of a fickle mood; <br> But when an elder shares the act he looks <br> Both to the past and future, and provides <br> What is most fitting and the best for all.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake, and both the Greeks and Trojans heard <br> His words with joy, and hoped the hour was come <br> To end the hard-fought war. They reined their steeds <br> Back to the ranks, alighted, and put off <br> Their armor, which they laid upon the ground <br> Near them in piles, with little space between.</p>
<p class="verse">Then Hector sent two heralds forth with speed <br> Into the town, to bring the lambs and call <br> King Priam. Meanwhile Agamemnon bade <br> Talthybius seek the hollow ships and find <br> A lamb for the altar. He obeyed the words <br> Of noble Agamemnon, king of men.</p>
<p class="verse">Meanwhile to white-armed Helen Iris came <br> A messenger. She took a form that seemed <br> Laodice, the sister of Paris, whom <br> Antenor’s son, King Helicaon, wed⁠— <br> Fairest of Priam’s daughters. She drew near <br> To Helen, in the palace, weaving there <br> An ample web, a shining double-robe, <br> Whereon were many conflicts fairly wrought, <br> Endured by the horse-taming sons of Troy <br> And brazen-mailed Achaians for her sake <br> Upon the field of Mars. Beside her stood <br> Swift-footed Iris, and addressed her thus:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Dear lady, come and see the Trojan knights <br> And brazen mailed Achaians doing things <br> To wonder at. They who, in this sad war, <br> Eager to slay each other, lately met <br> In murderous combat on the field, are now <br> Seated in silence, and the war hath ceased. <br> They lean upon their shields, their massive spears <br> Are near them, planted in the ground upright. <br> Paris, and Menelaus, loved of Mars, <br> With their long lances will contend for thee, <br> And thou wilt be declared the victor’s spouse.”</p>
<p class="verse">She said, and in the heart of Helen woke <br> Dear recollections of her former spouse <br> And of her home and kindred. Instantly <br> She left her chamber, robed and veiled in white. <br> And shedding tender tears; yet not alone, <br> For with her went two maidens⁠—Aethra, child <br> Of Pitheus, and the large-eyed Clymenè. <br> Straight to the Scaean gates they walked, by which <br> Panthoüs, Priam, and Thymoetes sat, <br> Lampus and Clytius, Hicetaon sprung <br> From Mars, Antenor and Ucalegon, <br> Two sages⁠—elders of the people all. <br> Beside the gates they sat, unapt, through age, <br> For tasks of war, but men of fluent speech, <br> Like the cicadas that within the wood <br> Sit on the trees and utter delicate sounds. <br> Such were the nobles of the Trojan race <br> Who sat upon the tower. But when they marked <br> The approach of Helen, to each other thus <br> With wingèd words, but in low tones, they said:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Small blame is theirs, if both the Trojan knights <br> And brazen-mailed Achaians have endured <br> So long so many evils for the sake <br> Of that one woman. She is wholly like <br> In feature to the deathless goddesses. <br> So be it: let her, peerless as she is, <br> Return on board the fleet, nor stay to bring <br> Disaster upon us and all our race.”</p>
<p class="verse">So spake the elders. Priam meantime called <br> To Helen: “Come, dear daughter, sit by me. <br> Thou canst behold thy former husband hence, <br> Thy kindred and thy friends. I blame thee not; <br> The blame is with the immortals who have sent <br> These pestilent Greeks against me. Sit and name <br> For me this mighty man, the Grecian chief, <br> Gallant and tall. True, there are taller men; <br> But of such noble form and dignity <br> I never saw: in truth, a kingly man.”</p>
<p class="verse">And Helen, fairest among women, thus <br> Answered: “Dear second father, whom at once <br> I fear and honor, would that cruel death <br> Had overtaken me before I left, <br> To wander with thy son, my marriage-bed, <br> And my dear daughter, and the company <br> Of friends I loved. But that was not to be; <br> And now I pine and weep. Yet will I tell <br> What thou dost ask. The hero whom thou seest <br> Is the wide-ruling Agamemnon, son <br> Of Atreus, and is both a gracious king <br> And a most dreaded warrior. He was once <br> Brother-in-law to me, if I may speak⁠— <br> Lost as I am to shame⁠—of such a tie.”</p>
<p class="verse">She said, the aged man admired, and then <br> He spake again: “O son of Atreus, born <br> Under a happy fate, and fortunate <br> Among the sons of men! A mighty host <br> Of Grecian youths obey thy rule. I went <br> To Phrygia once⁠—that land of vines⁠—and there <br> Saw many Phrygians, heroes on fleet steeds, <br> The troops of Otreus, and of Mygdon, shaped <br> Like one of the immortals. They encamped <br> By the Sangarius. I was an ally; <br> My troops were ranked with theirs upon the day <br> When came the unsexed Amazons to war. <br> Yet even there I saw not such a host <br> As this of black-eyed Greeks who muster here.”</p>
<p class="verse">Then Priam saw Ulysses, and inquired:⁠— <br> “Dear daughter, tell me also who is that, <br> Less tall than Agamemnon, yet more broad <br> In chest and shoulders. On the teeming earth <br> His armor lies, but he, from place to place, <br> Walks round among the ranks of soldiery, <br> As when the thick-fleeced father of the flocks <br> Moves through the multitude of his white sheep.”</p>
<p class="verse">And Jove-descended Helen answered thus:⁠— <br> “That is Ulysses, man of many arts, <br> Son of Laertes, reared in Ithaca, <br> That rugged isle, and skilled in every form <br> Of shrewd device and action wisely planned.”</p>
<p class="verse">Then spake the sage Antenor: “Thou hast said <br> The truth, O lady. This Ulysses once <br> Came on an embassy, concerning thee, <br> To Troy with Menelaus, great in war; <br> And I received them as my guests, and they <br> Were lodged within my palace, and I learned <br> The temper and the qualities of both. <br> When both were standing mid the men of Troy, <br> I marked that Menelaus’s broad chest <br> Made him the more conspicuous, but when both <br> Were seated, greater was the dignity <br> Seen in Ulysses. When they both addressed <br> The council, Menelaus briefly spake <br> In pleasing tones, though with few words⁠—as one <br> Not given to loose and wandering speech⁠—although <br> The younger. When the wise Ulysses rose, <br> He stood with eyes cast down, and fixed on earth, <br> And neither swayed his sceptre to the right <br> Nor to the left, but held it motionless, <br> Like one unused to public speech. He seemed <br> An idiot out of humor. But when forth <br> He sent from his full lungs his mighty voice, <br> And words came like a fall of winter snow, <br> No mortal then would dare to strive with him <br> For mastery in speech. We less admired <br> The aspect of Ulysses than his words.”</p>
<p class="verse">Beholding Ajax then, the aged king <br> Asked yet again: “Who is that other chief <br> Of the Achaians, tall, and large of limb⁠— <br> Taller and broader-chested than the rest?”</p>
<p class="verse">Helen, the beautiful and richly-robed, <br> Answered: “Thou seest the mighty Ajax there, <br> The bulwark of the Greeks. On the other side, <br> Among his Cretans, stands Idomeneus, <br> Of godlike aspect, near to whom are grouped <br> The leaders of the Cretans. Oftentimes <br> The warlike Menelaus welcomed him <br> Within our palace, when he came from Crete. <br> I could point out and name the other chiefs <br> Of the dark-eyed Achaians. Two alone, <br> Princes among their people, are not seen⁠— <br> Castor the fearless horseman, and the skilled <br> In boxing, Pollux⁠—twins; one mother bore <br> Both them and me. Came they not with the rest <br> From pleasant Lacedaemon to the war? <br> Or, having crossed the deep in their good ships, <br> Shun they to fight among the valiant ones <br> Of Greece, because of my reproach and shame?”</p>
<p class="verse">She spake; but they already lay in earth <br> In Lacedaemon, their dear native land.</p>
<p class="verse">And now the heralds through the city bore <br> The sacred pledges of the gods⁠—two lambs, <br> And joyous wine, the fruit of Earth, within <br> A goat-skin. One of them⁠—Idasus⁠—brought <br> A glistening vase and golden drinking-cups, <br> And summoned, in these words, the aged king:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Son of Laomedon, arise! The chiefs <br> Who lead the Trojan knights and brazen-mailed <br> Achaians pray thee to descend at once <br> Into the plain, that thou mayst ratify <br> A faithful compact. Alexander now <br> And warlike Menelaus will contend <br> With their long spears for Helen. She and all <br> Her treasures are to be the conqueror’s prize; <br> While all the other Trojans, having made <br> A faithful league of amity, shall dwell <br> On Ilium’s fertile plain, and all the Greeks <br> Return to Argos, famed for noble steeds, <br> And to Achaia, famed for lovely dames.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake, and Priam, shuddering, heard and bade <br> The attendants yoke the horses to his car. <br> Soon were they yoked; he mounted first and drew <br> The reins; Antenor took a place within <br> The sumptuous car, and through the Scaean gates <br> They guided the fleet coursers toward the field.</p>
<p class="verse">Now when the twain had come where lay the hosts <br> Of Trojans and Achaians, down they stepped <br> Upon the teeming earth, and went among <br> The assembled armies. Quickly, as they came, <br> Rose Agamemnon, king of men, and next <br> Uprose the wise Ulysses. To the spot <br> The illustrious heralds brought the sacred things <br> That bind a treaty, and with mingled wine <br> They filled a chalice, and upon the hands <br> Of all the kings poured water. Then the son <br> Of Atreus drew a dagger which he wore <br> Slung by his sword’s huge sheath, and clipped away <br> The forelocks of the lambs, and parted them <br> Among the Trojan and Achaian chiefs, <br> And stood with lifted hands and prayed aloud:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“O Father Jupiter, who rulest all <br> From Ida, mightiest, most august! And thou, <br> O all-beholding and all-hearing Sun! <br> Ye Rivers, and thou Earth, and ye who dwell <br> Beneath the earth and punish after death <br> Those who have sworn false oaths, bear witness ye, <br> And keep unbroken this day’s promises. <br> If Alexander in the combat slay <br> My brother Menelaus, he shall keep <br> Helen and all her wealth, while we return <br> Homeward in our good ships. If, otherwise, <br> The bright-haired Menelaus take the life <br> Of Alexander, Helen and her wealth <br> Shall be restored, and they of Troy shall pay <br> Such fine as may be meet, and may be long <br> Remembered in the ages yet to come. <br> And then if, after Alexander’s fall, <br> Priam and Priam’s sons refuse the fine, <br> I shall make war for it, and keep my place <br> By Troy until I gain the end I seek.”</p>
<p class="verse">So spake the king, and with the cruel steel <br> Cut the lambs’ throats, and laid them on the ground, <br> Panting and powerless, for the dagger took <br> Their lives away. Then over them they poured <br> Wine from the chalice, drawn in golden cups, <br> And prayed to the ever-living gods; and thus <br> Were Trojans and Achaians heard to say:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“O Jupiter most mighty and august! <br> Whoever first shall break these solemn oaths, <br> So may their brains flow down upon the earth⁠— <br> Theirs and their children’s⁠—like the wine we pour, <br> And be their wives the wives of other men.”</p>
<p class="verse">Such was the people’s vow. Saturnian Jove <br> Confirmed it not. Then Priam, of the line <br> Of Dardanus, addressed the armies thus:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Hear me, ye Trojans, and ye well-greaved Greeks! <br> For me I must return to wind-swept Troy. <br> I cannot bear, with these old eyes, to look <br> On my dear son engaged in desperate fight <br> With Menelaus, the beloved of Mars. <br> Jove and the ever-living gods alone <br> Know which of them shall meet the doom of death.”</p>
<p class="verse">So spake the godlike man, and placed the lambs <br> Within his chariot, mounted, and drew up <br> The reins. Antenor by him took his place <br> Within the sumptuous chariot. Then they turned <br> The horses and retraced their way to Troy.</p>
<p class="verse">But Hector, son of Priam, and the great <br> Ulysses measured off a fitting space, <br> And in a brazen helmet, to decide <br> Which warrior first should hurl the brazen spear, <br> They shook the lots, while all the people round <br> Lifted their hands to heaven and prayed the gods; <br> And thus the Trojans and Achaians said:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“O Father Jove, who rulest from the top <br> Of Ida, mightiest one and most august! <br> Whichever of these twain has done the wrong, <br> Grant that he pass to Pluto’s dwelling, slain, <br> While friendship and a faithful league are ours.”</p>
<p class="verse">So spake they. Hector of the beamy helm <br> Looked back and shook the lots. Forth leaped at once <br> The lot of Paris. Then they took their seats <br> In ranks beside their rapid steeds, and where <br> Lay their rich armor. Paris the divine, <br> Husband of bright-haired Helen, there put on <br> His shining panoply⁠—upon his legs <br> Fair greaves, with silver clasps, and on his breast <br> His brother’s mail, Lycaon’s, fitting well <br> His form. Around his shoulders then he hung <br> His silver-studded sword, and stout, broad shield, <br> And gave his glorious brows the dreadful helm, <br> Dark with its horse-hair plume. A massive spear <br> Filled his right hand. Meantime the warlike son <br> Of Atreus clad himself in like array.</p>
<p class="verse">And now when both were armed for fight, and each <br> Had left his host, and, coming forward, walked <br> Between the Trojans and the Greeks, and frowned <br> Upon the other, a mute wonder held <br> The Trojan cavaliers and well-greaved Greeks. <br> There near each other in the measured space <br> They stood in wrathful mood with lifted spears.</p>
<p class="verse">First Paris hurled his massive spear; it smote <br> The round shield of Atrides, but the brass <br> Broke not beneath the blow; the weapon’s point <br> Was bent on that strong shield. The next assault <br> Atrides Menelaus made, but first <br> Offered this prayer to Father Jupiter:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“O sovereign Jove! Vouchsafe that I avenge <br> On guilty Paris wrongs which he was first <br> To offer; let him fall beneath my hand, <br> That men may dread hereafter to requite <br> The friendship of a host with injury.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake, and flung his brandished spear; it smote <br> The round shield of Priamides; right through <br> The shining buckler went the rapid steel, <br> And, cutting the soft tunic near the flank, <br> Stood fixed in the fair corselet. Paris bent <br> Sideways before it and escaped his death. <br> Atrides drew his silver studded sword, <br> Lifted it high and smote his enemy’s crest. <br> The weapon, shattered to four fragments, fell. <br> He looked to the broad heaven, and thus exclaimed:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“O Father Jove! Thou art of all the gods <br> The most unfriendly. I had hoped to avenge <br> The wrong by Paris done me, but my sword <br> Is broken in my grasp, and from my hand <br> The spear was vainly flung and gave no wound.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake, and, rushing forward, seized the helm <br> Of Paris by its horse-hair crest, and turned <br> And dragged him toward the well-armed Greeks. Beneath <br> His tender throat the embroidered band that held <br> The helmet to the chin was choking him. <br> And now had Menelaus dragged him thence, <br> And earned great glory, if the child of Jove, <br> Venus, had not perceived his plight in time. <br> She broke the ox-hide band; an empty helm <br> Followed the powerful hand; the hero saw, <br> Swung it aloft and hurled it toward the Greeks, <br> And there his comrades seized it. He again <br> Rushed with his brazen spear to slay his foe. <br> But Venus⁠—for a goddess easily <br> Can work such marvels⁠—rescued him, and, wrapped <br> In a thick shadow, bore him from the field <br> And placed him in his chamber, where the air <br> Was sweet with perfumes. Then she took her way <br> To summon Helen. On the lofty tower <br> She found her, midst a throng of Trojan dames, <br> And plucked her perfumed robe. She took the form <br> And features of a spinner of the fleece, <br> An aged dame, who used to comb for her <br> The fair white wool in Lacedaemon’s halls, <br> And loved her much. In such an humble guise <br> The goddess Venus thus to Helen spake:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Come hither, Alexander sends for thee; <br> He now is in his chamber and at rest <br> On his carved couch; in beauty and attire <br> Resplendent, not like one who just returns <br> From combat with a hero, but like one <br> Who goes to mingle in the choral dance, <br> Or, when the dance is ended, takes his seat.”</p>
<p class="verse">She spake, and Helen heard her, deeply moved; <br> Yet when she marked the goddess’s fair neck, <br> Beautiful bosom, and soft, lustrous eyes, <br> Her heart was touched with awe, and thus she said:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Strange being! Why wilt thou delude me still? <br> Wouldst thou decoy me further on among <br> The populous Phrygian towns, or those that stud <br> Pleasant Maeonia, where there haply dwells <br> Someone of mortal race whom thou dost deign <br> To make thy favorite. Hast thou seen, perhaps, <br> That Menelaus, having overpowered <br> The noble Alexander, seeks to bear <br> Me, hated as I must be, to his home? <br> And hast thou therefore fallen on this device? <br> Go to him, sit by him, renounce for him <br> The company of gods, and never more <br> Return to heaven, but suffer with him; watch <br> Beside him till he take thee for his wife <br> Or handmaid. Thither I shall never go, <br> To adorn his couch and to disgrace myself. <br> The Trojan dames would taunt me. O, the griefs <br> That press upon my soul are infinite!”</p>
<p class="verse">Displeased, the goddess Venus answered: “Wretch, <br> Incense me not, lest I abandon thee <br> In anger, and detest thee with a zeal <br> As great as is my love, and lest I cause <br> Trojans and Greeks to hate thee, so that thou <br> Shalt miserably perish.” Thus she spake; <br> And Helen, Jove-begotten, struck with awe, <br> Wrapped in a robe of shining white, went forth <br> In silence from amidst the Trojan dames, <br> Unheeded, for the goddess led the way.</p>
<p class="verse">When now they stood beneath the sumptuous roof <br> Of Alexander, straightway did the maids <br> Turn to their wonted tasks, while she went up, <br> Fairest of women, to her chamber. There <br> The laughing Venus brought and placed a seat <br> Right opposite to Paris. Helen sat, <br> Daughter of aegis-bearing Jove, with eyes <br> Averted, and reproached her husband thus:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Com’st thou from battle? Rather would that thou <br> Hadst perished by the mighty hand of him <br> Who was my husband. It was once, I know, <br> Thy boast that thou wert more than peer in strength <br> And power of hand, and practice with the spear, <br> To warlike Menelaus. Go then now, <br> Defy him to the combat once again. <br> And yet I counsel thee to stand aloof, <br> Nor rashly seek a combat, hand to hand, <br> With fair haired Menelaus, lest perchance <br> He smite thee with his spear and thou be slain.”</p>
<p class="verse">Then Paris answered: “Woman, chide me not <br> Thus harshly. True it is, that, with the aid <br> Of Pallas, Menelaus hath obtained <br> The victory; but I may vanquish him <br> In turn, for we have also gods with us. <br> Give we the hour to dalliance; never yet <br> Have I so strongly proved the power of love⁠— <br> Not even when I bore thee from thy home <br> In pleasant Lacedaemon, traversing <br> The deep in my good ships, and in the isle <br> Of Cranaë made thee mine⁠—such glow of love <br> Possesses me, and sweetness of desire.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake, and to the couch went up; his wife <br> Followed, and that fair couch received them both.</p>
<p class="verse">Meantime Atrides, like a beast of prey, <br> Went fiercely ranging through the crowd in search <br> Of godlike Alexander. None of all <br> The Trojans, or of their renowned allies, <br> Could point him out to Menelaus, loved <br> Of Mars; and had they known his lurking-place <br> They would not for his sake have kept him hid, <br> For like black death they hated him. Then stood <br> Among them Agamemnon, king of men, <br> And spake: “Ye Trojans and Achaians, hear, <br> And ye allies. The victory belongs <br> To warlike Menelaus. Ye will then <br> Restore the Argive Helen and her wealth, <br> And pay the fitting fine, which shall remain <br> A memory to men in future times.”</p>
<p class="verse">Thus spake the son of Atreus, and the rest <br> Of the Achaian host approved his words.</p>
