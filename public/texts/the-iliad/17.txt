<p class="lede">Contest for the body of Patroclus, which is guarded by Menelaus⁠—Death of Euphorbus⁠—Retreat of Menelaus, and his return with Ajax, after which Hector is obliged to give way⁠—Hector reproved for this by Glaucus⁠—He puts on the armor of Patroclus, and renews the contest, driving back the Greeks⁠—Rally of the Greeks by Ajax⁠—Bravery of Aeneas⁠—Flight of Automedon with the horses and chariot of Patroclus⁠—The defenders of the body of Patroclus involved in darkness, which is dispelled at the prayer of Ajax⁠—A message sent to Achilles informing him of the death of Patroclus, whose body is rescued and borne off by Menelaus and Meriones.</p>
<p class="verse">The warlike Menelaus, Atreus’ son, <br> Beheld Patroclus fall by Trojan hands, <br> And came in glittering armor to the van <br> To guard the body of the slain. As walks <br> A heifer moaning round her new-born young, <br> So fair-haired Menelaus stalked around <br> The body of Patroclus, holding forth <br> His spear and great round shield, intent to slay <br> Whoever came against him. But the son <br> Of Panthoüs, mighty spearman, not the less <br> Intent to spoil the illustrious dead, drew near, <br> And spake to warlike Menelaus thus:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Atrides Menelaus, reared by Jove, <br> And leader of thy host, give way and leave <br> The dead, and quit to me his bloody spoil; <br> For none of our brave Trojans and allies <br> Smote him in deadly combat with the spear, <br> Before me. Leave me therefore to receive <br> The glory due me from the sons of Troy, <br> Else will I smite thee too, and thou wilt lose <br> Thy precious life!” Indignant at the word, <br> The fair-haired Menelaus answered him:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“O Father Jove! Unseemly boasts are these! <br> For not the panther’s nor the lion’s might, <br> Nor that of the fierce forest-boar whose rage <br> Is heightened into fury, is as great <br> As that which these distinguished spearmen, sons <br> Of Panthoüs, utter with their lips. And yet <br> The horseman Hyperenor did not long <br> Enjoy his youth when he with insolent words <br> Assailed me, and withstood me⁠—when he said <br> That I was the most craven wretch who bore <br> Arms in the Grecian host. He never turned, <br> I think, his footsteps homeward to delight <br> His reverend parents and beloved wife; <br> And I, like his, will take thy life, if thou <br> Oppose me. Heed my counsel, and withdraw <br> Among the crowd, and so avoid my stroke <br> Before thou come to harm. He is a fool <br> Who only sees the mischiefs that are past.”</p>
<p class="verse">He said: Euphorbus, heeding not his words <br> Of warning, spake again: “Now is my time, <br> Jove-nurtured Menelaus, to avenge <br> My brother, slain by thee, and over whom <br> Thou utteredst such swelling words, whose wife <br> In her new bridal chamber thou hast made <br> A widow, and upon her parents brought <br> Mourning and endless sorrow. It may make <br> The sorrow less, should I into the hands <br> Of Panthoüs and the noble Phrontis give <br> Thy head and armor. Let us now delay <br> The strife no longer: it will show with whom <br> The valor dwells, and who is moved by fear.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake, and smote his enemy’s round shield, <br> But pierced it not; the stubborn metal turned <br> The weapon’s point. Then Menelaus, son <br> Of Atreus, with a prayer to Jupiter, <br> Struck, as Euphorbus made a backward step, <br> His throat, and drave the weapon with strong hand <br> Through the soft neck. He fell with clashing arms. <br> His locks, which were like those the Graces wear, <br> And ringlets, bound with gold and silver bands, <br> Were drenched with blood. As when some husbandman <br> Rears in a lonely and well-watered spot <br> An olive-tree with widely spreading boughs, <br> Beautiful with fresh shoots, and putting forth <br> White blossoms, gently waved by every wind, <br> A sudden blast descends with mighty sweep <br> And tears it from its bed, and lays it prone <br> Upon the earth⁠—so lay Euphorbus, skilled <br> To wield the spear and son of Panthoüs, slain <br> And spoiled by Menelaus, Atreus’ son. <br> As when a lion of the mountain wilds, <br> Fearless and strong, bears from the browsing herd <br> The fairest of the kine, and breaks her neck <br> With his strong teeth, and, tearing her, devours <br> The bloody entrails, while a clamorous throng <br> Of dogs and herdsmen, with incessant cries, <br> Gather around him, yet approach him not, <br> Withheld by fear, so of the warriors round so <br> The gallant Menelaus none could find <br> The courage to encounter him; and then <br> Atrides easily had borne away <br> The sumptuous armor worn by Panthoüs’ son, <br> If envious Apollo had not moved <br> Hector to meet him. Putting on the form <br> Of Mentes, chief of the Ciconian band, <br> He said to him aloud, with wingèd words:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Hector, thou art pursuing what thy feet <br> Will never overtake, the steeds which draw <br> The chariot of Achilles. Hard it were <br> For mortal man to tame them or to guide, <br> Save for Achilles, goddess-born. Meanwhile <br> Hath warlike Menelaus, Atreus’ son, <br> Guarding the slain Patroclus, overthrown <br> Euphorbus, bravest of the Trojan host, <br> A son of Panthoüs; he will fight no more.”</p>
<p class="verse">Thus spake the god, and disappeared among <br> The warring squadrons. Bitter was the grief <br> That seized the heart of Hector as he looked <br> Along the ranks and saw the Greek bear off <br> The sumptuous arms, and saw the Trojan lie <br> Weltering in blood. At once he made his way <br> To the front rank, all armed in glittering brass, <br> And with loud shouts. As terrible he came <br> As Vulcan’s inextinguishable fires. <br> The son of Atreus heard that mighty shout, <br> And thus to his great soul lamenting said:⁠— <br> “If I abandon these rich spoils and leave <br> Patroclus, who has perished in my cause, <br> I fear the Greeks will look upon the act <br> With indignation. If, through dread of shame, <br> I fight alone with Hector and his men, <br> I fear to be o’erwhelmed by multitudes, <br> For crested Hector leads the whole array <br> Of Trojans hither. Yet why question thus? <br> For when a warrior ventures to assault <br> One whom a god protects, a bitter doom <br> Is his. Then none of all the Greeks should blame <br> If I give way to Hector, whom a god <br> Hath sent against me. Yet could I but hear <br> The voice of mighty Ajax, we would both <br> Return, and even against a god renew <br> The combat, that we haply might restore <br> Patroclus to Achilles, Peleus’ son. <br> Such in this choice of evils were the least.”</p>
<p class="verse">As thus he mused, the men of Troy came on, <br> With Hector at their head. The Greek gave way <br> And left the slain. As when a lion, driven <br> With pikes and clamor from the herdsman’s stalls <br> By men and dogs, unwillingly retreats, <br> His valiant heart still raging in his breast, <br> So did the fair-haired Menelaus leave <br> Patroclus. When he reached the Grecian ranks, <br> He turned and stood and looked about to find <br> The mighty Ajax, son of Telamon, <br> And him he soon beheld on the left edge <br> Of battle, rallying there and heartening <br> His men; for Phoebus from above had sent <br> A panic fear among them. To him then <br> The son of Atreus went in haste and said:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Ajax, my friend, come hither where we fight <br> Around Patroclus. Let us strive at least <br> To bring Achilles back the hero’s corpse, <br> Though stripped; for crested Hector hath his arms.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake; the courage of the warlike son <br> Of Telamon was kindled by his words. <br> To the front rank he hastened, and with him <br> Went fair-haired Menelaus. Hector there <br> Had spoiled Patroclus of his glorious arms, <br> And now was dragging him apart to hew <br> The head away with his keen sword, and give <br> The body to the dogs of Troy. Just then <br> Came Ajax, bearing, like a tower, his shield, <br> And Hector mingled with the Trojan ranks, <br> And leaped into his car; but first he gave <br> His friends the glittering spoil to bear away <br> To Troy⁠—a glory to the conqueror; <br> While Ajax, over Menoetiades <br> Holding his ample shield, stood firm as stands <br> A lion o’er his whelps, when, as he comes <br> Leading them through the wood, the hunters rush <br> Upon him, and his look is terrible <br> As his knit eyebrows cover his fierce eyes. <br> So Ajax moved around the hero’s corpse, <br> While warlike Menelaus by his side, <br> The son of Atreus, stood in bitter grief.</p>
<p class="verse">Then with a look of anger, Glaucus spake⁠— <br> Son of Hippolochus, and chief among <br> The Lycians⁠—thus to Hector: “Though thy form, <br> Hector, be noble, yet in prowess thou <br> Art wanting, and thy fame in feats of war <br> Is not deserved, since thou dost fly the foe. <br> Think whether thou alone, with others born <br> In Troy, canst save the city and the state. <br> For henceforth will no Lycian fight for Troy <br> Against the Greeks; this conflict without end <br> Has never earned them thanks. Inglorious chief! <br> How wilt thou be the shield of humbler men, <br> If thou canst leave Sarpedon, who has been <br> Thy comrade and thy guest, to be the prey <br> And spoil of the Greek warriors? While he lived, <br> Great was the aid he brought thy cause and thee, <br> And now thou dost not seek to drive away <br> The dogs from his neglected corpse. For this, <br> If any of the Lycians heed my words, <br> They will go home, and imminent will be <br> The ruin of thy city. If that firm <br> And resolute valor lived in Trojan hearts <br> Which they should cherish who in the defence <br> Of their own country bear the toils and face <br> The dangers of the field, we might this hour <br> Drag off the slain Patroclus into Troy. <br> And should we bear him from the thick of fight <br> To the great city of Priam, soon the Greeks <br> Would let us ransom the rich armor worn <br> By our Sarpedon, and bring back his corpse; <br> For he lies slain who was the bosom friend <br> Of the most valiant chieftain at the fleet <br> Of Greece and leader of her bravest men. <br> But thou, when great-souled Ajax fixed his eye <br> Upon thee, didst not venture to remain <br> And fight with him; he is more brave than thou.”</p>
<p class="verse">The crested Hector frowned and thus replied:⁠— <br> “Why, Glaucus, should a warrior such as thou <br> Utter such violent words? My friend, I deemed <br> That thou wert wise above all other men <br> Of fertile Lycia, but I now must blame <br> Thy judgment when thou say’st I shrink to meet <br> The mighty Ajax. I do neither dread <br> The battle’s fury nor the rush of steeds; <br> But all prevailing are the purposes <br> Of aegis-bearing Jove, who makes the brave <br> To flee, and takes from him the victory, <br> And then again impels him to the fight. <br> Come then, my friend, stand by me; see if I <br> Skulk this time from the conflict, as thou say’st, <br> Or tame the courage of whatever Greek, <br> The bravest, who defends Patroclus slain.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake, and, shouting, cheered the Trojans on: <br> “Trojans and Lycians and Dardanians, trained <br> To combat hand to hand, let it be seen, <br> My friends, that ye are men, and still retain <br> Your ancient valor; while I buckle on <br> The glorious armor of the illustrious son <br> Of Peleus, taken from Patroclus slain.”</p>
<p class="verse">So spake the crested Hector, and withdrew <br> From the fierce conflict, and with rapid steps <br> O’ertook his comrades as they bore away <br> Townward the glorious arms of Peleus’ son. <br> There from that deadly strife apart he stood, <br> And changed his coat of mail. He gave his own <br> To his companions, to be carried thence <br> To sacred Ilium, and he buckled on <br> The immortal armor of Achilles, son <br> Of Peleus, which the gods of heaven bestowed <br> Upon his father, who in his old age <br> Consigned them to Achilles; but the son <br> Was never in that armor to grow old.</p>
<p class="verse">And when the Cloud-compeller Jove beheld <br> Hector apart, accoutred in the arms <br> Of Peleus’ godlike son, he shook his head, <br> And to himself he said: “Unhappy man! <br> Death even now is near to thee, and yet <br> Is not in all thy thoughts. Thou puttest on <br> The heavenly armor of the terrible chief, <br> Before whom others tremble; thou hast slain <br> His friend, the brave and gentle, and hast stripped, <br> To do him shame, the armor from his limbs. <br> Yet will I for the moment give to thee <br> Fresh triumphs, since Andromache shall ne’er <br> Receive, when thou returnest from the field, <br> The armor of Pelides from thy hands.”</p>
<p class="verse">The son of Saturn spake, and gave the nod <br> With his dark brows. Well did that coat of mail <br> Suit Hector’s form. Meantime the god of war <br> In all his fierceness entered Hector’s breast: <br> Fresh vigor filled and nerved his frame; he went <br> Along the ranks of his renowned allies <br> With shouts; that glittering armor made him seem <br> The large-souled son of Peleus. To them all <br> He spake in turn, encouraging their hearts⁠— <br> To Mesthles, Glaucus, and Thersilochus, <br> Medon, Deisenor, and Hippothoüs, <br> Asteropaeus, Phorcys, Chromius, <br> And Ennomus the Augur; these the chief <br> Exhorted to the fight with wingèd words:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Hear me, ye mighty throng of our allies, <br> Dwellers of nations round us! Not to make <br> Our army vast in numbers did I send <br> To summon you, each from his native town, <br> But that your willing valor might defend <br> The wives and children of the sons of Troy <br> From the assailing Greeks. I therefore give <br> Most freely of our substance in large gifts <br> And banquets, that ye all may be content; <br> And now let some of you move boldly on <br> To do or die, which is the chance of war. <br> To him who from the field will drag and bring <br> The slain Patroclus to the Trojan knights, <br> Compelling Ajax to give way⁠—to him <br> I yield up half the spoil; the other half <br> I keep, and let his glory equal mine.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake, and all that mighty multitude <br> With lifted lances threw themselves against <br> The Grecian ranks. They hoped to bear away <br> The dead from Ajax, son of Telamon. <br> Ah, idle hope! that hero o’er the dead <br> Took many a Trojan’s life. Then Ajax thus <br> To Menelaus, great in battle, spake:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“O friend, O Menelaus, reared by Jove, <br> No longer now I hope our safe return <br> From battle. Not the greatest of my fears <br> Is for Patroclus, whom the dogs of Troy <br> And birds of prey full quickly will devour, <br> But for my life and thine. That cloud of war, <br> Hector, o’ershadows all, and over us <br> Impends the doom of death. Yet let us call <br> Our mighty men, if they perchance may hear.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake, and Menelaus, great in war, <br> Obeyed his wish and shouted to the Greeks:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“O friends, the princes and the chiefs of Greece, <br> Who at the public feasts with Atreus’ sons⁠— <br> King Agamemnon and his brother chief⁠— <br> Drink wine⁠—who each command a host, and hold <br> Your honors and your state from Jove⁠—my eyes <br> Cannot discern you in the thick of fight; <br> But some of you, who cannot bear to leave <br> Patroclus to the dogs of Troy, draw near!”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake; Oilean Ajax, swift of foot, <br> Heard and came forward, hastening through the fight; <br> And after him Idomeneus, who brought <br> Meriones, his armor-bearer, fierce <br> As the man-slayer Mars. But who could tell <br> The names of all the other Greeks that sprang <br> To mingle in the strife? The Trojans made <br> The first assault, and Hector led them on.</p>
<p class="verse">As at the mouth of some great river, swol’n <br> By rains from Jove, the mighty ocean-wave <br> Meets it with roaring, and the cliffs around <br> Rebellow, while the surges toss without, <br> With such a clamor came the Trojans on, <br> While round Patroclus closed, with one accord, <br> The Greeks, protected by their brazen shields, <br> And o’er their shining helmets Saturn’s son <br> Poured darkness. For when Menoetiades <br> Yet lived, attendant upon Peleus’ son, <br> Jove looked on him with no unkind regard, <br> And now he would not that his corse should feed <br> The enemy’s dogs, and therefore moved his friends <br> To rescue him. At first the Trojans drave <br> The dark-eyed Greeks before them; back they fell <br> And left the dead; yet, fiercely as they came, <br> The Trojans slew no man, but dragged away <br> The dead. A moment, and no more, the Greeks <br> Fell back; for Ajax quickly rallied them⁠— <br> Ajax, who, next to Peleus’ valiant son, <br> Excelled them all in form and feats of war; <br> He through the foremost warriors brake, as strong <br> As a wild bear that on the mountain’s side <br> Breaks through the shrubs, and scatters with a bound <br> A band of youths and dogs. The illustrious son <br> Of honored Telamon thus put to rout <br> The Trojan phalanxes environing <br> Patroclus, in the hope to bear him thence <br> Townward with glory. There Hippothoüs, son <br> Of Lethus the Pelasgian, having bound <br> A thong about the sinewy ankle, toiled <br> To drag away the slain man by the foot <br> From that fierce strife⁠—a grateful spectacle <br> To Hector and the Trojans. Yet on him <br> A vengeance which no friendly arm could ward <br> Fell suddenly. The son of Telamon <br> Rushed through the crowd, and in close combat smote <br> His helmet’s brazen cheek. That plumèd helm <br> Was cleft by the huge spear and vigorous hand, <br> And where the weapon struck Hippothoüs, <br> Mingled with blood the brain gushed forth; the life <br> Forsook his limbs; he dropped from nerveless hands <br> The foot of brave Patroclus, and beside <br> The corpse fell headlong⁠—far from the rich fields <br> Of his Larissa, never to repay <br> With gentle cares in their old age the love <br> Of his dear parents; for his life was short, <br> Slain by the spear of Ajax, large of soul.</p>
<p class="verse">Then Hector aimed again his shining spear <br> At Ajax, who perceived it as it came, <br> And just avoided it. The weapon struck <br> Schedius, the valiant son of Iphitus, <br> And bravest of the Phocians, whose abode <br> Was Panopeus the famous, where he ruled <br> O’er many men. Beneath the collar-bone <br> It pierced him, and passed through; the brazen point <br> Came out upon the shoulder; to the ground <br> He fell, his armor clashing with his fall. <br> Then Ajax smote the valiant Phorcys, son <br> Of Phoenops, in the navel. Through the mail <br> The brazen weapon broke, and roughly tore <br> The entrails. In the dust he fell, and clenched <br> The earth with dying hands. The foremost ranks, <br> Led by illustrious Hector, at the sight <br> Yielded the ground; the Greeks with fearful shouts <br> Dragged off the bodies of Hippothoüs <br> And Phorcys, and despoiled them of their arms.</p>
<p class="verse">Then would the Trojans have been put to flight <br> Before the warlike Greeks, and, craven like, <br> Gone up to Troy, and great had been the fame <br> Gained by the might and courage of the Greeks, <br> Beyond what Jupiter designed to give, <br> Had not Apollo brought Aeneas forth <br> By putting on the form of Periphas, <br> The herald and the son of Epytus, <br> Who in that office as a prudent friend <br> And counsellor had served, till he grew old, <br> The father of Aeneas. In his shape <br> Thus spake Apollo, son of Jupiter:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Aeneas, ye might even hold the towers <br> Of lofty Ilium safe against a god, <br> Were ye to act as some whom I have seen⁠— <br> Valiant, and confident in their own might <br> And multitude of dauntless followers. <br> And now Jove favors us and offers us <br> The victory o’er the Greeks, and yet ye flee <br> In abject terror, and refuse to fight.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake; Aeneas, looking at him, knew <br> The archer-god, and with a mighty voice <br> Called out to Hector: “Hector! Thou and all <br> Who lead the troops of Troy, and our allies, <br> Great shame it were if we were put to rout <br> Before the warlike Greeks, and beaten back <br> To Troy like cowards. Standing by my side, <br> One of the gods already hath declared <br> That Jupiter, All-wise, is our ally <br> In battle. Let us therefore boldly fall <br> Upon the Greeks, nor suffer them to bear <br> Patroclus unmolested to their fleet.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake, and, springing to the foremost ranks <br> Stood firm; the Trojans also turned and faced <br> The Achaians. Then Aeneas with his spear <br> Struck down Leocritus, the gallant friend <br> Of Lycomedes and Arisbas’ son. <br> The warlike Lycomedes saw his fall <br> With grief, and came and cast his shining spear <br> At Apisaon, son of Hippasus, <br> A shepherd of the people. Underneath <br> The midriff, through the liver went the spear, <br> And he fell lifeless. He had come to Troy <br> From rich Pseonia, and was great in war, <br> Next to Asteropaeus. As he saw <br> His comrade fall, Asteropaeus, moved <br> By grief, advanced to combat with the Greeks, <br> But could not; for the group that stood around <br> Patroclus showed a fence of shields, and held <br> Their spears before them. Ajax moved among <br> The warriors, charging them that none should leave <br> The corpse, and none should step beyond the rest <br> To strike the foe, but stay to guard the dead, <br> And combat hand to hand. Such was the charge <br> Of mighty Ajax. All the earth around <br> Was steeped with blood, and many a corpse was heaped <br> On corpse of Trojans and their brave allies, <br> And of the Greeks, for even on their side <br> The strife was not unbloody, though of Greeks <br> There perished fewer; each was on the watch <br> To ward the battle’s dangers from the rest.</p>
<p class="verse">Then did they fight like fire. You could not say <br> The sun was safe, nor yet the moon, so thick <br> A darkness gathered over the brave men <br> Around the corpse of Menoetiades. <br> The other Trojans and the well-armed Greeks <br> Fought freely under the clear sky; the sun <br> Shed o’er them his full brightness; not a cloud <br> Shadowed the earth, or rested on the hills. <br> From time to time they paused, and warily <br> They shunned each other’s cruel darts, and kept <br> Far from each other, while in the mid-war <br> Struggled the combatants in darkness, galled <br> By the remorseless weapons of their foes. <br> Yet Thrasymedes and Antilochus, <br> Two famous Grecian warriors, had not learned <br> That excellent Patroclus was no more, <br> But thought that, still alive, he led the war <br> Against the Trojans, fighting in the van. <br> They watched the flight and slaughter of the Greeks, <br> And fought apart, for Nestor so enjoined, <br> Who sent them to the battle from the fleet.</p>
<p class="verse">But they who held the middle space around <br> The friend of swift Aeacides, maintained <br> A desperate strife all day; the knees, the thighs, <br> The feet, the hands, the eyes of those who fought <br> Were faint with weariness and foul with sweat. <br> As when an ample ox-hide, steeped in fat, <br> Is given to workmen to be stretched, they stand <br> Around it in a circle, pulling it, <br> Till forth the moisture issues, and the oil <br> Enters the skin, and by that constant strain <br> From many hands the hide is duly stretched, <br> So in small space the warriors drew the dead <br> Hither and thither; they of Ilium strove <br> To drag it to the city, they of Greece <br> To bear it to the fleet. The tumult then <br> Was terrible, and neither Mars himself, <br> The musterer of hosts, nor Pallas, roused <br> To her intensest wrath, had they been near <br> The struggle, would have seen it with disdain. <br> Such deadly strife of steeds and men was held <br> O’er slain Patroclus by the will of Jove.</p>
<p class="verse">The great Achilles knew not yet the fate <br> Of his Patroclus, for the warriors fought <br> Far from the fleet, beside the wall of Troy. <br> He never thought of him as one whose death <br> Was near, but trusted that, when once he reached <br> The Trojan wall, he would return alive; <br> Nor ever deemed he that without his aid, <br> Or even with it, would Patroclus sack <br> The city. This was what he oft had heard <br> From Thetis, who disclosed to him apart <br> The counsel of Almighty Jupiter. <br> Yet had his mother never once revealed <br> The present evil⁠—that the one whom most <br> He loved of all his friends should perish thus.</p>
<p class="verse">Still round the dead they fought with their keen spears, <br> And slew each other. Then of the mailed Greeks <br> Someone would say: “O friends, it were disgrace <br> Should we fall back upon our roomy ships. <br> First let the dark earth swallow us; for this <br> Were better than to let the Trojan knights <br> Drag off the dead in triumph to their town.”</p>
<p class="verse">And some among the large-souled sons of Troy <br> Would say: “O friends, though all of us should fall <br> Beside this corpse, let no one turn and flee.” <br> Thus they, encouraging each other, spake, <br> And thus the fight went on. The iron din <br> Rose through the waste air to the brazen heaven.</p>
<p class="verse">Meantime aloof from battle stood the steeds <br> Of Peleus’ son, and sorrowed when they knew <br> That he who guided them lay stretched in dust <br> By Hector’s slaughtering hand. Automedon, <br> The brave son of Diores, often tried <br> The lash, and gentle words as oft, and oft <br> Shouted forth threats; yet neither would they move <br> Toward the broad Hellespont, where lay the fleet, <br> Nor toward the Greeks in combat, but remained <br> Motionless as a funeral column, reared <br> To mark a man’s or woman’s tomb. So stood <br> The coursers yoked to that magnificent car, <br> With drooping heads, and tears that from their lids <br> Flowed hot, for sorrow at the loss of him <br> Who was their charioteer, and their fair manes, <br> Sweeping the yoke below, were foul with dust. <br> The son of Saturn saw their grief, and shook <br> His head in pity, saying to himself:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Why did the gods bestow you, luckless pair, <br> On Peleus⁠—on a king of mortal birth⁠— <br> You who shall never feel old age or death? <br> Was it that ye might share with human-kind <br> Their sorrows? for the race of mortal men <br> Of all that breathe and move upon the earth <br> Is the most wretched. Yet of this be sure⁠— <br> That ye shall never in that sumptuous car <br> Bear Hector. Is it not enough that he <br> Should wear that armor, uttering idle boasts? <br> And now will I infuse into your limbs <br> Spirit and strength, that ye may safely bear <br> Automedon across the battle-field <br> To where the roomy galleys lie. I yet <br> Must give more glory to the men of Troy, <br> And they must slay until they come again <br> To the good ships of Greece⁠—until the sun <br> Goes down and sacred darkness covers all.”</p>
<p class="verse">So spake the god, and breathed into the steeds <br> New life and vigor. From their manes they shook <br> The dust, and flew with that swift car among <br> The Greeks and Trojans. With the Trojan throng, <br> Automedon, though mourning his slain friend, <br> Maintained the fight; he rushed upon their ranks, <br> A vulture pouncing on a flock of geese. <br> Swiftly he passed from out the Trojan throng; <br> Swiftly again he charged their phalanxes <br> In fierce pursuit. Yet slew he none of those <br> Whom he pursued; he could not guide at once <br> The steeds and cast the spear, when seated thus <br> Alone within that sacred car. At last <br> A friend, the valorous Alcimedon, <br> Laërces’ son, of Aemon’s line, beheld <br> His plight, and, standing near his chariot, said:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“What god, Automedon, hath prompted thee <br> To these mad acts, and stolen thy better sense, <br> Fighting alone among the foremost ranks <br> Of Trojan warriors, thy companion slain, <br> And Hector in the field, who boastfully <br> Stalks in the armor of Aeacides?”</p>
<p class="verse">And thus Automedon, Diores’ son, <br> Made answer: “Who is there among the Greeks <br> Able like thee, Alcimedon, to rein <br> And curb the spirit of immortal steeds? <br> None were there save Patroclus while he lived, <br> Wise as a god in council. Death and fate <br> Now hold him. To thy hand I give the lash <br> And shining reins, while I descend and fight.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake, and into his swift chariot sprang <br> Alcimedon, and took the lash and reins. <br> Automedon leaped down. As Hector saw, <br> He thus bespake Aeneas at his side:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Aeneas, leader of the men of Troy, <br> Equipped in brazen armor, I have seen <br> Those coursers of the swift Aeacides <br> Driven through the battle by unwarlike hands, <br> And ’tis my hope, if thou wilt give thine aid, <br> To seize them. They who guide them will not dare <br> To stand and face us when we make the charge.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake; Anchises’ valiant son complied, <br> And, sheltered by their shields of tough ox-hide, <br> Well dried and firm, and strong with plates of brass, <br> The twain went forward. With them at their side <br> Went Chromius and Aretus, nobly formed, <br> In hope to lead away the high-necked steeds, <br> Their guardians slain. Vain dreamers! They were doomed <br> Not without bloody penance to return <br> From that encounter with Automedon, <br> Who prayed to Father Jove, and whose faint heart <br> Was strengthened and made bold. And thus the chief <br> Said to his faithful friend Alcimedon:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Keep not the steeds thou guidest far from me, <br> Alcimedon, but let them ever breathe <br> Upon my shoulders. Hector, Priam’s son, <br> I think, will not give over this assault <br> Before he either slays us, and ascends <br> The car to which these steeds with flowing manes <br> Are yoked, and puts to flight the phalanxes <br> Of Argive warriors, or himself is slain.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake, and called to both the Ajaxes <br> And Menelaus: “Ye who lead the Greeks,” <br> He said, and named the chieftains, “give in charge <br> The dead to your best warriors, to surround <br> And guard the corpse, and drive away the foe; <br> But hasten to avert the evil day <br> From us who are alive. For even now <br> Hector comes rushing through the deadly fight, <br> And brings Aeneas; these are the most brave <br> Of all the Trojan army. On the knees <br> Of the great gods the issue rests. I too <br> Will cast the spear, and leave the rest to Jove.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake, and lifting his huge spear he smote <br> The round shield of Aretus. There the blade <br> Stopped not, but, entering, pierced him through the belt. <br> As, when a vigorous youth with a keen axe <br> Strikes a wild bull behind the horns, and there <br> Severs the sinews, forward leaps the beast <br> And falls⁠—Aretus, springing forward thus, <br> Fell headlong. In the Trojan’s entrails still <br> Quivered the spear, and life forsook his limbs.</p>
<p class="verse">Then Hector aimed, to smite Automedon, <br> His shining spear. The Greek beheld and stooped, <br> And shunned the brazen weapon. Down it came, <br> And plunged into the earth, and stood, its stem <br> Still shaken with the blow, and spent its force. <br> Now would the twain have turned, and hand to hand <br> Fought with their swords, when suddenly came up <br> The warriors Ajax, hastening, at the call <br> Of their companion, through the crowd, and stayed <br> The combat. Hector and Aeneas then, <br> And Chromius, of the godlike form, withdrew <br> Through caution, leaving on the battle-field <br> Aretus lying mangled. The fierce chief <br> Automedon despoiled the dead, and spake <br> Boastfully: “Somewhat lighter on my heart <br> Lies now my grief for Menoetiades, <br> Though I have slain a man of meaner note.”</p>
<p class="verse">As thus he spake, he threw the bloody spoils <br> Into his chariot, mounting to the seat, <br> His feet and hands all crimson with the blood, <br> As when a lion has devoured an ox. <br> Then round Patroclus raged the strife again, <br> Murderous and sad to see; for Pallas there <br> Inflamed the strife, sent down from heaven by Jove, <br> To rouse the courage of the Greeks, since such <br> Was now his will. As when the god displays <br> To men a purple rainbow in the skies, <br> A sign of war or of a bitter storm, <br> Which drives the laborer from his task, and makes <br> The cattle droop, so, in a purple cloud <br> Concealed, she went among the Greeks, and filled <br> Their hearts with valor. Taking first the form <br> Of Phoenix, and his clear, unwearied voice, <br> She spake in stirring words to Atreus’ son, <br> The gallant Menelaus, standing near: <br> “Shame and dishonor will it be to thee, <br> O Menelaus, if, beneath the walls <br> Of Troy, the hungry dogs should tear the corpse <br> Of him who was in life the faithful friend <br> Of great Achilles. Fight thou therefore on <br> Bravely, and bid the other Greeks be brave.”</p>
<p class="verse">And Menelaus, great in war, rejoined: <br> “O Phoenix, aged father, who wert born <br> In days long past, would but Minerva give <br> The needed strength, and ward from me the stroke <br> Of weapons, then would I stand by and guard <br> Patroclus, for his death hath filled my heart <br> With grief. But Hector’s rage is like the rage <br> Of fire; he ceases not to slay; for Jove <br> Gives to his spear the glory of the day.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake, and well was blue-eyed Pallas pleased <br> That first to her of all the deities <br> He prayed; and therefore did she nerve his chest <br> And knees with strength, and put into his heart <br> The daring of the fly, that, often driven <br> From man, returns and bites, and finds how sweet <br> Is human blood. Such resolute zeal she woke <br> In his stern soul, as quickly he approached <br> Patroclus, and sent forth his shining spear. <br> Among the Trojans was Eëtion’s son, <br> Podes, the rich and brave, whom Hector held <br> In highest honor, choosing him to be <br> Companion of his feasts. Him in the waist <br> The fair-haired Menelaus, as he fled, <br> Smote, driving home the weapon. With a clash <br> He fell to earth, and Menelaus drew <br> The slain away among the Grecian ranks. <br> Then came Apollo, putting on the form <br> Of Phaenops, son of Asius, whose abode <br> Was in Abydos, and whom Hector most <br> Esteemed of all his guests. The archer-god <br> Drew near to Hector, and bespake him thus:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Hector, what other Greek will fear thee now, <br> Since thou dost shrink from Menelaus, deemed <br> Effeminate in war? Behold, he drags <br> Away a warrior from thy host; his hand <br> Hath slain thy faithful friend, Eëtion’s son, <br> Brave Podes, fighting in the foremost ranks.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake: a cloud of sorrow overspread <br> The soul of Hector. Armed in glittering brass, <br> He went among the warriors in the van. <br> Then did the son of Saturn lift on high <br> His fringèd aegis, gleaming; with a cloud <br> He covered Ida, sent his lightnings down, <br> And thundered terribly, and made the mount <br> Shake to its base, and gave the victory <br> To Troy, and put to rout the Grecian host.</p>
<p class="verse">Peneleus of Bueotia led the fight. <br> A spear that lighted on the shoulder-tip, <br> As he came forward, wounded him. The blade, <br> Hurled by Polydamas in close assault, <br> Entered and grazed the bone. Then Hector pierced <br> The wrist of Leitus, Alectryon’s son, <br> And made him leave the combat. As he fled <br> He looked around in fear, nor hoped again <br> To wield the spear against the men of Troy. <br> As Hector followed Leitus, he met <br> The long spear of Idomeneus, which struck <br> His corselet near the pap; the weapon broke <br> Sheer at the socket, and the Trojans raised <br> A shout, while Hector at Idomeneus <br> Let fly his spear. It missed the chief, but smote <br> Coeranus, who from pleasant Lyctus came, <br> The friend and follower of Meriones. <br> For on that day Idomeneus had come <br> From his good ships on foot, and great had been <br> The triumph of the Trojans at his fall, <br> If Coeranus had not with his swift steeds <br> Passed near and bid him mount. ’Twas thus he came <br> To save Idomeneus from death, and yield <br> To the man-queller Hector his own life; <br> The javelin entered underneath the ear, <br> By the jaw-bone, where, forcing out the teeth, <br> It cleft the tongue in twain. He fell to earth, <br> And dropped the reins. Meriones stooped down <br> And took them from the dust in his own hands, <br> And thus bespake Idomeneus: “Ply well <br> The lash, until thy coursers reach the fleet, <br> For thou mayst clearly see that victory <br> Today is not upon the Grecian side.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake: Idomeneus, fear-smitten, lashed <br> The long-maned steeds that hurried toward the fleet. <br> Nor now did Menelaus nor his friend, <br> The valiant Ajax, fail to see that Jove <br> Had changed the vantage to the side of Troy. <br> And thus the son of Telamon began:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Alas! The feeblest mind can now perceive <br> That Father Jove is with the sons of Troy, <br> And gives to them the glory of the day. <br> Their weapons smite, whoever sends them forth, <br> Coward or brave, for Jove directs them all; <br> Ours fall to earth in vain. But let us now <br> Consult how best to bear the corpse away, <br> And how, returning, we may meet our friends <br> With joy; for they are grieved as they behold <br> Our plight, and fear that we may not withstand <br> The fiery onset and invincible arm <br> Of the man-queller Hector. Would there were <br> Some comrade who would bear to Peleus’ son <br> The tidings of the day! For he, I think, <br> Has not yet heard that his dear friend is slain. <br> None such can I behold of all the Greeks, <br> For they are shrouded all⁠—their steeds and they⁠— <br> In darkness. Father Jove, deliver us <br> From darkness; clear the heavens and give our eyes <br> Again to see. Destroy us if thou wilt, <br> But O destroy us in the light of day!”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake: the All-Father saw him shedding tears, <br> And pitied him, and bade the shadows flee, <br> And swept away the cloud. The sun looked forth, <br> And all the battle lay in light. Then thus <br> To warlike Menelaus Ajax said:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“O Menelaus, foster-child of Jove, <br> Look round and see if yet Antilochus, <br> The large-souled son of Nestor, is alive, <br> And bid him bear the tidings in all haste <br> To the great son of Peleus, that the one <br> Of all his friends whom most he loved is slain.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake, and Menelaus, great in war, <br> Complied, and hastened forth, as from a fold <br> A lion stalks away, that long has kept <br> In fear the hounds and herdsmen, who all night <br> Have watched to drive him from their well-fed beeves, <br> While, eager for his prey, he rushes oft <br> Against them, but in vain, for many a spear <br> Is hurled at him, and many a blazing brand, <br> Which, fierce for ravin as he is, he dreads, <br> Till sullenly at early morn he goes. <br> So from Patroclus went unwillingly <br> The valiant Menelaus, for he feared <br> Lest, panic-struck, the Greeks should leave his corpse <br> The enemy’s prey. Thus earnestly he prayed <br> The warriors Ajax and Meriones:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Ye warriors Ajax, leaders of the Greeks! <br> And thou, Meriones! Let each of you <br> Bear well in mind how kindly was the mood <br> Of poor Patroclus; gentle in his life <br> Was he to all, and now is with the dead.” <br> The fair-haired Menelaus, speaking thus, <br> Withdrew. He looked around him as he went, <br> As looks an eagle, bird of sharpest sight⁠— <br> So men declare⁠—of all the fowls of air, <br> From which, though high in heaven, the nimble hare <br> Beneath the thicket is not hid; he stoops, <br> And takes the creature’s life. Thy piercing eyes, <br> O Menelaus, thus on every side <br> Were turned, in eager scrutiny, to find <br> Among the multitude of Greeks the son <br> Of Nestor living. Him he soon descried <br> Upon the battle’s left, where manfully <br> He cheered his fellows on. The fair-haired son <br> Of Atreus came and stood by him, and said:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Stay, foster-child of Jove, Antilochus! <br> And listen to the sorrowful news I bring <br> Of what should ne’er have been. Thou must have well <br> Perceived, I think, that some divinity <br> Doth heap disaster on our host, and give <br> The victory to the Trojans. He is dead⁠— <br> Patroclus⁠—the most valiant of the Greeks, <br> And great their sorrow is. Now hasten thou <br> To the Greek galleys; let Achilles know <br> The tidings; he may haply bring the corpse, <br> Stripped as it is, unmangled to the fleet, <br> For crested Hector has the arms he wore.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake, and at his words Antilochus <br> Was horror-struck; in grief too great for speech, <br> Tears filled his eyes, and his clear voice was choked. <br> Yet heeded he the mandate. Laying off <br> His arms, he gave them to his blameless friend, <br> Laodocus, who with his firm-paced steeds <br> Came toward him. Thus prepared he ran; his feet <br> Carried him swiftly from the battle-field <br> To bear the evil news to Peleus’ son.</p>
<p class="verse">Yet Menelaus, foster-child of Jove, <br> Thy spirit did not prompt thee to remain <br> And aid thy hard-pressed comrades at the spot <br> Whence thou didst send Antilochus, and where <br> The Pyleans longed to keep him. Yet he sent <br> The noble Thrasymedes to their aid, <br> While he returned to where Patroclus lay, <br> And stood beside the warriors there, and said:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“I sent to swift Achilles at the fleet <br> A messenger, yet think he will not come. <br> Though royal Hector’s deed hath roused his rage, <br> Unarmed he cannot meet the sons of Troy. <br> Consult we then how we may best convey <br> The body to the ships, and how ourselves <br> Escape the doom of death by Trojan hands.”</p>
<p class="verse">The mighty Ajax, son of Telamon, <br> Replied: “O Menelaus far-renowned, <br> Well hast thou spoken. Lift thou now the corse, <br> Thou and Meriones, and place yourselves <br> Beneath it, and convey it from the field. <br> We, following you, will combat with the sons <br> Of Troy and noble Hector⁠—we who, named <br> Alike and one in spirit, oft have borne <br> The fury of the battle side by side.”</p>
<p class="verse">He ended, and the warriors in their arms <br> Raised with main strength the body from the ground. <br> The Trojans, as they saw it borne away, <br> Shouted behind them, rushing on like hounds <br> That spring upon a wounded forest-boar <br> Before the hunter-youths now pressing close <br> Upon his flank, to tear him, then again, <br> Whene’er he turns upon them in his strength, <br> Retreating in dismay, and put to flight <br> Hither and thither. Thus, in hot pursuit <br> And close array, the Trojans following strook <br> With swords and two-edged spears; but when the twain <br> Turned and stood firm to meet them, every cheek <br> Grew pale, and not a single Trojan dared <br> Draw near the Greeks to combat for the corse.</p>
<p class="verse">Thus rapidly they bore away the dead <br> Toward their good galleys from the battle-field. <br> Onward with them the furious battle swept, <br> As spreads a fire that, kindled suddenly, <br> Seizes a city, and the dwellings sink <br> In the consuming blaze, and a strong wind <br> Roars through the flame. Such fearful din of steeds <br> And warriors followed the retreating Greeks. <br> As from a mountain summit strong-backed mules <br> Drag over the rough ways a ponderous beam <br> Or mast, till weary with the mighty strain <br> And streaming sweat, so they with resolute toil <br> Bore off the dead. Behind them as they went <br> Their two defenders kept the foe aloof. <br> As when a river-dike o’ergrown with trees <br> Crosses a plain, and holds the violent course <br> Of the swol’n stream in check, and, driving back <br> The waters, spreads them o’er the level fields, <br> Nor can their fury force a passage through⁠— <br> So did the warriors Ajax hold in check <br> The Trojans; yet they followed close, and two <br> More closely than the rest⁠—Aeneas, son <br> Of old Anchises, and the illustrious chief, <br> Hector. As when a company of daws <br> Or starlings, startled at a hawk’s approach, <br> The murderous enemy of the smaller birds, <br> Take wing with piercing cries, so, driven before <br> The might of Hector and Aeneas, fled <br> The Greeks with clamorous cries, and thought no more <br> Of combat. In the trench and near it lay <br> Many fair weapons, which the fugitive Greeks <br> Had dropped in haste, and still the war went on.</p>
