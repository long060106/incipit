<p class="lede">Agamemnon’s Proposal to raise the siege opposed by Diomed and Nestor⁠—A council⁠—Ulysses, Ajax, and Phoenix sent to Achilles to request a reconciliation⁠—Their reception, their persuasions, and their ill success.</p>
<p class="verse">The Trojans thus kept watch; while through the night <br> The power of Flight, companion of cold Fear, <br> Wrought on the Greeks, and all their bravest men <br> Were bowed beneath a sorrow hard to bear. <br> As when two winds upturn the fishy deep⁠— <br> The north wind and the west, that suddenly <br> Blow from the Thracian coast; the black waves rise <br> At once, and fling the sea-weed to the shore⁠— <br> Thus were the Achaians troubled in their hearts.</p>
<p class="verse">Atrides, deeply grieving, walked the camp, <br> And bade the clear-voiced heralds call by name <br> To council all the chiefs, but not aloud. <br> The king himself among the foremost gave <br> The summons. Sadly that assembly took <br> Their seats; and Agamemnon in the midst <br> Rose, shedding tears⁠—as down a lofty rock, <br> Darkening its face, a fountain’s waters flow⁠— <br> And, deeply sighing, thus addressed the Greeks:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“O friends! the chiefs and princes of the Greeks! <br> Saturnian Jove hath in an evil snare <br> Most cruelly entangled me. He gave <br> His promise once that I should overthrow <br> This strong-walled Ilium, and return; but now <br> He meditates a fraud, and sends me back <br> To Argos without glory, and with loss <br> Of many warriors. Thus doth it seem good <br> Doubtless to Jove Almighty, who hath cast <br> The towers of many a city down to earth, <br> And will cast others down⁠—his might excels <br> All other might. But let us now obey, <br> As I shall counsel you, and in our ships <br> Haste to our own dear country; for I see <br> That Troy with its broad streets can ne’er be ours.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake; and all were silent. Silent long <br> Remained the sorrow-stricken sons of Greece, <br> Till Diomed, the brave in battle, spake:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“First of the chiefs I speak, to disapprove, <br> Atrides, thy rash purpose: ’tis my right <br> In council; nor, O king, be thou displeased. <br> Thou first among the Greeks hast taunted me <br> With lack of valor, calling me unapt <br> For war and weak of arm. The young and old <br> Have heard the taunt. One of two gifts the son <br> Of wily Saturn hath bestowed on thee: <br> High rank and rule o’er all the rest he gave, <br> But gave thee not the nobler quality <br> Of fortitude. Dost thou then truly deem <br> The Greeks unapt for war and weak of arm, <br> As thou hast said? Thou longest to return: <br> Go, then; the way is open; by the sea <br> The barques that brought thee from Mycenae lie, <br> A numerous fleet. Yet others will remain⁠— <br> Long haired Achaians⁠—till we overthrow <br> The city. Should they also pine for home, <br> Then let them flee, with all their ships; while I <br> With Sthenelus fight on until we make <br> An end of Troy⁠—for with the gods we came.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake. The Greeks applauded; all admired <br> The words of the horse-tamer Diomed. <br> Nestor the knight then rose, and thus he spake:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“O son of Tydeus, eminently brave <br> Art thou among thy comrades in the field, <br> And great in council. No one here condemns <br> The sentence thou hast given; among the Greeks <br> Is no one who denies what thou hast said; <br> Yet hast thou not said all. Thy years are few⁠— <br> So few, thou mightest be my youngest son; <br> And yet thou speakest wisely to the kings <br> Of Greece, and thy discourse is just and right. <br> Now I, who boast of far more years than thou, <br> Will speak of this that yet remains, and none⁠— <br> Not even Agamemnon⁠—will gainsay <br> What I advise. A wretch without a tie <br> Of kin, a lawless man without a home, <br> Is he who takes delight in civil strifes. <br> But let us now give way to the dark night, <br> And make our banquets ready. Let the guards <br> Lie down within the trenches which we digged <br> Without the wall: be this the young men’s charge. <br> And thou, Atrides, do thou now begin, <br> Who art supreme, and make a feast for all <br> The elder chiefs; it shall become thee well: <br> Thy tents are full of wine, which ships from Thrace <br> Bring every day across the mighty deep, <br> And thou hast all things ready, and a host <br> Of menials. Then, when many throng the board, <br> Thou shalt defer to him who counsels thee <br> Most wisely; for the Greeks have urgent need <br> Of prudent counsels, when the foe so close <br> Beside our galleys lights his multitude <br> Of watch-fires. Who that sees them can rejoice? <br> This night will rescue or destroy our host.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake. They listened all, and willingly <br> Obeyed him. Forth in armor went the guards, <br> Led by the chieftain Thrasymedes, son <br> Of Nestor, by Ascalaphus, who claimed <br> His birth from Mars, and by Ialmenus <br> His brother, and Deipyrus, with whom <br> There followed Aphareus, Meriones, <br> And Lycomedes, Creon’s noble son. <br> Seven were the leaders of the guards; with each <br> A hundred youths in warlike order marched, <br> Bearing long spears; and when they reached the space <br> Between the trench and wall they sat them down, <br> And kindled fires and made their evening meal.</p>
<p class="verse">Atrides brought the assembled elder chiefs <br> To his pavilion, and before them set <br> A generous banquet. They put forth their hands <br> And shared the feast; and when the calls of thirst <br> And hunger ceased, the aged Nestor first <br> Began to counsel them; the chief, whose words <br> Had lately seemed of wisest import, now <br> Addressed the assembly with well-ordered speech:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Atrides Agamemnon, glorious king! <br> What I shall say begins and ends with thee, <br> For thou dost rule o’er many nations. Jove <br> Hath given to thee the sceptre, and the power <br> To make their laws, that thou mayst seek their good. <br> Thou, therefore, of all men, shouldst speak and hear <br> In council, and shouldst follow willingly <br> Another’s judgment when it best promotes <br> The general weal; for all depends on thee. <br> Now let me say what seems to me most wise; <br> For better counsel none can give than this <br> Which now I meditate, and which to give <br> I purposed from the hour when thou, great king, <br> Didst bear the maid Briseis from the tent <br> Of the enraged Achilles, unapproved <br> By me, who strove to change thy rash design. <br> Then didst thou yield thee to thy haughty will, <br> And didst dishonor a most valiant man, <br> Whom the immortals honor. Thou didst take <br> And still dost keep the prize he fairly won. <br> Let it be now our study to appease <br> The hero with large gifts and soothing words.”</p>
<p class="verse">Then Agamemnon, king of men, replied:⁠— <br> “O ancient man, most truly hast thou named <br> My faults. I erred, and I deny it not. <br> That man indeed is equal to a host <br> Whom Jupiter doth love and honor thus, <br> Humbling the Achaian people for his sake. <br> And now, since, yielding to my wayward mood <br> I erred, let me appease him, if I may, <br> With gifts of priceless worth. Before you all <br> I number them⁠—seven tripods which the fire <br> Hath never touched, six talents of pure gold, <br> And twenty shining cauldrons, and twelve steeds <br> Of hardy frame, victorious in the race, <br> Whose feet have won me prizes in the games. <br> No beggar would he be, nor yet with store <br> Of gold unfurnished, in whose coffers lay <br> The prizes those swift steeds have brought to me. <br> Seven faultless women, skilled in household arts, <br> I give moreover⁠—Lesbians, whom I chose <br> When he o’erran the populous Lesbian isle⁠— <br> Damsels in beauty who excel their sex. <br> These I bestow, and with them I will send <br> Her whom I took away⁠—Briseis, pure⁠— <br> I swear it with a mighty oath⁠—as pure <br> As when she left his tent. All these I give <br> At once; and if by favor of the gods <br> We lay the mighty city of Priam waste, <br> He shall load down his galley with large store <br> Of gold and silver, entering first when we, <br> The Greeks, divide the spoil. Then may he choose <br> Twice ten young Trojan women, beautiful <br> Beyond their sex save Helen. If we come <br> Safe to Achaian Argos, richly stocked <br> With milky kine, he may become to me <br> A son-in-law, and cherished equally <br> With my sole son Orestes, who is reared <br> Most royally. Three daughters there, within <br> My stately palace-walls⁠—Chrysothemis, <br> Laodice, and Iphianassa⁠—dwell, <br> And he may choose among them, and may lead <br> Home to the house of Peleus her who best <br> Deserves his love. Nor need he to endow <br> The bride, for I will give an ampler dower <br> Than ever father to his daughter gave⁠— <br> Seven cities with thronged streets⁠—Cardamyle, <br> Enope, grassy Hira, Pherae famed <br> Afar, Antheia with rich pasture-fields, <br> Aepeia beautiful, and Pedasus <br> With all its vineyards; all are near the sea, <br> And stand the last before you reach the coast <br> Of sandy Pylos. Rich in flocks and herds <br> Their dwellers are, and they will honor him <br> As if he were a god, and, ruled by him, <br> Will pay large tribute. These will I bestow, <br> Let but his anger cool and his resolve <br> Give way. ’Tis Pluto who is deaf to prayer <br> And ne’er relents, and he, of all the gods, <br> Most hateful is to men. Now let the son <br> Of Peleus yield at length to me, who stand <br> Above him in authority and years.”</p>
<p class="verse">Then answered Nestor the Gerenian knight:⁠— <br> “Atrides Agamemnon! Glorious king! <br> Gifts not to be contemned thou offerest <br> To Prince Achilles. Let us now despatch <br> A chosen embassy, who shall proceed <br> At once to where Pelides holds his tent. <br> I name the men; and cheerfully will they <br> Perform the duty: Phoenix, dear to Jove, <br> Shall be their leader, mighty Ajax next, <br> And then high-born Ulysses; heralds twain <br> Shall follow⁠—Hodius and Eurybates. <br> And now be water brought to cleanse our hands, <br> And charge be given that no ill-omened word <br> Be uttered, while we pray that Jupiter, <br> The son of Saturn, will assist our need.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake; and all approved the words he said. <br> Then poured the heralds water on the hands <br> Of those who sat. The young men crowned with wine <br> The goblets, and in seemly order passed <br> The brimming cups, distributing to each. <br> Part to the gods they poured, and next they drank <br> As each might choose, and then the embassy <br> Hastened from Agamemnon’s tent. To each <br> Gerenian Nestor spake in turn, and fixed <br> His eyes on each intently⁠—most of all <br> Upon Ulysses⁠—and with many a charge <br> To turn Pelides from his angry mood. <br> Along the edge of the resounding deep <br> They went, and as they walked they offered prayer <br> To earth-embracing Neptune, that their words <br> Might move the great soul of Aeacides. <br> And now they came where lay the Myrmidons <br> Among their tents and ships. Achilles there <br> Drew solace from the music of a harp <br> Sweet-toned and shapely, in a silver frame, <br> Part of the spoil he took when he o’erthrew <br> Eëtion’s town. To soothe his mood he sang <br> The deeds of heroes. By him sat alone <br> Patroclus, silent till the song should cease. <br> On moved the messengers⁠—before them walked <br> High-born Ulysses⁠—till they stood beside <br> Achilles. He beheld, and with the harp <br> Sprang from his seat, surprised. Patroclus saw <br> The heroes also, and arose. Their hands <br> The swift Achilles took in his, and said:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Welcome! Ye come as friends. Some pressing cause <br> Must surely bring you hither, whom I prize, <br> Wronged as I am, beyond all other Greeks.”</p>
<p class="verse">Thus speaking, the great son of Peleus led <br> His guests still farther on, and seated them <br> On couches spread with purple coverings, <br> And thus addressed Patroclus, who was near:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Son of Menoetius, bring a larger vase, <br> And mingle purer wine, and place a cup <br> For each, since these are most beloved friends⁠— <br> These warriors who now sit beneath my roof.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake. Patroclus hearkened, and obeyed <br> His well-beloved friend, who meantime placed <br> A block beside the fire, and on it laid <br> Chines of a sheep and of a fatling goat, <br> And of a sow, the fattest of her kind. <br> Automedon stood by and held them fast; <br> Achilles took the knife and skilfully <br> Carved them in portions, and transfixed the parts <br> With spits. Patroclus, the divine in form, <br> Woke to a blaze the fire; and when the flame <br> Had ceased to rise he raked the glowing coals <br> Apart, and o’er them stretched the spits, and strewed, <br> Raising the flesh, the sacred salt o’er all. <br> And when he had made ready and had spread <br> The banquet on the board, Patroclus took <br> The bread and offered it to all the guests <br> In shapely canisters. Achilles served <br> The meats, and took his seat against the wall, <br> In front of great Ulysses. There he bade <br> His friend Patroclus offer sacrifice, <br> Casting the first rich morsels to the flames. <br> The guests put forth their hands and shared the feast; <br> And when the calls of hunger and of thirst <br> Were felt no longer, Ajax gave a nod <br> To Phoenix, which divine Ulysses saw, <br> And filled his cup and drank to Peleus’ son:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Thy health, Achilles! Princely feasts like this <br> Attend us both in Agamemnon’s tent <br> And here⁠—for here is all that makes a feast <br> Complete; yet now is not the time to think <br> Of pleasant banquets, for our thoughts are turned⁠— <br> O Jove-born warrior!⁠—to a fearful time <br> Of slaughter, and the fate of our good ships⁠— <br> Whether we save them harmless, or the foe <br> Destroy them, if thou put not on thy might. <br> For now the haughty Trojans, and the troops <br> Who come from far to aid them, pitch their camp <br> Close to our fleet and wall, and all around <br> Kindle their many fires, and boast that we <br> No longer have the power to drive them back <br> From our black galleys. Jupiter, the son <br> Of Saturn, shows them favorable signs <br> With lightnings from above; and, terrible <br> In aspect and in valor, Hector makes <br> Sad havoc, trusting in the aid of Jove, <br> And neither reverences gods nor men⁠— <br> Such rage possesses him. He prays that soon <br> The morn may rise, that he may hew the prows <br> From all our ships and give them to the flames, <br> And slay the Greeks, bewildered with the smoke. <br> For me, I greatly fear the gods will grant <br> That he fulfil his threat, and that our doom <br> Will be to perish on the Trojan coast, <br> And far away from Argos, famed for steeds. <br> Rise, then, though late⁠—rise with a resolute mind, <br> And from the hard-pressed sons of Greece drive back <br> The assailing Trojans. Thou wilt else lament <br> Hereafter, when the evil shall be done <br> And shall admit no cure. Bethink thee well <br> How from the Greeks thou mayst avert the day <br> Of their destruction. O my friend, when first <br> He sent thee forth to Agamemnon’s help <br> From Phthia’s coast, thy father Peleus said:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“ ‘My child, from Juno and Minerva comes <br> The gift of valor, if they choose to give. <br> But curb thou the high spirit in thy breast, <br> For gentle ways are best, and keep aloof <br> From sharp contentions, that the old and young <br> Among the Greeks may honor thee the more.’</p>
<p class="verse">“Such was the old man’s charge, forgotten now. <br> Yield, then, and lay thy wrath aside. Large gifts <br> Doth Agamemnon offer, to appease <br> Thy wounded spirit. Hear me, if thou wilt, <br> Recount what gifts the monarch in his tent <br> Hath promised thee:⁠—Seven tripods which the fire <br> Hath never touched; six talents of pure gold; <br> And twenty shining cauldrons; and twelve steeds <br> Of hardy frame, victorious in the race, <br> Whose feet have won him prizes in the games. <br> No beggar would he be, nor yet with store <br> Of gold unfurnished, in whose coffers lay <br> The prizes those swift-footed steeds have won. <br> Seven faultless women, skilled in household arts, <br> He offers⁠—Lesbians, whom he chose when thou <br> Didst overrun the populous Lesbian isle⁠— <br> In beauty eminent among their sex. <br> These he bestows, and with them he will send <br> Her whom he took away⁠—Briseis, pure <br> He swears it with a mighty oath⁠—as pure <br> As when she left thy tent. All these he gives <br> At once; and if, by favor of the gods, <br> We lay the mighty city of Priam waste, <br> Thou shalt load down thy galley with large store <br> Of gold and silver, entering first when we, <br> The Greeks, divide the spoil. Then mayst thou choose <br> Twice ten young Trojan women, beautiful <br> Beyond their sex save Helen. If we come <br> Safe to Achaian Argos, richly stocked <br> With milky kine, thou mayst become to him <br> A son-in-law, and cherished equally <br> With his sole son Orestes, who is reared <br> Right royally. Three daughters there, within <br> The monarch’s stately halls⁠—Chrysothemis, <br> Laodice, and Iphianassa⁠—dwell, <br> And thou mayst choose among them, and mayst lead <br> Home to the house of Peleus her who best <br> Deserves thy love. Nor needest thou endow <br> The bride, for he will give an ampler dower <br> Than ever father to his daughter gave⁠— <br> Seven cities with thronged streets⁠—Cardamyle, <br> Enope, grassy Hira, Pherae famed <br> Afar, Antheia with rich pasture-grounds, <br> Aepeia beautiful, and Pedasus <br> With all its vineyards; all are near the sea, <br> And stand the last before you reach the coast <br> Of sandy Pylos. Rich in flocks and herds <br> Their dwellers are, and they will honor thee <br> As if thou wert a god, and, ruled by thee, <br> Will pay large tribute. These will he bestow, <br> Let but thine anger cease. But if the son <br> Of Atreus and his gifts still move thy hate, <br> At least have pity on the afflicted Greeks, <br> Pent in their camp, who now would honor thee <br> As if thou wert a god; and thou shalt gain <br> Great glory as their champion, and shalt slay <br> This Hector, who even now is close at hand, <br> And in a murderous frenzy makes his boast <br> That none of all the chieftains whom the fleet <br> Of Greece brought hither equals him in might.”</p>
<p class="verse">The swift Achilles answered him and said:⁠— <br> “Son of Laertes, nobly born, and versed <br> In wise devices, let me frankly speak <br> Just as I think, and just as I shall act, <br> And then ye will not importune me more. <br> Hateful to me, as are the gates of hell, <br> Is he who, hiding one thing in his heart, <br> Utters another. I shall speak as seems <br> To me the best; nor deem I that the son <br> Of Atreus or the other Greeks can move <br> My settled purpose, since no thanks are paid <br> To him who with the enemy maintains <br> A constant battle: equal is the meed <br> Of him who stands aloof and him who fights <br> Manfully; both the coward and the brave <br> Are held in equal honor, and they die <br> An equal death⁠—the idler and the man <br> Of mighty deeds. For me there is no store <br> Of wealth laid up from all that I have borne, <br> Exposing life in battle. As a bird <br> Brings to her unfledged young the food she finds, <br> Though she herself be fasting, so have I <br> Had many a night unvisited by sleep, <br> And passed in combat many a bloody day, <br> Fighting beside these warriors for their wives. <br> Twelve cities have I with my fleet laid waste, <br> And with my Myrmidons have I o’erthrown <br> Eleven upon this fertile Trojan coast. <br> Full many a precious spoil from these I bore, <br> And to Atrides Agamemnon gave. <br> He, loitering in his fleet, received them all; <br> Few he distributed, and many kept. <br> To chiefs and princes he indeed assigned <br> Prizes, which now they hold. From me alone <br> Of all the Greeks he takes my prize; he takes <br> My bride, whom well I loved;⁠—and let him keep <br> The damsel. But what need is there that Greeks <br> Wage war against the Trojans? For what cause <br> Did Agamemnon, gathering from our realms <br> An army, lead it hither? Was it not <br> Because of fair-haired Helen? Are the sons <br> Of Atreus, then, the only men on earth <br> Who love their wives? Nay, every good man loves <br> And cherishes his spouse; and mine I loved <br> Tenderly, though the captive of my spear: <br> And now, since he hath taken my reward <br> Away and treacherously dealt with me, <br> Let him not try again, for I am warned, <br> And he will not persuade me. Let him take <br> Counsel with thee, Ulysses, and the rest, <br> How to drive back the enemy and save <br> The fleet from flames. Already has he done <br> Much without me; a rampart he has raised, <br> And round it dug a deep, broad trench, and filled <br> The trench with palisades. Yet can he not <br> Resist the man-destroyer Hector thus. <br> This Hector, when I fought among the Greeks, <br> Never would fight at distance from the walls, <br> And ventured not beyond the Scaean gates <br> And beechen tree. There waited he for me <br> Upon a time, and scarce escaped with life <br> From my assault. Now, since I do not choose <br> To fight with noble Hector, I shall pay, <br> Tomorrow, sacrifice to Jupiter <br> And all the gods, and load my galleys well, <br> And draw them to the water. Then shalt thou <br> See⁠—if thou care for such a sight⁠—my ships <br> Sailing upon the fishy Hellespont <br> At early morning, with their crews on board <br> Eager to pull the oar; and if the god <br> Of ocean grant a prosperous voyage, then <br> On the third day we reach the fertile coast <br> Of Phthia. Large possessions left I there <br> When I came hither in an evil hour; <br> And thither I shall carry with me gold <br> And ruddy brass, and women of fair forms, <br> And burnished steel⁠—the spoils I won in war. <br> The prize he gave me, Agamemnon, son <br> Of Atreus, takes, with many insults, back. <br> Bear him this message⁠—give it openly, <br> That others of the Greeks may be like me <br> Indignant should he impudently dare <br> To wrong them also:⁠—Let him ne’er again, <br> Though shameless, dare to look me in the face. <br> I will not join in council or in act <br> With him: he has deceived and wronged me once, <br> And now he cannot wheedle me with words. <br> Let once suffice. I leave him to himself, <br> To perish. All-providing Jupiter <br> Hath made him mad. I hate his gifts; I hold <br> In utter scorn the giver. Were his gifts <br> Tenfold⁠—nay, twenty-fold⁠—the worth of all <br> That he possesses, and with added wealth <br> From others⁠—all the riches that flow in <br> Upon Orchomenus, or Thebes, the pride <br> Of Egypt, where large treasures are laid up, <br> And through whose hundred gates rush men and steeds, <br> Two hundred through each gate;⁠—nay, should he give <br> As many gifts as there are sands and dust <br> Of earth⁠—not even then shall Atreus’ son <br> Persuade me, till I reap a just revenge <br> For his foul contumelies. I will wed <br> No child of Agamemnon. Even though <br> She vied with golden Venus in her charms, <br> And with the blue-eyed Pallas in her skill, <br> I would not wed her. Let him choose among <br> The Greeks a fitter husband⁠—one whose rule <br> Is wider than my own. For if the gods <br> Preserve me, and I reach my home again, <br> My father, Peleus, will bestow on me <br> A consort. Many are the Achaian maids, <br> Daughters of chiefs who hold our citadels <br> In Hellas, and in Phthia, and of these, <br> Her who shall most delight me I will make <br> My well-beloved wife. My soul has longed <br> Earnestly, with a fitting spouse betrothed <br> Duly, to make my dwelling there, and there <br> Enjoy the wealth which aged Peleus won; <br> For not to be compared with life is all <br> The wealth which, as men say, was treasured up <br> In Ilium’s populous town in time of peace, <br> Ere the Greeks came, nor all the stores contained <br> Within the stony threshold of the god <br> Who bears the bow, Apollo, on the coast <br> Of rocky Pytho. We may gather spoil <br> Of oxen and of fatling sheep, and bring <br> Tripods from war, and yellow-manèd steeds: <br> The breath of man no force can seize or hold, <br> And when it leaves the enclosure of the teeth <br> It comes not back. My mother said to me⁠— <br> The goddess, silver-footed Thetis, said⁠— <br> A twofold fate conducts me to my death;⁠— <br> If I remain to fight beneath the walls <br> Of Ilium, my return will be cut off, <br> But deathless my renown; if I return <br> To the dear land in which my fathers dwell, <br> My glory will be nought, but long my life, <br> And late will come to me the stroke of death. <br> And now I counsel all to sail for home, <br> For never will ye see the overthrow <br> Of lofty Ilium. Jove the Thunderer <br> Stretches his great hand o’er her, and her sons <br> Take courage. Go ye now, and take with you <br> This message to the princes of the Greeks⁠— <br> As is the office of an embassy⁠— <br> And bid them meditate some wiser plan <br> To save their galleys and the host of Greeks <br> Within the hollow barques. The plan which brought <br> You hither cannot serve you while I keep <br> My anger unappeased. Let Phoenix stay <br> To pass the night with us, that he may sail <br> Tomorrow, if it please him, to the land <br> We love; I take him not against his will.”</p>
<p class="verse">He ceased; and silent were the ambassadors, <br> Astonished at his passionate words. At last <br> Phoenix, the aged knight, with many tears <br> And sighs, took up the word, in grief and fear <br> Lest Hector should destroy the Grecian fleet:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Illustrious son of Peleus, if indeed <br> Thou wilt return, nor carest to repel <br> From our swift galleys the consuming fire, <br> Because thou art offended, how shall I, <br> Dear child, remain without thee? When at first <br> Peleus, the aged knight, from Phthia sent <br> Thee, yet a boy, to Agamemnon’s aid, <br> Unskilled as then thou wert in cruel war <br> And martial councils⁠—where men also gain <br> A great renown⁠—he sent me with thee, charged <br> To teach thee both, that so thou mightst become <br> In words an orator, in warlike deeds <br> An actor. Therefore, my beloved child, <br> Not willingly shall I remain behind; <br> Not even though a god should promise me <br> That, overcoming the decays of age, <br> I might become a beardless youth again, <br> As when from Hellas and its companies <br> Of lovely maids I came a fugitive, <br> And left Amyntor, son of Ormenus⁠— <br> My father⁠—angry with me for the sake <br> Of a fair-tressed wanton, whom he loved, <br> Treating my mother basely. To my knees <br> My mother came and prayed me ceaselessly, <br> First, to possess the woman, that she then <br> Might loathe the elder one; and I obeyed. <br> My father knew it, and with many a curse <br> Invoked the hateful furies to forbid <br> That any child who owed his birth to me <br> Should ever sit upon his knees. The gods⁠— <br> The Jove of Hades and dread Proserpine⁠— <br> Confirmed his curse. To slay him with the sword <br> Was my first thought. Some god subdued my wrath, <br> Reminding me of what the public voice <br> Would say, and infamy that would ensue⁠— <br> Lest I among the Achaians should be called <br> A parricide. I could not brook to dwell <br> Within my father’s palace while he thus <br> Was wroth with me. My kindred and my friends <br> Came round me, and besought me to remain, <br> And stayed beside me. Many a fatling ewe <br> And many a slow-paced ox with curving horns <br> They slew, and many a fattened swine they stretched <br> Over the flame of Vulcan. From the casks <br> Of the old chief his wine was freely drawn. <br> Nine nights they slept surrounding me, while each <br> Kept watch in turn: nor ever were the fires <br> Put out; one blazed beneath the portico <br> Of the fair hall, and near the chamber-door <br> Another glimmered in the vestibule. <br> But when upon me rose the tenth dark night, <br> I broke my aptly-jointed chamber-doors, <br> And issued forth, and easily o’erleaped <br> The wall around the palace, quite unseen <br> Of watching men and of the serving maids. <br> I fled through spacious Hellas to the fields <br> Of Phthia, nurse of flocks, and to her king, <br> Peleus, who kindly welcomed me, and loved <br> Me as a father loves his only son, <br> Born to large wealth in his declining years. <br> He made me rich, and gave me sovereign rule <br> Over much people. My abode was fixed <br> In farthest Phthia, where I was the prince <br> Of the Dolopians. As for thee, my care, <br> Godlike Achilles, made thee what thou art. <br> I loved thee from my soul: thou wouldst not go <br> With any other to the feast, nor take <br> Thy food at home until upon my knees <br> I placed thee, carved thy meats, and gave them thee, <br> And poured thy wine. The tunic on my breast <br> Was often wetted by thee when the wine <br> Gushed in thy petulant childhood from thy lips. <br> Thus many things did I endure for thee, <br> And many toils perform; and since the gods <br> Vouchsafed no son to me, it was my thought <br> To train thee as a son, that thou mightst be, <br> O godlike man! the bulwark of my age. <br> And now subdue that mighty spirit of thine: <br> Ill it becomes thee to be merciless: <br> The gods themselves are placable, though far <br> Above us all in honor and in power <br> And virtue. We propitiate them with vows, <br> Incense, libations, and burnt-offerings, <br> And prayers for those who have offended. Prayers <br> Are daughters of almighty Jupiter⁠— <br> Lame, wrinkled, and squint-eyed⁠—that painfully <br> Follow Misfortune’s steps; but strong of limb <br> And swift of foot Misfortune is, and, far <br> Outstripping all, comes first to every land, <br> And there wreaks evil on mankind, which prayers <br> Do afterwards redress. Whoe’er receives <br> Jove’s daughters reverently when they approach, <br> Him willingly they aid, and to his suit <br> They listen. Whosoever puts them by <br> With obstinate denial, they appeal <br> To Jove, the son of Saturn, and entreat <br> That he will cause Misfortune to attend <br> The offender’s way in life, that he in turn <br> May suffer evil and be punished thus. <br> Wherefore, Achilles! do thou also yield <br> The honor due Jove’s daughters, freely given <br> By other valiant men. If Atreus’ son <br> Brought thee no gifts, nor promised others still, <br> But kept his anger, I would never ask <br> That thou shouldst lay aside thy wrath and come <br> To help the Argives in their bitter need. <br> But he bestows large gifts, and adds a pledge <br> Of others yet in store, and he hath sent <br> The best men of the army, who to thee <br> Are dearest, to entreat thee. Spurn thou not <br> These, nor their embassy, although at first <br> Thine anger was not causeless. We have heard <br> The praise of heroes of the elder time, <br> Inflamed to vehement anger, yet appeased <br> By gifts, and yielding to persuasive words. <br> One instance I remember: long ago <br> It happened, and I will relate it here <br> Among my friends. Around the city-walls <br> Of Calydon did the Curetes strive <br> In battle with the Aetolians; they destroyed <br> Each other fearfully. The Aetolians fought <br> To save the pleasant town of Calydon, <br> And the Curetes warred to lay it waste. <br> Diana of the golden throne had caused <br> The war, displeased with Oeneus, who withheld <br> From her the first-fruits of his fertile field: <br> While hecatombs were burnt in sacrifice <br> To feast the other gods, to her alone⁠— <br> Daughter of Jove⁠—no offering was brought; <br> For either he forgot, or thought the rite <br> Of little moment; but he greatly erred. <br> And now the child of Jove, the archer-queen, <br> Incensed, sent forth against him from the wood <br> A white-tusked wild boar, which upon his lands <br> Entered, and ravaged them, and brought to earth <br> Many tall trees: tree after tree they fell, <br> With roots uptorn, and all the blossoms on, <br> That promised fruit. Him Meleager, son <br> Of Oeneus, slew, with many hunters called <br> From neighboring cities, bringing many hounds. <br> A few could not subdue him: he had made <br> Many already mount the funeral pile. <br> Diana kindled round the boar a strife <br> For the beast’s head and bristly hide⁠—a war <br> ’Twixt the Curetes and the Aetolian band <br> Of braves. The war, while Meleager fought, <br> Went not with the Curetes, nor could they, <br> Though many, keep the field. But wrath at last <br> Seized Meleager⁠—wrath, which rages oft <br> Even in prudent minds. Incensed against <br> Althaea, his own mother, he remained <br> At home with Cleopatra, his young wife, <br> The beauteous, whom a delicate-footed dame <br> Marpessa, daughter of Evenus, bore <br> To Idas, bravest in his time among <br> The sons of men⁠—so brave that once he drew <br> A bow against Apollo for the sake <br> Of his neat-footed bride. The honored pair <br> Within the palace used to call their child <br> Alcyone; for when the archer-god, <br> Apollo, from her husband bore away <br> The mother, Cleopatra sadly wailed, <br> As wails the halcyon. So beside his spouse <br> Dwelt Meleager, brooding ever o’er <br> The violent anger which his mother’s curse <br> Had kindled. Grieving for a brother’s death, <br> She supplicated heaven, and often struck <br> Her hands against the teeming earth, and called⁠— <br> Kneeling, her bosom all bedewed with tears⁠— <br> On Pluto and the cruel Proserpine, <br> To put her son to death. From Erebus <br> The pitiless Erinnys, wandering <br> In darkness, heard the prayer. Then straightway rose <br> A sound of fearful tumult at the gates: <br> The towers were battered, and the elder chiefs <br> Of the Aetolians hastened to entreat <br> The aid of Meleager, and they sent <br> Priests of the gods, a chosen band, to pray <br> That he would come to their defence. Large gifts <br> They promised. Where the soil of Calydon <br> Was best, they bade him choose a fruitful field <br> Of fifty acres, half for vines, and half, <br> Cleared of the trees, for tillage. Earnestly <br> Did aged Oeneus, famed for horsemanship, <br> Beseech him; to the chamber of his son, <br> High-roofed, he climbed, and at the threshold shook <br> The massive doors with knocking as he sued. <br> His sisters and his reverend mother joined <br> Their supplications: he resisted still. <br> And much his friends, the dearest and most prized, <br> Besought him, but they vainly strove to swerve <br> His steadfast mind, till his own chamber felt <br> The assault, and the Curetes climbed the walls <br> To fire the populous city. Then the nymph, <br> His graceful wife, entreated him with tears, <br> And spake of all the horrors which o’ertake <br> A captured city⁠—all the men cut off <br> By massacre, the houses given to flames, <br> The children and deep-bosomed women dragged <br> Into captivity. Her sorrowful words <br> He heard; his spirit was disturbed; he went <br> To gird his glittering armor on, and thus <br> He saved the Aetolians from a fearful doom, <br> Obeying his own impulse. The reward <br> Of rare and costly gifts they gave him not, <br> Though thus he rescued them. Be not thy thought <br> Like his, my friend; let no invisible power <br> Persuade thee thus to act. Far worse it were <br> To wait, and when our fleet is all on fire <br> Offer thy aid. Accept the gifts at once: <br> Then will the Greeks, as if thou wert a god, <br> Hold thee in honor. If without the gifts <br> Thou enter later on the field of fight, <br> Thou wilt not have like honor with the host, <br> Although thou turn the assault of battle back.”</p>
<p class="verse">Then did Achilles, swift of foot, reply:⁠— <br> “O ancient Phoenix, father, loved of Jove, <br> Such honor need I not; for the decree <br> Of Jove, I deem, already honors me, <br> And will detain me by my beakèd ships <br> While breath is in my lungs, and I have power <br> To move these knees. Yet one thing I would say⁠— <br> And bear it thou in mind⁠—vex not my soul <br> With weeping and lamenting for the sake <br> Of Agamemnon; it becomes thee not⁠— <br> Thou who art loved by me⁠—to yield thy love <br> To him, unless thou wouldst incur my hate. <br> And thou shouldst be the enemy of him <br> Who wrongs me. Reign thou equally with me, <br> And share my honors. These will carry back <br> My answer. Thou remain, and, softly couched, <br> Sleep here: with early morn will we consult <br> Whether to leave this region or remain.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake, and, nodding to Patroclus, gave <br> A signal to prepare an ample couch <br> For Phoenix, while the other chiefs prepared <br> To leave the tent. Then Ajax Telamon, <br> The godlike chief, addressed his comrades thus:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Son of Laertes, nobly born, and skilled <br> In sage devices, let us now depart, <br> Since, as it seems, the end for which we came <br> Cannot be compassed thus, and we must bear <br> With speed the unwelcome answer to the Greeks, <br> Who sit expecting us; while in his breast <br> The implacable Achilles bears a fierce <br> And haughty heart, nor doth he heed the claim <br> Of that close friendship of his fellow-chiefs, <br> Which at the Grecian fleet exalted him <br> Above all others. Unrelenting one! <br> Even for a brother’s death a price is paid, <br> Or when a son is slain: the slayer dwells <br> At home among his people, having made <br> The appointed expiation. He to whom <br> The fine is offered takes it, and his thirst <br> Of vengeance is appeased. But in thy heart <br> The gods have kindled an unquenchable rage, <br> All for a single damsel⁠—and behold, <br> Seven more we offer, passing beautiful, <br> With many gifts beside. Let, then, thy mood <br> Be softened: have respect to thine own roof; <br> For we are guests beneath it, sent from all <br> The assembled host, and strong is our desire <br> To be thy dearest and most cherished friends <br> Of all the Achaians, many as they are.”</p>
<p class="verse">Achilles the swift-footed answered thus:⁠— <br> “Illustrious Ajax, son of Telamon, <br> Prince of the people! All that thou hast said, <br> I well perceive, is prompted by thy heart. <br> Mine swells with indignation when I think <br> How King Atrides mid the assembled Greeks <br> Heaped insults on me, as if I had been <br> A wretched vagabond. But go ye now <br> And bear my message. I shall never think <br> Of bloody war till noble Hector, son <br> Of Priam, slaughtering in his way the Greeks, <br> Shall reach the galleys of the Myrmidons, <br> To lay the fleet in flames. But when he comes <br> To my own tent and galley, he, I think, <br> Though eager for the combat, will desist.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake. Each raised a double cup and poured <br> Libations to the gods; they then returned <br> Beside the fleet. Ulysses led the way.</p>
<p class="verse">Patroclus bade the attendant men and maids <br> Strew with all speed a soft and ample bed <br> For Phoenix. They obeyed, and spread the couch <br> With skins of sheep, dyed coverlets, and sheets <br> Of lawn; and there the old man lay to wait <br> The glorious morn. Meantime Achilles slept <br> Within the tent’s recess, and by him lay <br> Phorbas’s daughter, whom he carried off <br> From Lesbos⁠—Diomede, rosy-cheeked. <br> Upon the other side Patroclus lay, <br> With slender-waisted Iphis by his side, <br> Given by the great Achilles when he took <br> Scyros the tall, where Enyeus ruled.</p>
<p class="verse">Now when the ambassadors were come within <br> The tent of Agamemnon, all the chiefs <br> Rose, one by one, and, lifting up to them <br> Their golden goblets, asked the news they brought; <br> And first Atrides, king of men, inquired:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Renowned Ulysses, glory of the Greeks! <br> Tell me, will he protect our fleet from flames, <br> Or does he, in his wrath and pride, refuse?”</p>
<p class="verse">Then spake the hardy chief Ulysses thus:⁠— <br> “Atrides Agamemnon, glorious king <br> Of men! He will not let his wrath abate, <br> But rages yet more fiercely, and contemns <br> Thee and thy gifts. He leaves thee to consult <br> With thine Achaians by what means to save <br> The fleet and army; for himself he means <br> Tomorrow, with the early dawn, to launch <br> His well-appointed galleys on the sea, <br> And will advise the other Greeks to spread <br> The sails for home, since they will never see <br> The overthrow of lofty Troy, for Jove <br> The Thunderer stretches his protecting hand <br> Above her, and her sons have taken heart. <br> Such are his words; and those who went with me <br> Are present⁠—Ajax and the heralds both, <br> Sage men⁠—the witnesses to what I say. <br> The aged Phoenix stays behind to sleep, <br> And on the morrow to attend his chief <br> To their beloved country⁠—if he will, <br> For else by no means will he take him hence.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake; and all were silent, all amazed <br> At what they heard, for these were bitter words. <br> Long sat the sons of Greece in silent thought, <br> Till Diomed, the great in battle, spake:⁠— <br> “Atrides Agamemnon, glorious king <br> Of men! I would thou hadst not deigned to ask <br> The illustrious son of Peleus for his aid, <br> With offer of large gifts; for arrogant <br> He is at all times: thou hast made him now <br> More insolent. Now leave him to himself, <br> To go or to remain: he yet will fight <br> When his mood changes, or some god within <br> Shall move him. Let us do what I advise:⁠— <br> Betake we all ourselves to rest, but first <br> Refresh ourselves with food and wine; in them <br> Is strength and spirit. When the rosy morn <br> Shall shine, command thou that the foot and horse <br> Be speedily drawn up before the fleet, <br> And thou encourage them with cheerful words, <br> And fight among them in the foremost rank.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake. The kings assented, and admired <br> The words of the horse-tamer Diomed; <br> And, pouring out libations, to their tents <br> They all departed, and lay down to rest, <br> And took into their souls the balm of sleep.</p>
