<p class="lede">Successes of the Greeks⁠—Hector recalled to Troy by Helenus, to appoint a procession of the Trojan matrons to the temple of Minerva⁠—Meeting of Glaucus and Diomed, who recognize each other as old friends⁠—Their exchange of weapons⁠—Meeting of Hector and Andromache, and return of Hector and Paris to the field.</p>
<p class="verse">Now from that stubborn conflict of the Greeks <br> And Trojans had the gods withdrawn. The fight <br> Of men encountering men with brazen spears <br> Still raged from place to place upon the plain <br> Between the Xanthus and the Simoïs.</p>
<p class="verse">And first of all did Ajax Telamon, <br> The bulwark of the Achaians, break the ranks <br> Of Troy and raise the hopes of those who fought <br> Beside him; for he smote the bravest man <br> Of all the Thracian warriors⁠—Acamas, <br> Son of Eussorus, strong and large of limb. <br> His spear-head, through the plumèd helmet’s cone <br> Entering the forehead of the Thracian, pierced <br> The bone, and darkness gathered o’er his eyes. <br> The valiant Diomed slew Axylus, <br> The son of Teuthras. To the war he came <br> From nobly-built Arisba; great his wealth, <br> And greatly was he loved, for courteously <br> He welcomed to his house beside the way <br> All comers. None of these could interpose <br> Between him and his death, for Diomed <br> Slew him and his attendant charioteer, <br> Calysius; both went down below the earth.</p>
<p class="verse">And then Euryalus struck Dresus down, <br> And smote Opheltius, and went on to slay <br> Aesepus and his brother Pedasus;⁠— <br> A river-nymph, Abarbareia, bore <br> Both children to Bucolion the renowned. <br> Bucolion was the eldest of the sons <br> Of great Laomedon. His mother reared <br> The boy in secret. While he fed his sheep, <br> He with the river-nymph was joined in love <br> And marriage, and she bore him twins; and these, <br> Brave and of shapely limb, Mecisteus’ son <br> Struck down, and from their shoulders tore the mail. <br> The warlike Polypoetes overthrew <br> Astyalus; Ulysses smote to earth <br> Pidytes the Percosian with the spear, <br> And Teucer Aretaon, nobly born. <br> The glittering javelin of Antilochus, <br> The son of Nestor, laid Ablerus low; <br> And Agamemnon, king of men, struck down <br> Elatus, who on lofty Pedasus <br> Dwelt, by the smoothly flowing Satnio’s stream. <br> Brave Leitus slew Phylacus in flight, <br> And by Eurypylus Melanthius fell. <br> Then valiant Menelaus took alive <br> Adrastus, whose two coursers, as they scoured <br> The plain in terror, struck against a branch <br> Of tamarisk, and, there entangled, snapped <br> The chariot pole, and, breaking from it, fled <br> Whither were others fleeing. From the car <br> Adrastus to the dust beside the wheel <br> Fell, on his face. There, lifting his huge spear, <br> Atrides Menelaus o’er him stood. <br> Adrastus clasped the warrior’s knees and said:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“O son of Atreus, take me prisoner, <br> And thou shalt have large ransom. In the house <br> Of my rich father ample treasures lie⁠— <br> Brass, gold, and tempered steel⁠—and he shall send <br> Gifts without end when he shall hear that I <br> Am spared alive and in the Grecian fleet.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake, and moved the conqueror, who now <br> Was minded to give charge that one among <br> His comrades to the Grecian fleet should lead <br> The captive. Agamemnon came in haste, <br> And, lifting up his voice, rebuked him thus:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“O Menelaus, soft of heart, why thus <br> Art thou concerned for men like these? In sooth, <br> Great are the benefits thy household owes <br> The Trojans. Nay, let none of them escape <br> The doom of swift destruction by our hands. <br> The very babe within his mother’s womb, <br> Even that must die, and all of Ilium born <br> Perish unburied, utterly cut off.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake; the timely admonition changed <br> The purpose of his brother, who thrust back <br> The suppliant hero with his hand; and then <br> King Agamemnon smote him through the loins, <br> And prone on earth he fell. Upon the breast <br> Of the slain man Atrides placed his heel, <br> And from the body drew the ashen spear.</p>
<p class="verse">Then Nestor to the Argives called aloud:⁠— <br> “Friends, Grecian heroes, ministers of Mars! <br> Let no man here through eagerness for spoil <br> Linger behind the rest, that he may bear <br> Much plunder to the ships; but let us first <br> Strike down our enemies, and afterward <br> At leisure strip the bodies of the dead.”</p>
<p class="verse">Thus speaking, he revived in every breast <br> Courage and zeal. Then had the men of Troy <br> Sought refuge from the Greeks within their walls, <br> O’ercome by abject fear, if Helenus, <br> The son of Priam, and of highest note <br> Among the augurs, had not made his way <br> To Hector and Aeneas, speaking thus:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“O Hector and Aeneas, since on you <br> Is laid the mighty labor to command <br> The Trojans and the Lycians⁠—for the first <br> Are ye in battle, and in council first⁠— <br> Here make your stand, and haste from side to side, <br> Rallying your scattered ranks, lest they betake <br> Themselves to flight, and, rushing to their wives, <br> Become the scorn and laughter of the foe. <br> And then, so soon as ye shall have revived <br> The courage of your men, we here will bide <br> The conflict with the Greeks, though closely pressed; <br> For so we must. But, Hector, thou depart <br> To Troy and seek the mother of us both, <br> And bid her call the honored Trojan dames <br> To where the blue-eyed Pallas has her fane, <br> In the high citadel, and with a key <br> Open the hallowed doors, and let her bring <br> What she shall deem the fairest of the robes, <br> And amplest, in her palace, and the one <br> She prizes most, and lay it on the knees <br> Of the bright-haired Minerva. Let her make <br> A vow to offer to the goddess there <br> Twelve yearling heifers that have never borne <br> The yoke, if she in mercy will regard <br> The city, and the wives and little ones <br> Of its defenders; if she will protect <br> Our sacred Ilium from the ruthless son <br> Of Tydeus, from whose valor armies flee, <br> And whom I deem the bravest of the Greeks. <br> For not so greatly have we held in dread <br> Achilles, the great leader, whom they call <br> The goddess-born; but terrible in wrath <br> Is Diomed, nor hath his peer in might.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake, and Hector of his brother’s words <br> Was not unmindful. Instantly he leaped, <br> Armed, from his chariot, shaking his sharp spears; <br> And everywhere among the host he went, <br> Exhorting them to combat manfully; <br> And thus he kindled the fierce fight anew. <br> They, turning from the flight, withstood the Greeks. <br> The Greeks fell back and ceased to slay; they thought <br> That one of the immortals had come down <br> From out the starry heaven to help the men <br> Of Troy, so suddenly they turned and fought. <br> Then Hector to the Trojans called aloud:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“O valiant sons of Troy, and ye allies <br> Summoned from far! Be men, my friends; call back <br> Your wonted valor, while I go to Troy <br> To ask the aged men, our counsellors, <br> And all our wives, to come before the gods <br> And pray and offer vows of sacrifice.”</p>
<p class="verse">So the plumed Hector spake, and then withdrew, <br> While the black fell that edged his bossy shield <br> Struck on his neck and ankles as he went.</p>
<p class="verse">Now came into the midst between the hosts <br> Glaucus, the offspring of Hippolochus, <br> And met the son of Tydeus⁠—both intent <br> On combat. But when now the twain were near, <br> And ready to engage, brave Diomed <br> Spake first, and thus addressed his enemy:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Who mayst thou be, of mortal men? Most brave <br> Art thou, yet never in the glorious fight <br> Have I beheld thee. Thou surpassest now <br> All others in thy daring, since thou com’st <br> Within the reach of my long spear. The sons <br> Of most unhappy men are they who meet <br> My arm; but⁠—if thou comest from above, <br> A god⁠—I war not with the gods of heaven; <br> For even brave Lycurgus lived not long, <br> The son of Dryas, who engaged in strife <br> With the celestial gods. He once pursued <br> The nurses of the frantic Bacchus through <br> The hallowed ground of Nyssa. All at once <br> They flung to earth their sacred implements, <br> Lycurgus the man-slayer beating them <br> With an ox-driver’s goad. Then Bacchus fled <br> And plunged into the sea, where Thetis hid <br> The trembler in her bosom, for he shook <br> With panic at the hero’s angry threats. <br> Thenceforward were the blessed deities <br> Wroth with Lycurgus. Him did Saturn’s son <br> Strike blind, and after that he lived not long, <br> For he was held in hate by all the gods. <br> So will I never with the gods contend. <br> But if thou be indeed of mortal race, <br> And nourished by the fruits of earth, draw near; <br> And quickly shalt thou pass the gates of death.”</p>
<p class="verse">Hippolochus’s son, the far-renowned, <br> Made answer thus: “O large-souled Diomed, <br> Why ask my lineage? Like the race of leaves <br> Is that of humankind. Upon the ground <br> The winds strew one year’s leaves; the sprouting grove <br> Puts forth another brood, that shoot and grow <br> In the spring season. So it is with man: <br> One generation grows while one decays. <br> Yet since thou takest heed of things like these, <br> And askest whence I sprang⁠—although to most <br> My birth is not unknown⁠—there is a town <br> Lapped in the pasture-grounds where graze the steeds <br> Of Argos, Ephyra by name, and there <br> Dwelt Sisyphus Aeolides, most shrewd <br> Of men; his son was Glaucus, and the son <br> Of Glaucus was the good Bellerophon, <br> To whom the gods gave beauty and the grace <br> Of winning manners. Proetus sought his death <br> And banished him, for Proetus was the chief <br> Among the Argives; Jupiter had made <br> That people subject to his rule. The wife <br> Of Proetus, nobly-born Anteia, sought <br> With passionate desire his secret love, <br> But failed to entice, with all her blandishments, <br> The virtuous and discreet Bellerophon. <br> Therefore went she to Proetus with a lie⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“ ‘Die, Proetus, thou, or put Bellerophon <br> To death, for he has offered force to me.’</p>
<p class="verse">“The monarch hearkened, and was moved to wrath; <br> And then he would not slay him, for his soul <br> Revolted at the deed; he sent him thence <br> To Lycia, with a fatal tablet, sealed, <br> With things of deadly import writ therein, <br> Meant for Anteia’s father, in whose hand <br> Bellerophon must place it, and be made <br> To perish. So at Lycia he arrived <br> Under the favoring guidance of the gods; <br> And when he came where Lycian Xanthus flows, <br> The king of that broad realm received his guest <br> With hospitable welcome, feasting him <br> Nine days, and offering up in sacrifice <br> Nine oxen. But when rosy-fingered Morn <br> Appeared for the tenth time, he questioned him <br> And bade him show the token he had brought <br> From Proetus. When the monarch had beheld <br> The fatal tablet from his son-in-law, <br> The first command he gave him was, to slay <br> Heaven-born Chimaera, the invincible. <br> No human form was hers: a lion she <br> In front, a dragon in the hinder parts, <br> And in the midst a goat, and terribly <br> Her nostrils breathed a fierce, consuming flame; <br> Yet, trusting in the portents of the gods, <br> He slew her. Then it was his second task <br> To combat with the illustrious Solymi⁠— <br> The hardest battle he had ever fought⁠— <br> So he declared⁠—with men; and then he slew⁠— <br> His third exploit⁠—the man-like Amazons. <br> Then he returned to Lycia; on his way <br> The monarch laid a treacherous snare. He chose <br> From his wide Lycian realm the bravest men <br> To lie in ambush for him. Never one <br> Of these came home again⁠—Bellerophon <br> The matchless slew them all. And when the king <br> Saw that he was the offspring of a god, <br> He kept him near him, giving him to wife <br> His daughter, and dividing with him all <br> His kingly honors, while the Lycians set <br> Their richest fields apart⁠—a goodly spot, <br> Ploughlands and vineyards⁠—for the prince to till. <br> And she who now became his wife brought forth <br> Three children to the sage Bellerophon⁠— <br> Isandrus and Hippolochus; and, last, <br> Laodameia, who in secret bore <br> To all-providing Jupiter a son⁠— <br> Godlike Sarpedon, eminent in arms. <br> But when Bellerophon upon himself <br> Had drawn the anger of the gods, he roamed <br> The Alcian fields alone, a prey to thoughts <br> That wasted him, and shunning every haunt <br> Of humankind. The god whose lust of strife <br> Is never sated, Mars, cut off his son <br> Isandrus, warring with the illustrious race <br> Of Solymi; and Dian, she who guides <br> Her car with golden reins, in anger slew <br> His daughter. I am of Hippolochus; <br> From him I claim my birth. He sent me forth <br> To Troy with many counsels and commands, <br> Ever to bear myself like a brave man, <br> And labor to excel, and never bring <br> Dishonor on the stock from which I sprang⁠— <br> The bravest stock by far in Ephyra <br> And the wide realm of Lycia. ’Tis my boast <br> To be of such a race and such a blood.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake. The warlike Diomed was glad, <br> And, planting in the foodful earth his spear, <br> Addressed the people’s shepherd blandly thus:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Most surely thou art my ancestral guest; <br> For noble Oeneus once within his halls <br> Received the blameless chief Bellerophon, <br> And kept him twenty days, and they bestowed <br> Gifts on each other, such as host and guest <br> Exchange; a purple baldric Oeneus gave <br> Of dazzling color, and Bellerophon <br> A double golden goblet; this I left <br> Within my palace when I came to Troy. <br> Of Tydeus I remember nothing, since <br> He left me, yet a little child, and went <br> To Thebes, where perished such a host of Greeks. <br> Henceforward I will be thy host and friend <br> In Argos; thou shalt be the same to me <br> In Lycia when I visit Lycia’s towns; <br> And let us in the tumult of the fray <br> Avoid each other’s spears, for there will be <br> Of Trojans and of their renowned allies <br> Enough for me to slay whene’er a god <br> Shall bring them in my way. In turn for thee <br> Are many Greeks to smite whomever thou <br> Canst overcome. Let us exchange our arms, <br> That even these may see that thou and I <br> Regard each other as ancestral guests.”</p>
<p class="verse">Thus having said, and leaping from their cars, <br> They clasped each other’s hands and pledged their faith. <br> Then did the son of Saturn take away <br> The judging mind of Glaucus, when he gave <br> His arms of gold away for arms of brass <br> Worn by Tydides Diomed⁠—the worth <br> Of fivescore oxen for the worth of nine.</p>
<p class="verse">And now had Hector reached the Scaean gates <br> And beechen tree. Around him flocked the wives <br> And daughters of the Trojans eagerly; <br> Tidings of sons and brothers they required, <br> And friends and husbands. He admonished all <br> Duly to importune the gods in prayer, <br> For woe, he said, was near to many a one.</p>
<p class="verse">And then he came to Priam’s noble hall⁠— <br> A palace built with graceful porticos, <br> And fifty chambers near each other, walled <br> With polished stone, the rooms of Priam’s sons <br> And of their wives; and opposite to these <br> Twelve chambers for his daughters, also near <br> Each other; and, with polished marble walls, <br> The sleeping-rooms of Priam’s sons-in-law <br> And their unblemished consorts. There he met <br> His gentle mother on her way to seek <br> Her fairest child, Laodice. She took <br> His hand and held it fast, while thus she spake:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Why art thou come, my child, and why hast left <br> The raging fight? Full hard these hateful Greeks <br> Press us, in fighting round the city-walls. <br> Thy heart, I know, hath moved thee to repair <br> To our high citadel, and lift thy hands <br> In prayer to Jupiter. But stay thou here <br> Till I bring pleasant wine, that thou mayst pour <br> A part to Jove and to the other gods, <br> And drink and be refreshed; for wine restores <br> Strength to the weary, and I know that thou <br> Art weary, fighting for thy countrymen.”</p>
<p class="verse">Great Hector of the crested helm replied:⁠— <br> “My honored mother, bring not pleasant wine, <br> Lest that unman me, and my wonted might <br> And valor leave me. I should fear to pour <br> Dark wine to Jupiter with hands unwashed. <br> Nor is it fitting that a man like me, <br> Defiled with blood and battle-dust, should make <br> Vows to the cloud-compeller, Saturn’s son. <br> But thou, with incense, seek the temple reared <br> To Pallas the despoiler⁠—calling first <br> Our honored dames together. Take with thee <br> What thou shalt deem the fairest of the robes, <br> And amplest, in thy palace, and the one <br> Thou prizest most, and lay it on the knees <br> Of the bright-haired Minerva. Make a vow <br> To offer to the goddess in her fane <br> Twelve yearling heifers that have never borne <br> The yoke, if she in mercy will regard <br> The city, and the wives and little ones <br> Of its defenders; if she will protect <br> Our sacred Ilium from the ruthless son <br> Of Tydeus, from whose valor armies flee. <br> So to the shrine of Pallas, warrior-queen, <br> Do thou repair, while I depart to seek <br> Paris, if he will listen to my voice. <br> Would that the earth might open where he stands, <br> And swallow him! Olympian Jupiter <br> Reared him to be the bane of all who dwell <br> In Troy, to large-souled Priam and his sons. <br> Could I behold him sinking to the shades, <br> My heart would lose its sense of bitter woe.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake. His mother, turning homeward, gave <br> Charge to her handmaids, who through all the town <br> Passed, summoning the matrons, while the queen <br> Descended to her chamber, where the air <br> Was sweet with perfumes, and in which were laid <br> Her rich embroidered robes, the handiwork <br> Of Sidon’s damsels, whom her son had brought⁠— <br> The godlike Alexander⁠—from the coast <br> Of Sidon, when across the mighty deep <br> He sailed and brought the high born Helen thence. <br> One robe, most beautiful of all, she chose, <br> To bring to Pallas, ampler than the rest, <br> And many-hued; it glistened like a star, <br> And lay beneath them all. Then hastily <br> She left the chamber with the matron train.</p>
<p class="verse">They reached Minerva’s temple, and its gates <br> Were opened by Theano, rosy-cheeked, <br> The knight Antenor’s wife and Cisseus’ child, <br> Made priestess to the goddess by the sons <br> Of Troy. Then all the matrons lifted up <br> Their voices and stretched forth their suppliant hands <br> To Pallas, while the fair Theano took <br> The robe and spread its folds upon the lap <br> Of fair-haired Pallas, and with solemn vows <br> Prayed to the daughter of imperial Jove:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“O venerated Pallas, Guardian-Power <br> Of Troy, great goddess! Shatter thou the lance <br> Of Diomed, and let him fall in death <br> Before the Scaean gates, that we forthwith <br> May offer to thee in thy temple here <br> Twelve yearling heifers that have never worn <br> The yoke, if thou wilt pity us and spare <br> The wives of Trojans and their little ones.”</p>
<p class="verse">So spake she, supplicating; but her prayer <br> Minerva answered not; and while they made <br> Vows to the daughter of Almighty Jove, <br> Hector was hastening to the sumptuous home <br> Of Alexander, which that prince had built <br> With aid of the most cunning architects <br> In Troy the fruitful, by whose hands were made <br> The bed-chamber and hall and ante-room. <br> There entered Hector, dear to Jove; he bore <br> In hand a spear eleven cubits long: <br> The brazen spear-head glittered brightly, bound <br> With a gold circle. In his room he there <br> Found Paris, busied with his shining arms⁠— <br> Corselet and shield; he tried his curved bow; <br> While Argive Helen with the attendant maids <br> Was sitting, and appointed each a task. <br> Hector beheld, and chid him sharply thus:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Strange man! A fitting time indeed is this, <br> To indulge thy sullen humor, while in fight <br> Around our lofty walls the men of Troy <br> Are perishing, and for thy sake the war <br> Is fiercely blazing all around our town. <br> Thou wouldst thyself reprove him, shouldst thou see <br> Another warrior as remiss as thou <br> In time of battle. Rouse thee, then, and act, <br> Lest we behold our city all in flames.”</p>
<p class="verse">Then answered Paris of the godlike form:⁠— <br> “Hector! Although thou justly chidest me, <br> And not beyond my due, yet let me speak. <br> Attend and hearken. Not in sullenness, <br> Nor angry with the Trojans, sat I here <br> Within my chamber, but that I might give <br> A loose to sorrow. Even now my wife <br> With gentle speeches has besought of me <br> That I return to battle; and to me <br> That seems the best, for oft doth victory <br> Change sides in war. Remain thou yet awhile, <br> Till I put on my armor; or go thou, <br> And I shall follow and rejoin thee soon.”</p>
<p class="verse">He ended. Hector of the beamy helm <br> Heard him, and answered not; but Helen spake, <br> And thus with soothing words addressed the chief:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Brother-in-law⁠—for such thou art, though I <br> Am lost to shame, and cause of many ills⁠— <br> Would that some violent blast when I was born <br> Had whirled me to the mountain wilds, or waves <br> Of the hoarse sea, that they might swallow me, <br> Ere deeds like these were done! But since the gods <br> Have thus decreed, why was I not the wife <br> Of one who bears a braver heart and feels <br> Keenly the anger and reproach of men? <br> For Paris hath not, and will never have, <br> A resolute mind, and must abide the effect <br> Of his own folly. Enter thou meanwhile, <br> My brother; seat thee here, for heavily <br> Must press on thee the labors thou dost bear <br> For one so vile as I, and for the sake <br> Of guilty Paris. An unhappy lot, <br> By Jupiter’s appointment, waits us both⁠— <br> A theme of song for men in time to come.”</p>
<p class="verse">Great Hector of the beamy helm replied:⁠— <br> “Nay, Helen, ask me not to sit; thy speech <br> Is courteous, but persuades me not. My mind <br> Is troubled for the Trojans, to whose aid <br> I hasten, for they miss me even now. <br> But thou exhort this man, and bid him haste <br> To overtake me ere I leave the town. <br> I go to my own mansion first, to meet <br> My household⁠—my dear wife and little child; <br> Nor know I whether I may come once more <br> To them, or whether the great gods ordain <br> That I must perish by the hands of Greeks.”</p>
<p class="verse">So spake the plumèd Hector, and withdrew, <br> And reached his pleasant palace, but found not <br> White-armed Andromache within, for she <br> Was in the tower, beside her little son <br> And well-robed nurse, and sorrowed, shedding tears. <br> And Hector, seeing that his blameless wife <br> Was not within, came forth again, and stood <br> Upon the threshold questioning the maids.</p>
<p class="verse">“I pray you, damsels, tell me whither went <br> White-armed Andromache? Has she gone forth <br> To seek my sisters, or those stately dames, <br> My brothers’ wives? Or haply has she sought <br> The temple of Minerva, where are met <br> The other bright-haired matrons of the town <br> To supplicate the dreaded deity?”</p>
<p class="verse">Then said the diligent housewife in reply:⁠— <br> “Since thou wilt have the truth⁠—thy wife is gone <br> Not to thy sisters, nor those stately dames, <br> Thy brothers’ wives; nor went she forth to join <br> The other bright-haired matrons of the town, <br> Where in Minerva’s temple they are met <br> To supplicate the dreaded deity <br> But to the lofty tower of Troy she went <br> When it was told her that the Trojan troops <br> Lost heart, and that the valor of the Greeks <br> Prevailed. She now is hurrying toward the walls, <br> Like one distracted, with her son and nurse.”</p>
<p class="verse">So spake the matron. Hector left in haste <br> The mansion, and retraced his way between <br> The rows of stately dwellings, traversing <br> The mighty city. When at length he reached <br> The Scaean gates, that issue on the field, <br> His spouse, the nobly-dowered Andromache, <br> Came forth to meet him⁠—daughter of the prince <br> Eëtion, who, among the woody slopes <br> Of Placos, in the Hypoplacian town <br> Of Thebé, ruled Cilicia and her sons, <br> And gave his child to Hector great in arms. <br> She came attended by a maid, who bore <br> A tender child⁠—a babe too young to speak⁠— <br> Upon her bosom⁠—Hector’s only son, <br> Beautiful as a star, whom Hector called <br> Scamandrius, but all else Astyanax⁠— <br> The city’s lord⁠—since Hector stood the sole <br> Defence of Troy. The father on his child <br> Looked with a silent smile. Andromache <br> Pressed to his side meanwhile, and, all in tears, <br> Clung to his hand, and, thus beginning, said:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Too brave! Thy valor yet will cause thy death. <br> Thou hast no pity on thy tender child, <br> Nor me, unhappy one, who soon must be <br> Thy widow. All the Greeks will rush on thee <br> To take thy life. A happier lot were mine, <br> If I must lose thee, to go down to earth, <br> For I shall have no hope when thou art gone⁠— <br> Nothing but sorrow. Father have I none, <br> And no dear mother. Great Achilles slew <br> My father when he sacked the populous town <br> Of the Cilicians⁠—Thebé with high gates. <br> ’Twas there he smote Eëtion, yet forbore <br> To make his arms a spoil; he dared not that, <br> But burned the dead with his bright armor on, <br> And raised a mound above him. Mountain-nymphs, <br> Daughters of aegis-bearing Jupiter, <br> Came to the spot and planted it with elms. <br> Seven brothers had I in my father’s house, <br> And all went down to Hades in one day. <br> Achilles the swift-footed slew them all <br> Among their slow-paced bullocks and white sheep. <br> My mother, princess on the woody slopes <br> Of Placos, with his spoils he bore away, <br> And only for large ransom gave her back. <br> But her Diana, archer-queen, struck down <br> Within her father’s palace. Hector, thou <br> Art father and dear mother now to me, <br> And brother and my youthful spouse besides. <br> In pity keep within the fortress here, <br> Nor make thy child an orphan nor thy wife <br> A widow. Post thine army near the place <br> Of the wild fig-tree, where the city-walls <br> Are low and may be scaled. Thrice in the war <br> The boldest of the foe have tried the spot⁠— <br> The Ajaces and the famed Idomeneus, <br> The two chiefs born to Atreus, and the brave <br> Tydides, whether counselled by some seer <br> Or prompted to the attempt by their own minds.”</p>
<p class="verse">Then answered Hector, great in war: “All this <br> I bear in mind, dear wife; but I should stand <br> Ashamed before the men and long-robed dames <br> Of Troy, were I to keep aloof and shun <br> The conflict, coward-like. Not thus my heart <br> Prompts me, for greatly have I learned to dare <br> And strike among the foremost sons of Troy, <br> Upholding my great father’s fame and mine; <br> Yet well in my undoubting mind I know <br> The day shall come in which our sacred Troy, <br> And Priam, and the people over whom <br> Spear-bearing Priam rules, shall perish all. <br> But not the sorrows of the Trojan race, <br> Nor those of Hecuba herself, nor those <br> Of royal Priam, nor the woes that wait <br> My brothers many and brave⁠—who all at last, <br> Slain by the pitiless foe, shall lie in dust⁠— <br> Grieve me so much as thine, when some mailed Greek <br> Shall lead thee weeping hence, and take from thee <br> Thy day of freedom. Thou in Argos then <br> Shalt, at another’s bidding, ply the loom, <br> And from the fountain of Messeis draw <br> Water, or from the Hypereian spring, <br> Constrained unwilling by thy cruel lot. <br> And then shall someone say who sees thee weep, <br> ‘This was the wife of Hector, most renowned <br> Of the horse-taming Trojans, when they fought <br> Around their city.’ So shall someone say, <br> And thou shalt grieve the more, lamenting him <br> Who haply might have kept afar the day <br> Of thy captivity. O, let the earth <br> Be heaped above my head in death before <br> I hear thy cries as thou art borne away!”</p>
<p class="verse">So speaking, mighty Hector stretched his arms <br> To take the boy; the boy shrank crying back <br> To his fair nurse’s bosom, scared to see <br> His father helmeted in glittering brass, <br> And eying with affright the horse-hair plume <br> That grimly nodded from the lofty crest. <br> At this both parents in their fondness laughed; <br> And hastily the mighty Hector took <br> The helmet from his brow and laid it down <br> Gleaming upon the ground, and, having kissed <br> His darling son and tossed him up in play, <br> Prayed thus to Jove and all the gods of heaven:</p>
<p class="verse">“O Jupiter and all ye deities, <br> Vouchsafe that this my son may yet become <br> Among the Trojans eminent like me, <br> And nobly rule in Ilium. May they say, <br> ‘This man is greater than his father was!’ <br> When they behold him from the battle-field <br> Bring back the bloody spoil of the slain foe⁠— <br> That so his mother may be glad at heart.”</p>
<p class="verse">So speaking, to the arms of his dear spouse <br> He gave the boy; she on her fragrant breast <br> Received him, weeping as she smiled. The chief <br> Beheld, and, moved with tender pity, smoothed <br> Her forehead gently with his hand and said:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Sorrow not thus, beloved one, for me. <br> No living man can send me to the shades <br> Before my time; no man of woman born, <br> Coward or brave, can shun his destiny. <br> But go thou home, and tend thy labors there⁠— <br> The web, the distaff⁠—and command thy maids <br> To speed the work: The cares of war pertain <br> To all men born in Troy, and most to me.”</p>
<p class="verse">Thus speaking, mighty Hector took again <br> His helmet, shadowed with the horse-hair plume, <br> While homeward his beloved consort went, <br> Oft looking back, and shedding many tears. <br> Soon was she in the spacious palace-halls <br> Of the min-queller Hector. There she found <br> A troop of maidens⁠—with them all she shared <br> Her grief; and all in his own house bewailed <br> The living Hector, whom they thought no more <br> To see returning from the battle-field, <br> Safe from the rage and weapons of the Greeks,</p>
<p class="verse">Nor waited Paris in his lofty halls, <br> But when he had put on his glorious arms, <br> Glittering with brass, he traversed with quick steps <br> The city; and as when some courser, fed <br> With barley in the stall, and wont to bathe <br> In some smooth-flowing river, having snapped <br> His halter, gayly scampers o’er the plain, <br> And in the pride of beauty bears aloft <br> His head, and gives his tossing mane to stream <br> Upon his shoulders, while his flying feet <br> Bear him to where the mares are wont to graze⁠— <br> So came the son of Priam⁠—Paris⁠—down <br> From lofty Pergamus in glittering arms, <br> And, glorious as the sun, held on his way <br> Exulting and with rapid feet. He found <br> His noble brother Hector as he turned <br> To leave the place in which his wife and he <br> Had talked together. Alexander then⁠— <br> Of godlike form⁠—addressed his brother thus:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“My elder brother! I have kept thee here <br> Waiting, I fear, for me, though much in haste, <br> And came less quickly than thou didst desire.”</p>
<p class="verse">And Hector of the plumèd helm replied:⁠— <br> “Strange being, no man justly can dispraise <br> Thy martial deeds, for thou art truly brave. <br> But oft art thou remiss and wilt not join <br> The combat. I am sad at heart to hear <br> The Trojans⁠—they who suffer for thy sake <br> A thousand hardships⁠—speak so ill of thee. <br> Yet let us go: we will confer of this <br> Another time, if Jove should e’er vouchsafe <br> That to the immortal gods of heaven we pour <br> In our own halls the cup of liberty <br> When we have chased the well-armed Greeks from Troy.”</p>
