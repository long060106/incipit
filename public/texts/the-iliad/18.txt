<p class="lede">Lamentation of Achilles over Patroclus⁠—A visit of condolence from Thetis and her nymphs⁠—Appearance of Achilles on the entrenchments, and consequent alarm of the Trojans⁠—A council of war held by the Trojan Chiefs⁠—Advice of Polydamas to withdraw from the field into Troy opposed by Hector, and rejected⁠—Vulcan, engaged by Thetis to forge a new suit of armor for Achilles.</p>
<p class="verse">As thus they fought with all the rage of fire, <br> Antilochus, the nimble-footed, came <br> With tidings to Achilles. Him he found <br> Before his lofty galleys, deep in thought <br> Of what he knew had happened. With a sigh <br> The hero to his mighty spirit said:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Ah me! Why should the Grecians thus be driven <br> In utter disarray across the plain? <br> I tremble lest the gods should bring to pass <br> What most I dread. My mother told me once <br> That the most valiant of the Myrmidons, <br> While yet I live, cut off by Trojan hands, <br> Shall see the sun no more. It must be so: <br> The brave son of Menoetius has been slain. <br> Unhappy! ’Twas my bidding that, when once <br> The enemy with his firebrands was repulsed, <br> He should not think to combat gallantly <br> With Hector, but should hasten to the fleet.”</p>
<p class="verse">As thus he mused, illustrious Nestor’s son <br> Drew near Achilles, and with eyes that shed <br> Warm tears he gave his sorrowful message thus:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Son of the warlike Peleus, woe is me! <br> For bitter are the tidings thou must hear <br> Of what should not have been. Patroclus lies <br> A naked corpse, and over it the hosts <br> Are fighting; crested Hector hath his arms.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake, and a black cloud of sorrow came <br> Over the chieftain. Grasping in both hands <br> The ashes of the hearth, he showered them o’er <br> His head, and soiled with them his noble face. <br> They clung in dark lumps to his comely vest. <br> Prone in the dust of earth, at his full length, <br> And tearing his disordered hair, he lay. <br> Then wailed aloud the maidens whom in war <br> He and Patroclus captured. Forth they came, <br> And, thronging round him, smote their breasts and swooned. <br> Antilochus mourned also, and shed tears, <br> Holding Achilles by the hand, for much <br> His generous nature dreaded that the chief <br> Might aim at his own throat the sword he wore.</p>
<p class="verse">Loud were the hero’s cries, and in the deep <br> His gracious mother, where she sat beside <br> Her aged father, heard them. She too raised <br> A wail of sorrow. All the goddesses, <br> Daughters of Nereus, dwelling in the depths <br> Of ocean, gathered to her side. There came <br> Glaucè, Thaleia, and Cymodocè, <br> Nesaea, Speio, Halia with large eyes, <br> And Thoa, and Cymothoë; nor stayed <br> Actaea, Limnoreia, Melita, <br> Amphithoë, Iaera, Agavè, <br> Doto, and Proto, and Dynamenè. <br> There came Dexamene, Amphinomè, <br> Pherusa, Callianira, Panopè, <br> Doris, and Galateia, the renowned. <br> With these Nemertes and Apseudes came, <br> And Callianassa. Clymenè was there, <br> Janeira and Janassa, and with them <br> Maera, and Amatheia with bright hair, <br> And Orithya, and whoever else, <br> Children of Nereus, bide within the deep. <br> The concourse filled the glimmering cave; they beat <br> Their bosoms, while the sorrowing Thetis spake:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Hear, sister Nereids, that ye all may know <br> The sharpness of my sorrows. Woe is me, <br> Unhappy! Woe is me! In evil hour, <br> The mother of a hero⁠—me who gave <br> Birth to so noble and so brave a son, <br> The first among the warriors, saw him grow <br> Like a green sapling, reared him like a plant <br> Within a fruitful field, and sent him forth <br> With his beaked ships to Ilium and the war <br> Against the Trojans. Never shall I see <br> That son returning to his home, the halls <br> Of Peleus. While he lives and sees the light <br> Of day his lot is sorrow, nor can I <br> Help him in aught, though at his side; and yet <br> I go to look on my beloved son, <br> And learn from him what grief, while he remains <br> Aloof from war, o’ertakes him in his tent.”</p>
<p class="verse">She spake, and left the cavern. All the nymphs <br> Went with her weeping. Round their way the waves <br> Of ocean parted. When they reached the fields <br> Of fertile Troas, up the shore they went <br> In ordered files to where, a numerous fleet, <br> Drawn from the water, round Achilles lay <br> The swift ships of the Myrmidons. To him <br> His goddess mother came, and with a cry <br> Of grief embraced the head of her dear son, <br> And, mourning o’er him, spake these wingèd words:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Why weepest thou, my son? What sorrow now <br> O’ercomes thy spirit? Speak, and hide it not. <br> All thou didst pray for once, with lifted hands, <br> Has been fulfilled by Jove; the sons of Greece, <br> Driven to their galleys, and with thy good help <br> Withdrawn from them, are routed and disgraced.”</p>
<p class="verse">The swift Achilles, sighing deeply, made <br> This answer: “O my mother! True it is <br> Olympian Jove hath done all this for me; <br> But how can that delight me, since my friend, <br> My well-beloved Patroclus, is no more? <br> He whom, of all my fellows in the war, <br> I prized the most, and loved as my own self, <br> Is lost to me, and Hector, by whose hand <br> He was cut off, has spoiled him of his arms⁠— <br> His dreaded arms, a wonder to the sight <br> And glorious, which the gods of heaven bestowed <br> On Peleus, sumptuous bridal gifts, when thou <br> Wert led by them to share a mortal’s bed. <br> Yet would that thou hadst evermore remained <br> Among the immortal dwellers of the deep, <br> And Peleus had espoused a mortal maid, <br> Since now thy heart must ache with infinite grief <br> For thy slain son, whom thou shalt never more <br> Welcome returning to his home. No wish <br> Have I to live or to concern myself <br> In men’s affairs, save this: that Hector first, <br> Pierced by my spear, shall yield his life, and pay <br> The debt of vengeance for Patroclus slain.”</p>
<p class="verse">And Thetis, weeping, answered: “O my son! <br> Soon must thou die; thou sayest true; that fate <br> Hangs over thee as soon as Hector dies.”</p>
<p class="verse">Again the swift Achilles, sighing, spake: <br> “Then quickly let me die, since fate denied <br> That I should aid my friend against the foes <br> That slew him. Far from his own land he fell, <br> And longed for me to rescue him. And now, <br> Since I am never more to see the land <br> I love, and since I went not to defend <br> Patroclus, nor the other Greeks, my friends, <br> Of whom so many have fallen by the hand <br> Of noble Hector, but beside the fleet <br> Am sitting here, a useless weight on earth, <br> Mighty in battle as I am beyond <br> The other Grecian warriors, though excelled <br> By other men in council⁠—would that Strife <br> Might perish among gods and men, with Wrath, <br> Which makes even wise men cruel, and, though sweet <br> At first as dropping honey, growing, fills <br> The heart with its foul smoke. Such was my rage, <br> Aroused by Agamemnon, king of men. <br> Yet now, though great my wrong, let things like these <br> Rest with the past, and, as the time requires, <br> Let us subdue the spirit in our breasts. <br> I go in quest of Hector, by whose hand <br> My friend was slain. My death will I accept <br> Whene’er to Jove and to the other gods <br> It shall seem good to send it. Hercules, <br> Though mighty and beloved of Jupiter, <br> The son of Saturn, could not shun his death, <br> For fate and Juno’s cruel wrath prevailed <br> Against him. I shall lie in death like him, <br> If a like fate be measured out for me. <br> Yet now shall I have glory; I shall do <br> What many a Trojan and Dardanian dame, <br> Deep-bosomed, wiping with both hands the tears <br> From their fair cheeks, shall bitterly lament; <br> And well shall they perceive that, till this hour, <br> I paused from war. Thou lov’st me; but seek not <br> To keep me from the field, for that were vain.”</p>
<p class="verse">The silver-footed Thetis thus rejoined: <br> “Truly, my son, thy purpose is not ill, <br> To rescue thy endangered friends from death. <br> But with the Trojans are thy beautiful arms, <br> Brazen and dazzling bright; their crested chief, <br> Hector, exults to wear them: no long space, <br> I think, will he exult; his death is near. <br> Yet go not to the battle-field until <br> Thine eyes shall look upon me yet again. <br> I come tomorrow with the sun, and bring <br> Bright arms, the work of Vulcan’s royal hand.”</p>
<p class="verse">So having said, and turning from her son, <br> She thus bespake her sisters of the sea: <br> “Return to the broad bosom of the deep, <br> To its gray Ancient and my father’s halls, <br> And tell him all. I hasten to ascend <br> The summits of Olympus, there to ask <br> Of Vulcan, the renowned artificer, <br> Armor of glorious beauty for my son.”</p>
<p class="verse">She spake: at once they plunged into the deep, <br> While Thetis, silver-footed goddess, sought <br> Olympus, whence it was her hope to bring <br> New armor for her son. As thus her feet <br> Bore her toward heaven, the Achaians, fleeing fast, <br> With infinite clamor, driven before the arm <br> Of the man-queller Hector, reached the ships <br> And Hellespont. Nor could the well-armed Greeks <br> Bear off Patroclus from the shower of darts; <br> For rushing on them came both foot and horse, <br> And Hector, son of Priam, like a flame <br> In fury. Thrice illustrious Hector seized <br> The body by the heels to drag it off, <br> And called his Trojans with a mighty shout. <br> Thrice did the chieftains Ajax, terrible <br> In resolute valor, drive him from the dead. <br> Yet kept he to his purpose, confident <br> In his own might, now charging through the crowd, <br> Now standing firm and shouting to his men, <br> And never losing ground. As when, at night, <br> Herdsmen that watch their cattle strive in vain <br> To drive a lion, fierce and famine-pinched, <br> From some slain beast, so the two Ajaxes, <br> With all their valor, vainly strove to keep <br> Hector, the son of Priam, from the corpse. <br> And now would he have dragged it thence, and won <br> Infinite glory, had not Iris come⁠— <br> The goddess whose swift feet are like the wind⁠— <br> To Peleus’ son, a messenger from heaven, <br> In haste, unknown to Jupiter and all <br> The other gods⁠—for Juno sent her down⁠— <br> To bid the hero arm. She came and stood <br> Beside him, speaking thus with wingèd words:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Pelides, rise, most terrible of men, <br> In rescue of Patroclus, over whom <br> They struggle fiercely at the fleet; for there <br> They slay each other⁠—these who fight to keep <br> The dead, and those, the men of Troy, who charge <br> To drag him off to Ilium’s airy heights; <br> And chief, illustrious Hector longs to seize <br> The corpse, and from the delicate neck to hew <br> The head, and fix it on a stake. Arise, <br> Loiter no longer;⁠—rise, ashamed to leave <br> Patroclus to be torn by Trojan dogs. <br> For thine will be the infamy, if yet <br> The corpse be brought dishonored to thy tent.”</p>
<p class="verse">The swift Achilles listened and inquired: <br> “Which of the gods, O Iris, speaks by thee?” <br> And Iris, whose swift feet are like the wind, <br> Answered: “The glorious spouse of Jupiter, <br> Juno, hath sent me. Even Saturn’s son, <br> On his high throne, knows not that I am sent, <br> Nor any other of the gods who dwell <br> Upon Olympus overspread with snow.”</p>
<p class="verse">“But how,” the swift Achilles asked again, <br> “Shall I go forth to war? They have my arms, <br> And my beloved mother strictly bade <br> That I should put no armor on until <br> I saw her face again. She promised me <br> A suit of glorious mail from Vulcan’s hand. <br> Nor know I any warrior here whose arms <br> Might serve me, save, perhaps, it were the shield <br> Of Telamonian Ajax, who, I hope, <br> Is in the van, and dealing death among <br> The foe, in vengeance for Patroclus slain.”</p>
<p class="verse">Then the swift-footed Iris spake again: <br> “They have thy glorious armor; that we know <br> But go thou to the trench, and show thyself <br> To them of Troy, that, haply smit with fear, <br> They may desist from battle, and the host <br> Of Grecian warriors, overtoiled, may breathe <br> In a brief respite from the stress of war.”</p>
<p class="verse">So the fleet Iris spake, and passed away, <br> And then arose Achilles, dear to Jove, <br> While o’er his ample shoulders Pallas held <br> Her fringèd aegis. The great goddess caused <br> A golden cloud to gather round his head <br> And kindled in the cloud a dazzling flame. <br> And as when smoke, ascending to the sky, <br> Hangs o’er some city in a distant isle, <br> Which enemies beleaguer, swarming forth <br> From their own city, and in hateful strife <br> Contend all day, but when the sun goes down <br> Forthwith blaze many bale-fires, sending up <br> A brightness which the neighboring realms may see, <br> That haply they may send their ships and drive <br> The war away⁠—so from the hero’s head <br> That flame streamed upward to the sky. He came <br> Without the wall and stood beside the trench, <br> Nor mingled with the Greeks, for he revered <br> His mother’s words. He stood and called aloud, <br> And Pallas, from the host, returned his shout⁠— <br> A shout that carried infinite dismay <br> Into the Trojan squadrons. As the sound <br> Of trumpet rises clear when deadly foes <br> Lay siege to a walled city, such was heard <br> The clear shout uttered by Aeacides. <br> The hearts of all who heard that brazen voice <br> Were troubled, and their steeds with flowing manes <br> Turned backward with the chariots⁠—such the dread <br> Of coming slaughter. When the charioteers <br> Beheld the terrible flame that played unquenched <br> Upon the brow of the magnanimous son <br> Of Peleus, lighted by the blue-eyed maid <br> Minerva, they were struck with panic fear. <br> Thrice o’er the trench Achilles shouted; thrice <br> The men of Troy and their renowned allies <br> Fell into wild disorder. Then there died, <br> Entangled midst their chariots, and transfixed <br> By their own spears, twelve of their bravest chiefs. <br> The Greeks bore off Patroclus from the field <br> With eager haste, and placed him on a bier, <br> And there the friends that loved him gathered round <br> Lamenting. With them swift Achilles came, <br> The hot tears on his cheeks, as he beheld <br> His faithful comrade lying on his bier, <br> Mangled with many wounds, whom he had sent <br> With steeds and car to battle, never more <br> To welcome him alive on his return.</p>
<p class="verse">Now Juno, large-eyed and august, bade set <br> The never-wearied sun; unwillingly <br> He sank into the ocean streams. Then paused <br> The noble Greeks from that ferocious strife, <br> Deadly in equal measure to both hosts. <br> The Trojans also paused, and from their cars <br> Unharnessed the fleet steeds, and ere they took <br> Their evening meal assembled to consult. <br> Standing they held the council; no man cared <br> To sit, for all were trembling from the hour <br> When, long a stranger to the bloody field, <br> Achilles showed himself again. And now <br> The son of Panthoüs, wise Polydamas, <br> Began to speak. Beyond the rest he saw <br> Things past and things to come, and he had been <br> Hector’s companion, born in the same night, <br> Mighty in speech as Hector with the spear. <br> With prudent admonitions thus he spake:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Consider well, my friends. My counsel is <br> That we return, nor wait the holy morn <br> Here, by the fleet and in the open plain, <br> Far from our city ramparts. While this man <br> Was wroth with Agamemnon, we maintained <br> A strife of far less peril with the Greeks, <br> And I was ever ready to encamp <br> By night beside the galleys, which we hoped <br> To make our prize; but now I fear the might <br> Of swift Pelides. He will not remain <br> Content upon the space between the fleet <br> And town, where Greeks and Trojans wage a war <br> Of changeful fortune, but will strive to take <br> The city, and to carry off our wives. <br> March we then homeward. Let my words prevail⁠— <br> It must be so. The gentle Night now keeps <br> The nimble-footed hero from the war. <br> But if tomorrow, issuing forth in arms, <br> He find us here, there are among us those <br> Who will have cause to know him. Gladly then <br> Will he find refuge who escapes his arm <br> In sacred Troy, and many a Trojan corpse <br> Will feed the dogs and vultures. May mine ear <br> Hear of it never. But if ye will heed <br> My words, though sorrowful, ye shall be safe <br> Assembled in the city squares at night. <br> The lofty towers and gates, with massive beams <br> Polished and strongly fitted each to each, <br> Will keep the town. Tomorrow we shall take, <br> At dawn, our station on the towers, arrayed <br> In armor, and his difficult task will be, <br> Far from his ships, to fight us from below; <br> And after he has tired his high-necked steeds <br> With coursing round the ramparts to and fro, <br> Back to his galleys he must go; nor yet <br> With all his valor can he force his way <br> Into the town to lay its dwellings waste⁠— <br> The dogs will feed upon his carcass first.”</p>
<p class="verse">And crested Hector answered with a frown: <br> “The counsel thou hast given, Polydamas, <br> Pleases me not⁠—that we return to be <br> Pent up in Troy. Are ye not weary yet <br> Of lying long imprisoned within walls <br> And towers? The time has been that in all lands, <br> Wherever human speech is heard, the fame <br> Of Priam’s city, for its treasured gold <br> And brass, was in all mouths. Those treasures now <br> Have passed away; our dwellings have them not. <br> Much that we had was sold on Phrygia’s coast, <br> And in Maeonia’s pleasant land, for Jove <br> The mighty was displeased with us. But now, <br> When politic Saturn’s son hath granted me <br> To win great glory at the fleet, and hold <br> The Greeks imprisoned by the sea, refrain, <br> Idler, from laying counsels such as these <br> Before the people. Not a Trojan here <br> Will follow them, nor would I suffer it. <br> Now hearken all, and act as I advise: <br> First banquet, rank by rank, throughout the host, <br> And set your guards, and each of you keep watch; <br> And then, if any Trojan stands in fear <br> For his possessions, let him bring them all <br> Into the common stock, to be consumed; <br> Better that we enjoy them than the Greeks. <br> Tomorrow, with the dawn and all in arms, <br> We will do battle at the roomy ships <br> Valiantly. If in truth the noble son <br> Of Peleus choose to rise and to defend <br> The ships, so much the worse for him, since I <br> Shall not for him desert the field, but stand <br> Firmly against him, whether he obtain <br> The victory or I. The chance of war <br> Is equal, and the slayer oft is slain.”</p>
<p class="verse">So Hector spake: the Trojans shouted forth <br> Applause, the madmen! Pallas took away <br> Their reason; all approved the fatal plan <br> Of Hector; no one ventured to commend <br> The sober counsel of Polydamas. <br> And then they banqueted throughout the host; <br> But all night long the Achaians mourned with tears <br> Patroclus, while Pelides in the midst, <br> Leading the ceaseless lamentation, placed <br> His slaughter-dealing hands upon the breast <br> Of his companion with continual sighs. <br> As a maned lion, from whose haunt within <br> The thick, dark wood a hunter has borne off <br> The whelps, returning finds them gone, and grieves, <br> And roams the valleys, tracking as he goes <br> The robber, bent to find him, for his rage <br> Is fierce⁠—with such fierce sorrow Peleus’ son <br> Spake, deeply sighing, to his Myrmidons:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“O, idle were the words which once I spake, <br> When in our palace-halls I bade the chief <br> Menoetius bear a cheerful heart. I said <br> That I would bring to Opus yet again, <br> Laden with spoil from Ilium overthrown, <br> His valiant son. But Jove doth not fulfil <br> The plans of men. That both of us should stain <br> Earth with our blood in Troy was the decree <br> Of fate, and never will the aged knight <br> Peleus receive me in his palace-halls, <br> Returning from the war, nor Thetis, she <br> Who gave me birth; the earth will hold me here. <br> And now, since after thee I take my place <br> In earth, Patroclus, I will not perform <br> Thy funeral rites before I bring to thee <br> The arms and head of the magnanimous chief <br> Hector, who slew thee. By thy funeral pile <br> I will strike off in vengeance for thy death <br> The heads of twelve illustrious Trojan youths. <br> Thou meanwhile, lying at the beaked ships, <br> Shalt be lamented night and day, with tears, <br> By many a Trojan and Dardanian maid, <br> Deep-bosomed, won by our victorious spears <br> After hard wars and opulent cities sacked.”</p>
<p class="verse">Thus having said, the great Achilles bade <br> Place a huge tripod on the fire in haste, <br> To cleanse Patroclus from the clotted blood. <br> They brought and set upon the glowing hearth <br> A tripod for the bath, and in it poured <br> Water, and piled the wood beneath. The flame <br> Crept up the vessel’s rounded sides and warmed <br> The water. When within the murmuring brass <br> It boiled, they washed the dead, and with rich oil <br> Anointed him, and filled the open wounds <br> With ointment nine years old; and laying him <br> Upon a couch, they spread from head to foot <br> Fine linen over him, and covered all <br> With a white mantle. Through the hours of night <br> The Myrmidons, lamenting their dead chief, <br> Wept round the swift Achilles. Then did Jove <br> Thus to his wife and sister Juno speak:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Large-eyed, imperial Juno, thou hast now <br> Accomplished thy desire, for thou hast roused <br> The swift Achilles. There is not a doubt <br> The long-haired Argives owe their birth to thee.”</p>
<p class="verse">And large-eyed Juno answered: “What strange words, <br> Austere Saturnius, hast thou said? A man, <br> A mortal far less skilled in shaping means <br> To compass ends, might do what I have done <br> Against his fellow-man. Then should not I⁠— <br> Who boast to be the chief of goddesses <br> By birthright, and because I bear the name <br> Of wife to thee who rulest o’er the gods⁠— <br> Plan evil to the Trojans, whom I hate?”</p>
<p class="verse">So talked they. Silver-footed Thetis came <br> Meanwhile to Vulcan’s halls, eternal, gemmed <br> With stars, a wonder to the immortals, wrought <br> Of brass by the lame god. She found him there <br> Sweating and toiling, and with busy hand <br> Plying the bellows. He was fashioning <br> Tripods, a score, to stand beside the wall <br> Of his fair palace. All of these he placed <br> On wheels of gold, that, of their own accord, <br> They might roll in among the assembled gods, <br> And then roll back, a marvel to behold. <br> So far they all were finished; but not yet <br> Were added the neat handles, and for these <br> The god was forging rivets busily. <br> While thus he labored, with a mind intent <br> Upon his skilful task, on silver feet <br> Came Thetis. Charis, of the snowy veil, <br> The beautiful, whom the great god of fire, <br> Vulcan, had made his wife, beheld, and came <br> Forward to meet her, seized her hand, and said:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“O Thetis of the flowing robe, beloved <br> And honored, what has brought thee to our home <br> Thou dost not often visit us. Come in, <br> That I may pay the honors due a guest.”</p>
<p class="verse">So the bright goddess spake, and led the way, <br> And seated Thetis on a sumptuous throne, <br> With silver studs divinely wrought, and placed <br> A footstool, and called out to Vulcan thus: <br> “Come, Vulcan; Thetis here hath need of thee.”</p>
<p class="verse">And the great artist, Vulcan, thus replied: <br> “Then of a truth a goddess is within <br> Whom I must ever honor and revere; <br> Who from the danger of my terrible fall <br> Saved me, what time my shameless mother sought <br> To cast me from her sight, for I was lame. <br> Then great had been my misery, had not <br> Eurynomè and Thetis in their laps <br> Received me as I fell⁠—Eurynomè, <br> Daughter of billowy Ocean. There I dwelt <br> Nine years, and many ornaments I wrought <br> Of brass⁠—clasps, buckles, bracelets, necklaces⁠— <br> Within a vaulted cave, round which the tides <br> Of the vast ocean murmured and flung up <br> Their foam; nor any of the gods or men <br> Knew of my hiding-place, save only they <br> Who saved me, Thetis and Eurynomè. <br> And now, as she is with us, I must make <br> To fair-haired Thetis some thank-offering <br> For having rescued me. Haste, spread the board <br> Amply with generous fare, while I shall lay <br> Aside my bellows and my implements.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake, and from his anvil-block arose, <br> A mighty bulk; his weak legs under him, <br> Halting, moved painfully. He laid apart <br> His bellows from the fire, and gathered up <br> The scattered implements with which he wrought, <br> And locked them in a silver chest, and wiped <br> With a moist sponge his face and both his hands, <br> Stout neck and hairy chest. He then put on <br> His tunic, took his massive regal wand <br> Into his hand, and, tottering, sallied forth. <br> Two golden statues, like in form and look <br> To living maidens, aided with firm gait <br> The monarch’s steps. And mind was in their breasts, <br> And they had speech and strength, and from the gods <br> Had learned becoming arts. Beside their lord <br> They walked and tended him. As he drew near, <br> Halting, to Thetis on the shining throne, <br> He took the goddess by the hand and said:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“What cause, O Thetis of the flowing robe, <br> Honored and dear, has brought thee to our home? <br> Not often com’st thou hither. Freely say <br> Whatever lies upon thy mind. My heart <br> Commands me to obey, if it be aught <br> That can be done and may be done by me.”</p>
<p class="verse">And Thetis answered, with a gush of tears: <br> “O Vulcan! Of the goddesses who dwell <br> Upon Olympus, is there one who bears <br> Such bitter sorrows as Saturnian Jove <br> Inflicts on me, distressed above them all? <br> Me, of the ocean deities, he forced <br> To take a mortal husband⁠—Peleus, son <br> Of Aeacus⁠—and to his bed I came <br> Unwillingly. Within his palace-halls, <br> Worn with a late old age, my husband lies. <br> Now I have other woes; for when a son <br> Was granted me, and I had brought him forth <br> And reared him, flourishing like a young plant, <br> A sapling in a fertile field, and great <br> Among the heroes⁠—thus maturely trained, <br> I sent him with his beaked ships to Troy, <br> To combat with her sons; but never more <br> Will it be mine to welcome him returned <br> Home to the halls of Peleus. While to me <br> He lives, and sees the sunshine, he endures <br> Affliction, nor can I, though at his side, <br> Aid him in aught. The maiden whom the Greeks <br> Decreed him as his prize, the king of men, <br> Atrides, took away, and grief for her <br> Consumes his heart. The Trojans keep the Greeks <br> Beleaguered by their ships, nor suffer them <br> To pass beyond their gates. The elder chiefs <br> Implored him to relent, and offered him <br> Large presents; he refused to avert the doom <br> That threatened them himself, but sent instead <br> Patroclus to the war with his own arms, <br> And with him sent much people. All the day <br> They fought before the Scaean gates; and then <br> Had Ilium fallen, but that Apollo slew <br> The brave son of Menoetius, who had caused <br> Vast slaughter⁠—slew him fighting in the van <br> Of war, and gave the glory of his death <br> To Hector. Therefore I approach thy knees, <br> And ask for him, my son, so soon to die, <br> Buckler and helm, and beautiful greaves, shut close <br> With clasps, and all the other arms complete, <br> Which in the war my son’s companion lost. <br> For now Achilles lies upon the ground <br> Bitterly grieving in his inmost soul.”</p>
<p class="verse">And Vulcan, the great artist, answered her: <br> “Be comforted, and take no further thought <br> Of this; for would I could as certainly <br> Shield him from death’s dread summons when his hour <br> Is come at last, as I shall have for him <br> Beautiful armor ready to put on, <br> And such as every man, of multitudes <br> Who look on it hereafter, shall admire.”</p>
<p class="verse">So speaking he withdrew, and went where lay <br> The bellows, turned them toward the fire, and bade <br> The work begin. From twenty bellows came <br> Their breath into the furnaces⁠—a blast <br> Varied in strength as need might be; for now <br> They blew with violence for a hasty task, <br> And then with gentler breath, as Vulcan pleased <br> And as the work required. Upon the fire <br> He laid impenetrable brass, and tin, <br> And precious gold and silver; on its block <br> Placed the huge anvil, took the ponderous sledge, <br> And held the pincers in the other hand.</p>
<p class="verse">And first he forged the huge and massive shield, <br> Divinely wrought in every part⁠—its edge <br> Clasped with a triple border, white and bright. <br> A silver belt hung from it, and its folds <br> Were five; a crowd of figures on its disk <br> Were fashioned by the artist’s passing skill, <br> For here he placed the earth and heaven, and here <br> The great deep and the never-resting sun <br> And the full moon, and here he set the stars <br> That shine in the round heaven⁠—the Pleiades, <br> The Hyades, Orion in his strength, and the <br> Bear near him, called by some the Wain, <br> That, wheeling, keeps Orion still in sight, <br> Yet bathes not in the waters of the sea.</p>
<p class="verse">There placed he two fair cities full of men. <br> In one were marriages and feasts; they led <br> The brides with flaming torches from their bowers, <br> Along the streets, with many a nuptial song. <br> There the young dancers whirled, and flutes and lyres <br> Gave forth their sounds, and women at the doors <br> Stood and admired. Meanwhile a multitude <br> Was in the forum, where a strife went on⁠— <br> Two men contending for a fine, the price <br> Of one who had been slain. Before the crowd <br> One claimed that he had paid the fine, and one <br> Denied that aught had been received, and both <br> Called for the sentence which should end the strife. <br> The people clamored for both sides, for both <br> Had eager friends; the heralds held the crowd <br> In check; the elders, upon polished stones, <br> Sat in a sacred circle. Each one took, <br> In turn, a herald’s sceptre in his hand, <br> And, rising, gave his sentence. In the midst <br> Two talents lay in gold, to be the meed <br> Of him whose juster judgment should prevail.</p>
<p class="verse">Around the other city sat two hosts <br> In shining armor, bent to lay it waste, <br> Unless the dwellers would divide their wealth⁠— <br> All that their pleasant homes contained⁠—and yield <br> The assailants half. As yet the citizens <br> Had not complied, but secretly had planned <br> An ambush. Their beloved wives meanwhile, <br> And their young children, stood and watched the walls, <br> With aged men among them, while the youths <br> Marched on, with Mars and Pallas at their head, <br> Both wrought in gold, with golden garments on, <br> Stately and large in form, and over all <br> Conspicuous, in bright armor, as became <br> The gods; the rest were of an humbler size. <br> And when they reached the spot where they should lie <br> In ambush, by a river’s side, a place <br> For watering herds, they sat them down, all armed <br> In shining brass. Apart from all the rest <br> They placed two sentries, on the watch to spy <br> The approach of sheep and horned kine. Soon came <br> The herds in sight; two shepherds walked with them, <br> Who, all unweeting of the evil nigh, <br> Solaced their task with music from their reeds. <br> The warriors saw and rushed on them, and took <br> And drave away large prey of beeves, and flocks <br> Of fair white sheep, whose keepers they had slain. <br> When the besiegers in their council heard <br> The sound of tumult at the watering-place, <br> They sprang upon their nimble-footed steeds, <br> And overtook the pillagers. Both bands <br> Arrayed their ranks and fought beside the stream, <br> And smote each other. There did Discord rage, <br> And Tumult, and the great Destroyer, Fate. <br> One wounded warrior she had seized alive, <br> And one unbounded yet, and through the field <br> Dragged by the foot another, dead. Her robe <br> Was reddened o’er the shoulders with the blood <br> From human veins. Like living men they ranged <br> The battle-field, and dragged by turns the slain.</p>
<p class="verse">There too he sculptured a broad fallow field <br> Of soft rich mould, thrice ploughed, and over which <br> Walked many a ploughman, guiding to and fro <br> His steers, and when on their return they reached <br> The border of the field the master came <br> To meet them, placing in the hands of each <br> A goblet of rich wine. Then turned they back <br> Along the furrows, diligent to reach <br> Their distant end. All dark behind the plough <br> The ridges lay, a marvel to the sight, <br> Like real furrows, though engraved in gold.</p>
<p class="verse">There, too, the artist placed a field which lay <br> Deep in ripe wheat. With sickles in their hands <br> The laborers reaped it. Here the handfuls fell <br> Upon the ground; there binders tied them fast <br> With bands, and made them sheaves. Three binders went <br> Close to the reapers, and behind them boys, <br> Bringing the gathered handfuls in their arms. <br> Ministered to the binders. Staff in hand, <br> The master stood among them by the side <br> Of the ranged sheaves and silently rejoiced. <br> Meanwhile the servants underneath an oak <br> Prepared a feast apart; they sacrificed <br> A fatling ox and dressed it, while the maids <br> Were kneading for the reapers the white meal.</p>
<p class="verse">A vineyard also on the shield he graved, <br> Beautiful, all of gold, and heavily <br> Laden with grapes. Black were the clusters all; <br> The vines were stayed on rows of silver stakes. <br> He drew a blue trench round it, and a hedge <br> Of tin. One only path there was by which <br> The vintagers could go to gather grapes. <br> Young maids and striplings of a tender age <br> Bore the sweet fruit in baskets. Midst them all, <br> A youth from his shrill harp drew pleasant sounds, <br> And sang with soft voice to the murmuring strings. <br> They danced around him, beating with quick feet <br> The ground, and sang and shouted joyously.</p>
<p class="verse">And there the artist wrought a herd of beeves, <br> High-horned, and sculptured all in gold and tin. <br> They issued lowing from their stalls to seek <br> Their pasture, by a murmuring stream, that ran <br> Rapidly through its reeds. Four herdsmen, graved <br> In gold, were with the beeves, and nine fleet dogs <br> Followed. Two lions, seizing on a bull <br> Among the foremost cattle, dragged him off <br> Fearfully bellowing; hounds and herdsmen rushed <br> To rescue him. The lions tore their prey, <br> And lapped the entrails and the crimson blood. <br> Vainly the shepherds pressed around and urged <br> Their dogs, that shrank from fastening with their teeth <br> Upon the lions, but stood near and bayed.</p>
<p class="verse">There also did illustrious Vulcan grave <br> A fair, broad pasture, in a pleasant glade, <br> Full of white sheep, and stalls, and cottages, <br> And many a shepherd’s fold with sheltering roof.</p>
<p class="verse">And there illustrious Vulcan also wrought <br> A dance⁠—a maze like that which Daedalus, <br> In the broad realm of Gnossus once contrived <br> For fair-haired Ariadne. Blooming youths <br> And lovely virgins, tripping to light airs, <br> Held fast each other’s wrists. The maidens wore <br> Fine linen robes; the youths had tunics on <br> Lustrous as oil, and woven daintily. <br> The maids wore wreaths of flowers; the young men swords <br> Of gold in silver belts. They bounded now <br> In a swift circle⁠—as a potter whirls <br> With both his hands a wheel to try its speed, <br> Sitting before it⁠—then again they crossed <br> Each other, darting to their former place. <br> A multitude around that joyous dance <br> Gathered, and were amused, while from the crowd <br> Two tumblers raised their song, and flung themselves <br> About among the band that trod the dance.</p>
<p class="verse">Last on the border of that glorious shield <br> He graved in all its strength the ocean-stream.</p>
<p class="verse">And when that huge and massive shield was done, <br> He forged a corselet brighter than the blaze <br> Of fire; he forged a solid helm to fit <br> The hero’s temples, shapely and enchased <br> With rare designs, and with a crest of gold. <br> And last he forged him greaves of ductile tin.</p>
<p class="verse">When the great artist Vulcan saw his task <br> Complete, he lifted all that armor up <br> And laid it at the feet of her who bore <br> Achilles. Like a falcon in her flight, <br> Down plunging from Olympus capped with snow, <br> She bore the shining armor Vulcan gave.</p>
