<p class="lede">Preparations for the funeral of Patroclus hastened by his appearance to Achilles in a dream⁠—Wood brought from the forest for the funeral pile⁠—A funeral procession, with offerings of hair shorn from the heads of the chiefs and laid on the dead⁠—Sacrifice offered, and the twelve Trojan youths slain, and the pile kindled⁠—The funeral games, at which Achilles presides.</p>
<p class="verse">So mourned they in the city; but the Greeks, <br> When they had reached the fleet and Hellespont, <br> Dispersed, repairing each one to his ship, <br> Save that Achilles suffered not his band <br> Of Myrmidons to part in disarray. <br> And thus the chief enjoined his warrior friends:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Myrmidons, gallant knights, my cherished friends! <br> Let us not yet unyoke our firm-paced steeds, <br> But bring them with the chariots, and bewail <br> Patroclus with the honors due the dead, <br> And, when we have indulged in grief, release <br> Our steeds and take our evening banquet here.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake, and led by him the host broke forth <br> In lamentation. Thrice around the dead, <br> Weeping, they drave their steeds with stately manes, <br> While Thetis in their hearts awoke the sense <br> Of hopeless loss; their tears bedewed the sands, <br> And dropped upon their arms, so brave was he <br> For whom they sorrowed. Peleus’ son began <br> The mourning; on the breast of his dead friend <br> He placed his homicidal hands, and said:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Hail thou, Patroclus, even amid the shades! <br> For now shall I perform what once I vowed: <br> That, dragging Hector hither, I will give <br> His corse to dogs, and they shall rend his flesh; <br> And at thy funeral pile there shall be slain <br> Twelve noble Trojan youths, to avenge thy death.”</p>
<p class="verse">So spake he, meditating outrages <br> To noble Hector’s corse, which he had flung <br> Beside the bier of Menoetiades, <br> Amid the dust. The Myrmidons unbraced <br> Their shining brazen armor, and unyoked <br> Their neighing steeds, and sat in thick array <br> Beside the ship of swift Aeacides, <br> While he set forth a sumptuous funeral feast. <br> Many a white ox, that day, beneath the axe <br> Fell to the earth, and many bleating goats <br> And sheep were slain, and many fattened swine, <br> White-toothed, were stretched to roast before the flame <br> Of Vulcan, and around the corse the earth <br> Floated with blood. Meantime the Grecian chiefs <br> To noble Agamemnon’s royal tent <br> Led the swift son of Peleus, though he went <br> Unwillingly, such anger for the death <br> Of his companion burned within his heart. <br> As soon as they had reached his tent, the king <br> Bade the clear-throated heralds o’er the fire <br> Place a huge tripod, that Pelides there <br> Might wash away the bloody stains he bore. <br> Yet would he not, and with an oath replied:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“No! By the greatest and the best of gods, <br> By Jupiter, I may not plunge my head <br> Into the bath before I lay my friend <br> Patroclus on the fire, and heap his mound, <br> And till my hair is shorn; for never more <br> In life will be so great a sorrow mine. <br> But now attend we to this mournful feast. <br> And with the morn, O king of men, command <br> That wood be brought, and all things duly done <br> Which may beseem a warrior who goes down <br> Into the lower darkness. Let the flames <br> Seize fiercely and consume him from our sight, <br> And leave the people to the tasks of war.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake; they hearkened and obeyed, and all <br> Prepared with diligent hands the meal, and each <br> Sat down and took his portion of the feast. <br> And when their thirst and hunger were allayed, <br> Most to their tents betook them and to rest. <br> But Peleus’ son, lamenting bitterly, <br> Lay down among his Myrmidons, beside <br> The murmuring ocean, in the open space, <br> Where plashed the billows on the beach. And there, <br> When slumber, bringing respite from his cares, <br> Came softly and enfolded him⁠—for much <br> His shapely limbs were wearied with the chase <br> Of Hector round the windy Ilium’s walls⁠— <br> The soul of his poor friend Patroclus came, <br> Like him in all things⁠—stature, beautiful eyes, <br> And voice, and garments which he wore in life. <br> Beside his head the vision stood and spake:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Achilles, sleepest thou, forgetting me? <br> Never of me unmindful in my life, <br> Thou dost neglect me dead. O, bury me <br> Quickly, and give me entrance through the gates <br> Of Hades; for the souls, the forms of those <br> Who live no more, repulse me, suffering not <br> That I should join their company beyond <br> The river, and I now must wander round <br> The spacious portals of the House of Death. <br> Give me thy hand, I pray; for never more <br> Shall I return to earth when once the fire <br> Shall have consumed me. Never shall we take <br> Counsel together, living, as we sit <br> Apart from our companions; the hard fate <br> Appointed me at birth hath drawn me down. <br> Thou too, O godlike man, wilt fall beneath <br> The ramparts of the noble sons of Troy. <br> Yet this I ask, and if thou wilt obey, <br> This I command thee⁠—not to let my bones <br> Be laid apart from thine. As we were reared <br> Under thy roof together, from the time <br> When first Menoetius brought thee, yet a boy, <br> From Opus, where I caused a sorrowful death;⁠— <br> For by my hand, when wrangling at the dice, <br> Another boy, son of Amphidamas, <br> Was slain without design⁠—and Peleus made <br> His halls my home, and reared me tenderly, <br> And made me thy companion;⁠—so at last <br> May one receptacle, the golden vase <br> Given by thy gracious mother, hold our bones.”</p>
<p class="verse">The swift Achilles answered: “O most loved <br> And honored, wherefore art thou come, and why <br> Dost thou command me thus? I shall fulfil <br> Obediently thy wish; yet draw thou near, <br> And let us give at least a brief embrace, <br> And so indulge our grief.” He said, and stretched <br> His longing arms to clasp the shade. In vain; <br> Away like smoke it went, with gibbering cry, <br> Down to the earth. Achilles sprang upright, <br> Astonished, clapped his hands, and sadly said:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Surely there dwell within the realm below <br> Both soul and form, though bodiless. All night <br> Hath stood the spirit of my hapless friend <br> Patroclus near me, sad and sorrowful, <br> And asking many duties at my hands, <br> A marvellous semblance of the living man.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake, and moved the hearts of all to grief <br> And lamentation. Rosy-fingered Morn <br> Dawned on them as around the hapless dead <br> They stood and wept. Then Agamemnon sent <br> In haste from all the tents the mules and men <br> To gather wood, and summoned to the task <br> Meriones, himself a gallant chief, <br> Attendant on the brave Idomeneus. <br> These went with woodmen’s axes and with ropes <br> Well twisted, and before them went the mules. <br> O’er steep, o’er glen, by straight, by winding ways, <br> They journeyed till they reached the woodland wilds <br> Of Ida fresh with springs, and quickly felled <br> With the keen steel the towering oaks that came <br> Crashing to earth. Then, splitting the great trunk. <br> They bound them on the mules, that beat the earth <br> With hasty footsteps through the tangled wood, <br> Impatient for the plain. Each woodcutter <br> Shouldered a tree, for so Meriones, <br> Companion of the brave Idomeneus, <br> Commanded, and at last they laid them down <br> In order on the shore, where Peleus’ son <br> Planned that a mighty sepulchre should rise <br> Both for his friend Patroclus and himself.</p>
<p class="verse">So brought they to the spot vast heaps of wood, <br> And sat them down, a numerous crowd. But then <br> Achilles bade his valiant Myrmidons <br> Put on their brazen mail and yoke their steeds. <br> At once they rose, and put their harness on, <br> And they who fought from chariots climbed their seats <br> With those who reined the steeds. These led the van, <br> And after them a cloud of men on foot <br> By thousands followed. In the midst was borne <br> Patroclus by his comrades. Cutting off <br> Their hair, they strewed it, covering the dead. <br> Behind the corpse, Achilles in his hands <br> Sustained the head, and wept, for on that day <br> He gave to Hades his most cherished friend. <br> Now when they reached the spot which Pelcus’ son <br> Had chosen, they laid down the dead, and piled <br> The wood around him, while the swift of foot, <br> The great Achilles, bent on other thoughts, <br> Standing apart, cut off his amber hair, <br> Which for the river Sperchius he had long <br> Nourished to ample growth, and, sighing, turned <br> His eyes upon the dark-blue sea, and said:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Sperchius, in vain my father made a vow <br> That I, returning to my native shore, <br> Should bring my hair, an offering to thee, <br> And slay a consecrated hecatomb, <br> And burn a sacrifice of fifty rams, <br> Beside the springs where in a sacred field <br> Thy fragrant altar stands. Such was the vow <br> Made by the aged man, yet hast thou not <br> Fulfilled his wish. And now, since I no more <br> Shall see my native land, the land I love, <br> Let the slain hero bear these locks away.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake, and in his dear companion’s hands <br> He placed the hair, and all around were moved <br> To deeper grief; the setting sun had left <br> The host lamenting, had not Peleus’ son <br> Addressed Atrides, standing at his side:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Atrides, thou whose word the Greeks obey <br> Most readily, all mourning has an end. <br> Dismiss the people from the pyre to take <br> Their evening meal, while we with whom it rests <br> To pay these mournful duties to the dead <br> Will close the rites; but let the chiefs remain.”</p>
<p class="verse">This when the monarch Agamemnon heard, <br> Instantly he dismissed to their good ships <br> The people. They who had the dead in charge <br> Remained, and heaped the wood, and built a pyre <br> A hundred feet each way from side to side. <br> With sorrowful hearts they raised and laid the corse <br> Upon the summit. Then they flayed and dressed <br> Before it many fatlings of the flock, <br> And oxen with curved feet and crooked horns. <br> From these magnanimous Achilles took <br> The fat, and covered with it carefully <br> The dead from head to foot. Beside the bier, <br> And leaning toward it, jars of honey and oil <br> He placed, and flung, with many a deep-drawn sigh, <br> Twelve high-necked steeds upon the pile. Nine hounds <br> There were, which from the table of the prince <br> Were daily fed; of these Achilles struck <br> The heads from two, and laid them on the wood, <br> And after these, and last, twelve gallant sons <br> Of the brave Trojans, butchered by the sword; <br> For he was bent on evil. To the pile <br> He put the iron violence of fire, <br> And, wailing, called by name the friend he loved:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Rejoice, Patroclus, even in the land <br> Of souls. Lo! I perform the vow I made; <br> Twelve gallant sons of the brave men of Troy <br> The fire consumes with thee. For Hector’s corse, <br> The flames shall not devour it, but the dogs.”</p>
<p class="verse">Such was his threat; but Hector was not made <br> The prey of dogs, for Venus, born to Jove, <br> Drave off by night and day the ravenous tribe, <br> And with a rosy and ambrosial oil <br> Anointed him, that he might not be torn <br> When dragged along the earth. Above the spot <br> And all around it, where the body lay, <br> Phoebus Apollo drew a veil of clouds <br> Reaching from heaven, that on his limbs the flesh <br> And sinews might not stiffen in the sun.</p>
<p class="verse">The flame seized not upon the funeral pile <br> Of the dead chief. Pelides, swift of foot, <br> Bethought him of another rite. He stood <br> Apart, and offered vows to the two winds, <br> Boreas and Zephyr. Promising to bring <br> Fair offerings to their shrines, and pouring out <br> Libations from a golden cup, he prayed <br> That they would haste and wrap the pile in flames, <br> And burn the dead to ashes. At his prayer <br> Fleet Iris on a message to the Winds <br> Took instant wing. They sat within the halls <br> Of murmuring Zephyr, at a solemn feast. <br> There Iris lighted on the threshold-stone. <br> As soon as they beheld her, each arose <br> And bade her sit beside him. She refused <br> To seat her at the banquet, and replied:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Not now; for I again must take my way <br> Over the ocean currents to the land <br> Where dwell the Ethiopians, who adore <br> The gods with hecatombs, to take my share <br> Of sacrifice. Achilles supplicates, <br> With promise of munificent offerings, <br> Boreas and sounding Zephyrus to come <br> And blow the funeral structure into flames <br> On which, bewailed by all the Grecian host, <br> Patroclus lies, and waits to be consumed.”</p>
<p class="verse">So spake she, and departed. Suddenly <br> Arose the Winds with tumult, driving on <br> The clouds before them. Soon they reached the deep; <br> Beneath the violence of their sounding breath <br> The billows heaved. They swept the fertile fields <br> Of Troas, and descended on the pyre, <br> And mightily it blazed with fearful roar. <br> All night they howled and tossed the flames. All night <br> Stood swift Achilles, holding in his hand <br> A double beaker; from a golden jar <br> He dipped the wine, and poured it forth, and steeped <br> The earth around, and called upon the soul <br> Of his unhappy friend. As one laments <br> A newly married son upon whose corse <br> The flames are feeding, and whose death has made <br> His parents wretched, so did Peleus’ son, <br> Burning the body of his comrade, mourn, <br> As round the pyre he moved with frequent sighs.</p>
<p class="verse">Now when the star that ushers in the day <br> Appeared, and after it the morning, clad <br> In saffron robes, had overspread the sea, <br> The pyre sank wasted, and the flames arose <br> No longer, and the Winds, departing, flew <br> Homeward across the Thracian sea, which tossed <br> And roared with swollen billows as they went. <br> And now Pelides from the pyre apart <br> Weary lay down, and gentle slumber soon <br> Came stealing over him. Meantime the Greeks <br> Gathered round Agamemnon, and the stir <br> And bustle of their coming woke the chief, <br> Who sat upright and thus addressed his friends:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Atrides, and all ye who lead the hosts <br> Of Greece! Our task is, first to quench the pyre <br> With dark red wine where’er the flames have spread, <br> And next to gather, with discerning care, <br> The bones of Menoetiades. And these <br> May well be known; for in the middle space <br> He lay, and round about him, and apart <br> Upon the border, were the rest consumed⁠— <br> The bodies of the captives and the steeds. <br> Be his enclosed within a golden vase, <br> And wrapped around with caul, a double fold, <br> Till I too pass into the realm of Death. <br> And be a tomb not over-spacious reared, <br> But of becoming size, which afterward <br> Ye whom we leave behind in our good ships, <br> When we are gone, will build more broad and high.”</p>
<p class="verse">So spake the swift Pelides, and the chiefs <br> Complied; and first they quenched with dark red wine <br> The pyre, where’er the flames had spread, and where <br> Lay the deep ashes; then, with many tears, <br> Gathered the white bones of their gentle friend, <br> And laid them in a golden vase, wrapped round <br> With caul, a double fold. Within the tents <br> They placed them softly, wrapped in delicate lawn, <br> Then drew a circle for the sepulchre, <br> And, laying its foundations to enclose <br> The pyre, they heaped the earth, and, having reared <br> A mound, withdrew. Achilles yet detained <br> The multitude, and made them all sit down, <br> A vast assembly. From the ships he brought <br> The prizes⁠—cauldrons, tripods, steeds, and mules, <br> Oxen in sturdy pairs, and graceful maids, <br> And shining steel. Then for the swiftest steeds <br> A princely prize he offered first⁠—a maid <br> Of peerless form, and skilled in household arts, <br> And a two-handled tripod of a size <br> For two-and-twenty measures. He gave out <br> The second prize⁠—a mare unbroken yet, <br> Of six years old, and pregnant with a mule. <br> For the third winner in the race he staked <br> A cauldron that had never felt the fire, <br> Holding four measures, beautiful, and yet <br> Untarnished. For the fourth, he offered gold, <br> Two talents. For the fifth, and last, remained <br> A double vessel never touched by fire. <br> He rose and stood, and thus addressed the Greeks:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Atrides, and ye other well-armed Greeks, <br> These prizes lie within the chariot-course, <br> And wait the charioteers. Were but these games <br> In honor of another, then would I <br> Contend, and win and carry to my tent <br> The first among these prizes. For my steeds, <br> Ye know, surpass the rest in speed, since they <br> Are of immortal birth, by Neptune given <br> To Peleus, and by him in turn bestowed <br> On me his son. But I and they will keep <br> Aloof; they miss their skilful charioteer, <br> Who washed in limpid water from the fount <br> Their manes, and moistened them with softening oil. <br> And now they mourn their friend, and sadly stand <br> With drooping heads and manes that touch the ground. <br> Let such of you as trust in their swift steeds <br> And their strong cars prepare to join the games.”</p>
<p class="verse">Pelides spake: the abler charioteers <br> Arose, and, first of all, the king of men, <br> Eumelus, eminent in horsemanship, <br> The dear son of Admetus. Then arose <br> The valiant son of Tydeus, Diomed, <br> And led beneath the yoke the Trojan steeds <br> Won from Aeneas when Apollo saved <br> That chief from death. The son of Atreus next, <br> The noble Menelaus, yellow-haired, <br> Brought two swift coursers underneath the yoke, <br> King Agamemnon’s Aethè, and with her <br> His own Podargus. Echepolus once, <br> Anchises’ son, sent Aethè as a gift <br> To Agamemnon, that he might be free <br> From following with the army to the heights <br> Of Ilium, and enjoy the ease he loved; <br> For Jove had given him wealth, and he abode <br> On Sicyon’s plains. Now, eager for the race, <br> She took the yoke. Antilochus, the fourth, <br> The gallant son of the magnanimous king, <br> Neleian Nestor, harnessed next his steeds <br> With stately manes. Swift coursers that were foaled <br> At Pylus drew his chariot. To his side as <br> His father came and stood, and spake and gave <br> Wise counsels, though the youth himself was wise:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Antilochus, I cannot doubt that Jove <br> And Neptune both have loved thee, teaching thee, <br> Young as thou art, all feats of horsemanship. <br> Small is the need to instruct thee. Thou dost know <br> Well how to turn the goal, and yet thy steeds <br> Are slow, and ill for thee may be the event. <br> Their steeds are swift, yet have they never learned <br> To govern them with greater skill than thou. <br> Now then, dear son, bethink thee heedfully <br> Of all precautions, lest thou miss the prize. <br> By skill the woodman, rather than by strength, <br> Brings down the oak; by skill the pilot guides <br> His wind-tossed galley over the dark sea; <br> And thus by skill the charioteer o’ercomes <br> His rival. He who trusts too much his steeds <br> And chariot lets them veer from side to side <br> Along the course, nor keeps a steady rein <br> Straight on, while one expert in horsemanship, <br> Though drawn by slower horses, carefully <br> Observes the goal, and closely passes it, <br> Nor fails to know how soon to turn his course, <br> Drawing the leathern reins, and steadily <br> Keeps on, and watches him who goes before. <br> Now must I show the goal which, easily <br> Discerned, will not escape thine eye. It stands <br> An ell above the ground, a sapless post, <br> Of oak or larch⁠—a wood of slow decay <br> By rain, and at its foot on either side <br> Lies a white stone; there narrow is the way, <br> But level is the race-course all around. <br> The monument it is of one long dead, <br> Or haply it has been in former days <br> A goal, as the swift-footed Peleus’ son <br> Has now appointed it. Approach it near, <br> Driving thy chariot close upon its foot, <br> Then in thy seat lean gently to the left <br> And cheer the right-hand horse, and ply the lash, <br> And give him a loose rein, yet firmly keep <br> The left-hand courser close beside the goal⁠— <br> So close that the wheel’s nave may seem to touch <br> The summit of the post; yet strike thou not <br> The stone beside it, lest thou lame thy steeds <br> And break the chariot, to thy own disgrace <br> And laughter of the others. My dear son, <br> Be on thy guard; for if thou pass the goal <br> Before the rest, no man in the pursuit <br> Can overtake or pass thee, though he drave <br> The noble courser of Adrastus, named <br> Arion the swift-footed, which a god <br> Bade spring to life, or those of matchless speed <br> Reared here in Ilium by Laomedon.”</p>
<p class="verse">Neleian Nestor spake, and, having thus <br> Given all the needful cautions, took his seat <br> In his own place. Meriones, the fifth, <br> Harnessed his steeds with stately manes, and all <br> Mounted their chariots. Lots were cast; the son <br> Of Peleus shook the helmet, and the lot <br> Of Nestor’s son, Antilochus, leaped forth; <br> And next the lot of King Eumelus came; <br> And Menelaus, mighty with the spear, <br> Had the third lot; Meriones was next; <br> And to the bravest of them all, the son <br> Of Tydeus, fell the final lot and place. <br> They stood in order, while Achilles showed <br> The goal far off upon the level plain, <br> And near it, as the umpire of the race, <br> He placed the godlike Phoenix, who had been <br> His father’s armor-bearer, to observe <br> With judging eye, and bring a true report.</p>
<p class="verse">All raised at once the lash above their steeds, <br> And smote them with the reins, and cheered them on <br> With vehement cries. Across the plain they swept, <br> Far from the fleet; beneath them rose the dust, <br> A cloud, a tempest, and their tossing manes <br> Were lifted by the wind. And now the cars <br> Touched earth, and now were flung into the air. <br> Erect the drivers stood, with beating hearts, <br> Eager for victory, each encouraging <br> His steeds, that flew beneath the shroud of dust.</p>
<p class="verse">But when they turned their course, and swiftly ran <br> Back to the hoary deep to close the course, <br> Well did the skill of every chief appear. <br> They put their horses to the utmost speed, <br> And then did the quick-footed steeds that drew <br> Eumelus bear him on beyond the rest. <br> But with his Trojan coursers Diomed <br> Came next, so near it seemed that they would mount <br> The car before them, and upon the back. <br> And ample shoulders of Eumelus smote <br> Their steaming breath; for as they ran their heads <br> Leaned over him. And then would Diomed <br> Have passed him by, or would at least have made <br> The victory doubtful, had not Phoebus struck, <br> In his displeasure, from the hero’s hand <br> The shining scourge. It fell, and to his eyes <br> Started indignant tears; for now he saw <br> The others gaining on him, while the speed <br> Of his own steeds, which feared the lash no more, <br> Was slackened. Yet Apollo’s stratagem <br> Was not unseen by Pallas, who o’ertook <br> The shepherd of the people, and restored <br> The scourge he dropped, and put into his steeds <br> New spirit. In her anger she approached <br> Eumelus, snapped his yoke, and caused his mares <br> To start asunder from the track; the pole <br> Was dashed into the ground, and from the seat <br> The chief was flung beside the wheel, his mouth, <br> Elbows, and nostrils torn, his forehead bruised. <br> Grief filled his eyes with tears and choked his voice, <br> While Diomed drave by his firm-paced steeds, <br> Outstripping all the rest; for Pallas nerved <br> Their limbs with vigor, and bestowed on him <br> Abundant glory. After him the son <br> Of Atreus, fair-haired Menelaus, came, <br> While Nestor’s son cheered on his father’s steeds:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“On, on! Press onward with your utmost speed! <br> Not that I bid you strive against the steeds <br> Of warlike Diomed, for Pallas gives <br> Swiftness to them and glory to the man <br> Who holds the reins; but let us overtake <br> The horses of Atrides, nor submit <br> To be thus distanced, lest the victory <br> Of the mare Aethè cover you with shame. <br> Fleet as ye are, why linger? This at least <br> I tell you, and my words will be fulfilled: <br> Look not for kindly care at Nestor’s hands, <br> That shepherd of the people, but for death <br> With the sharp steel, if through your fault we take <br> A meaner prize. Then onward and away, <br> With all your strength, for this is my design⁠— <br> To pass by Menelaus where the way <br> Is narrow, and he cannot thwart my plan.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake, and they who feared their master’s threat <br> Mended their speed awhile. The warlike son <br> Of Nestor saw just then the narrow pass <br> Within the hollow way, a furrow ploughed <br> By winter floods, which there had torn the course <br> And deepened it. Atrides, to avoid <br> The clash of wheels, drave thither; thither too <br> Antilochus⁠—who turned his firm-paced steeds <br> A little from the track in which they ran⁠— <br> Followed him close. Atrides saw with fear, <br> And shouted to Antilochus aloud:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Antilochus, thou drivest rashly; rein <br> Thy horses in. The way is narrow here, <br> But soon will broaden, and thou then canst pass. <br> Beware lest with thy chariot-wheels thou dash <br> Against my own, and harm befall us both.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake; but all the more Antilochus <br> Urged on his coursers with the lash, as if <br> He had not heard. As far as flies a quoit <br> Thrown from the shoulder of a vigorous youth <br> Who tries his strength, so far they ran abreast. <br> The horses of Atrides then fell back; <br> He slacked the reins; for much he feared the steeds <br> Would dash against each other in the way, <br> And overturn the sumptuous cars, and fling <br> The charioteers contending for the prize <br> Upon the dusty track. With angry words <br> The fair-haired Menelaus chided thus:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Antilochus, there is no man so prone <br> As thou to mischief, and we greatly err, <br> We Greeks, who call thee wise. Go now, and yet <br> Thou shalt not take the prize without an oath.”</p>
<p class="verse">Again he spake, encouraging his steeds: <br> “Check not your speed, nor sorrowfully stand: <br> Their feet and knees will fail with weariness <br> Before your own; they are no longer young.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake; the coursers, honoring his voice. <br> Ran with fresh speed, and soon were near to those <br> Of Nestor’s son. Meantime the assembled Greeks <br> Sat looking where the horses scoured the plain <br> And filled the air with dust. Idomeneus, <br> The lord of Crete, descried the coursers first, <br> For on a height he sat above the crowd. <br> He heard the chief encouraging his steeds, <br> And knew him, and he marked before the rest <br> A courser, chestnut-colored save a spot <br> Upon the middle of the forehead, white, <br> And round as the full moon. And then he stood <br> Upright, and from his place harangued the Greeks:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“O friends, the chiefs and leaders of the Greeks, <br> Am I the sole one that descries the steeds, <br> Or do ye also? Those who lead the race, <br> I think, are not the same, and with them comes <br> A different charioteer. The mares, which late <br> Were foremost, may have somewhere come to harm. <br> I saw them first to turn the goal, and now <br> I can no more discern them, though my sight <br> Sweeps the whole Trojan plain from side to side. <br> Either the charioteer has dropped the reins, <br> And could not duly round the goal, or else <br> Met with disaster at the turn, o’erthrown, <br> His chariot broken, and the affrighted mares <br> Darting, unmastered, madly from the way. <br> But rise: look forth yourselves. I cannot well <br> Discern, but think the charioteer is one <br> Who, born of an Aetolian stock, commands <br> Among the Argives⁠—valiant Diomed, <br> A son of Tydeus, tamer of wild steeds.”</p>
<p class="verse">And Ajax, swift of foot, Oileus’ son, <br> Answered with bitter words: “Idomeneus, <br> Why this perpetual prating? Far away <br> The mares with rapid hoofs are traversing <br> The plain, and thou art not the youngest here <br> Among the Argives, nor hast such sharp eyes <br> Beneath thy brows, yet must thou chatter still. <br> Among thy betters here it ill becomes <br> A man like thee to be so free of tongue. <br> The coursers of Eumelus, which at first <br> Outran the rest, are yet before them all, <br> And he is drawing near and holds the reins.”</p>
<p class="verse">The Cretan leader angrily rejoined: <br> “Ajax, thou railer, first in brawls, yet known <br> As in all else below the other Greeks, <br> A man of brutal mood, come, let us stake <br> A tripod or a cauldron, and appoint <br> As umpire Agamemnon, to decide <br> Which horses are the foremost in the race, <br> That when thou losest thou mayst be convinced.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake: Oilean Ajax, swift of foot, <br> Started in anger from his seat, to cast <br> Reproaches back, and long and fierce had been <br> The quarrel if Achilles had not risen, <br> And said: “No longer let this strife go on, <br> Idomeneus and Ajax! Ill such words <br> Become you; ye would blame in other men <br> What now ye do. Sit then among the rest, <br> And watch the race; for soon the charioteers <br> Contending for the victory will be here, <br> And each of you⁠—for well ye know the steeds <br> Of the Greek chieftains⁠—for himself will see <br> Whose hold the second place, and whose are first.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake: Tydides rapidly drew near, <br> Lashing the shoulders of his steeds, and they <br> Seemed in the air as, to complete the course, <br> They flew along, and flung the dust they trod <br> Back on the charioteer. All bright with tin <br> And gold, the car rolled after them; its tires <br> Made but a slender trace in the light dust, <br> So rapidly they ran. And now he stopped <br> Within the circle, while his steeds were steeped <br> In sweat, that fell in drops from neck and breast. <br> Then from his shining seat he leaped, and laid <br> His scourge against the yoke. Brave Sthenelus <br> Came forward, and at once received the prize <br> For Diomed, and bade his comrades lead <br> The maid away, and in their arms bear off <br> The tripod, while himself unyoked the steeds.</p>
<p class="verse">Next the Neleian chief, Antilochus, <br> Came with his coursers. More by fraud than speed <br> He distanced Menelaus, yet that chief <br> Drave his fleet horses near him. Just so far <br> As runs the wheel behind a steed that draws <br> His master swiftly o’er the plain, his tail <br> Touching the tire with its long hairs, and small <br> The space between them as the spacious plain <br> Is traversed, Menelaus just so far as <br> Was distanced by renowned Antilochus. <br> For though at first he fell as far behind <br> As a quoit’s cast, yet was he gaining ground <br> Rapidly, now that Agamemnon’s mare, <br> Aethè the stately-maned, increased her speed, <br> And Menelaus, had the race for both <br> Been longer, would have passed his rival by, <br> Nor left the victory doubtful. After him, <br> A spear’s throw distant, came Meriones, <br> The gallant comrade of Idomeneus, <br> Whose full-maned steeds were slower than the rest, <br> And he unskilled in contests such as these. <br> And last of all Eumelus came. He drew <br> His showy chariot after him, and drave <br> His steeds before him. Great Achilles saw <br> With pity, and from where he stood among <br> The Greeks addressed him thus with wingèd words:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“The ablest horseman brings his steeds the last, <br> But let us, as is just, confer on him <br> The second prize; Tydides takes the first.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake, and all approved his words; and now <br> The mare, to please the Greeks, had been bestowed <br> Upon Eumelus, if Antilochus, <br> Son of magnanimous Nestor, had not risen <br> To plead for justice with Achilles thus:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Achilles, I shall deem it grave offence <br> If thou fulfil thy word; for thou wilt take <br> My prize, because thou seest that this man’s car <br> And his fleet steeds have suffered injury, <br> Though he be skilful. Yet he should have prayed <br> To the good gods; then had he not been seen <br> Bringing his steeds the last. But if thou feel <br> Compassion for him, and if so thou please, <br> Large store of brass and gold is in thy tent, <br> And thine are cattle, and handmaidens thine, <br> And firm-paced steeds; hereafter give of these <br> A nobler largess, or bestow it now, <br> And hear the Greeks applaud thee. But this prize <br> I yield not; let the warrior who may claim <br> To take it try with me his strength of arm.”</p>
<p class="verse">He ceased: the noble son of Peleus smiled, <br> And, pleased to see Antilochus succeed⁠— <br> For he was a beloved friend⁠—he spake <br> These wingèd words: “Since, then, Antilochus, <br> Thou wilt that I bestow some recompense <br> Upon Eumelus from my store, I give <br> The brazen corselet which my arm in war <br> Took from Asteropaeus, edged around <br> With shining tin⁠—a gift of no mean price.”</p>
<p class="verse">He ceased, and sent his friend Automedon <br> To bring it from the tent. He went and brought <br> The corselet, and Eumelus joyfully <br> Received it from Achilles. Then arose, <br> Among them Menelaus, ill at ease, <br> And angry with Antilochus. He took <br> The sceptre from a herald’s hand, who hushed <br> The crowd to silence, and the hero spake:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Antilochus, who wert till now discreet, <br> What hast thou done? Thou hast disgraced my skill <br> And wronged my steeds by thrusting in thine own, <br> Which were less fleet, before them. Now, ye chiefs <br> And leaders of the Achaians, judge between <br> This man and me, and judge impartially, <br> Lest that some warrior of the Greeks should say <br> That Menelaus, having overcome <br> Antilochus by falsehood, led away <br> The mare a prize; for his were slower steeds, <br> But he the mightier man in feats of arms. <br> Nay, I myself will judge; and none of all <br> The Greeks will censure me, for what I do <br> Will be but just. Antilochus, step forth, <br> Illustrious as thou art, and in due form, <br> Standing before thy horses and thy car, <br> And taking in thy hand the pliant scourge <br> Which thou just now hast wielded, touch thy steeds, <br> And swear by Neptune, whose embrace surrounds <br> The earth, that thou hast wittingly employed <br> No stratagem to break my chariot’s speed.”</p>
<p class="verse">And thus discreet Antilochus replied: <br> “Have patience with me: I am younger far <br> Than thou, King Menelaus; thou art both <br> My elder and my better. Thou dost know <br> The faults to which the young are ever prone; <br> The will is quick to act, the judgment weak. <br> Bear with me then. The mare which I received <br> I cheerfully make over to thy hands. <br> And if thou wilt yet more of what I have, <br> I give it willingly and instantly, <br> Rather, O loved of Jove, than lose a place <br> In thy good-will, and sin against the gods.”</p>
<p class="verse">The son of large-souled Nestor, speaking thus, <br> Led forth the mare, and gave her to the hand <br> Of Menelaus, o’er whose spirit came <br> A gladness. As upon a field of wheat <br> Bristling with ears gathers the freshening dew, <br> So was his spirit gladdened in his breast, <br> And he bespake the youth with wingèd words:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Antilochus, now shall my anger cease, <br> For hitherto thou hast not shown thyself <br> Foolish or fickle, though the heat of youth <br> Just now hath led thee wrong. In time to come, <br> Beware to practise stealthy arts on men <br> Of higher rank than thou. No other Greek <br> Would easily have made his peace with me. <br> But thou hast suffered much, and much hast done⁠— <br> Thou, and thy worthy father, and his son, <br> Thy brother⁠—for my sake. I therefore yield <br> To thy petition; yet I give to thee <br> The mare, though mine she be, that these who stand <br> Around us may perceive that I am not <br> Of unforgiving or unyielding mood.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake, and to Noëmon gave the mare⁠— <br> Noëmon, comrade of Antilochus⁠— <br> To lead her thence, while for himself he took <br> The shining cauldron. Then Meriones, <br> Fourth in the race, received the prize of gold⁠— <br> Two talents. But the fifth prize and the last, <br> The double goblet, still was left unclaimed; <br> And this Achilles carried through the crowd <br> Of Greeks, and placed in Nestor’s hands, and said:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Receive thou this, O ancient man, to keep <br> In memory of the funeral honors paid <br> Patroclus, whom thou never more shalt see <br> Among the Greeks. I give this prize, which thou <br> Hast not contended for, since thou wilt wield <br> No more the cestus, nor wilt wrestle more, <br> Nor hurl the javelin at the mark, nor join <br> The foot-race; age lies heavy on thy limbs.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake, and gave the prize, which Nestor took, <br> Well pleased, and thus with wingèd words replied:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Son, thou hast spoken rightly, for these limbs <br> Are strong no longer; neither feet nor hands <br> Move on each side with vigor as of yore. <br> Would I were but as young, with strength as great, <br> As when the Epeians in Buprasium laid <br> King Amarynceus in the sepulchre, <br> And funeral games were offered by his sons! <br> Then of the Epeians there was none like me. <br> Nor of the Pylian youths, nor yet among <br> The brave Aetolians. In the boxing-match <br> I took the prize from Clytomedes, son <br> Of Enops, and in wrestling overcame <br> Ancaeus the Pleuronian, who rose up <br> Against me. In the foot-race I outstripped, <br> Fleet as he was, Iphiclus, and beyond <br> Phyleus and Polydore I threw the spear. <br> Only the sons of Actor won the race <br> Against me with their chariot, and they won <br> Through force of numbers. Much they envied me, <br> And feared lest I should bear away the prize; <br> For largest in that contest of the steeds <br> Was the reward, and they were two⁠—one held, <br> Steadily held, the reins, the other swung <br> The lash. Such was I once. Now feats like these <br> Belong to other, younger men, and I, <br> Though eminent among the heroes once, <br> Must do as sad old age admonishes. <br> Go thou, and honor thy friend’s funeral <br> With games. Thy gift I willingly accept, <br> Rejoicing that thy thoughts revert to one <br> Who loves thee, and that thou forgettest not <br> To pay the honor due to me among <br> The Greeks. The gods will give thee thy reward.”</p>
<p class="verse">He ceased. The son of Peleus, having heard <br> This praise from Nestor, left him, and passed through <br> The mighty concourse of the Greeks. He laid <br> Before them prizes for the difficult strife <br> Between the boxers. To the middle space <br> He led a mule, and bound him, six years old <br> And strong for toil, unbroken and most hard <br> To break, while to the vanquished he assigned <br> A goblet. Rising, he addressed the host:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Ye sons of Atreus and ye well-armed Greeks, <br> We call for two of the most skilled to strive <br> For these, by striking with the lifted fist; <br> And he to whom Apollo shall decree <br> The victory, acknowledged by you all, <br> Shall have this sturdy mule to lead away. <br> The vanquished takes this goblet as his meed.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake. A warrior strong and huge of limb, <br> Skilled in the cestus, named Epeius, son <br> Of Panopeus, rose at the word, and laid <br> His hand upon the sturdy mule, and said:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Let him appear whose lot will be to take <br> The goblet. No man of the Grecian host <br> Will get the mule by overcoming me <br> In combat with the cestus⁠—so I deem. <br> In that I claim to be the best man here. <br> And should it not suffice that in the war <br> Others surpass me? All cannot excel <br> In everything alike. I promise this, <br> And shall fulfil my word⁠—that I will crush <br> His body, and will break his bones. His friend <br> Should all remain upon the ground to bear <br> Their comrade off when beaten by my hand.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake, and all were silent. Only rose <br> Euryalus, whose father was the king <br> Mecisteus of Talaion’s line, the same <br> Who went to Thebes and overcame, of old, <br> In all the funeral games of Oedipus, <br> The sons of Cadmus. To Euryalus <br> Came Diomed, the spearman, bidding him <br> Expect the victory which he greatly wished <br> His friend might gain. Around his waist he drew <br> A girdle, adding straps that from the hide <br> Of a wild bull were cut with dextrous care. <br> And, fully now arrayed, the twain stepped forth <br> Into the middle space, and both began <br> The combat. Lifting their strong arms, they brought <br> Their heavy hands together. Fearfully <br> Was heard the crash of jaws; from every limb <br> The sweat was streaming. As Euryalus <br> Looked round, his noble adversary sprang <br> And smote him on the cheek⁠—too rude a blow <br> To be withstood; his shapely limbs gave way <br> Beneath him. As upon the weedy shore, <br> When the fresh north wind stirs the water’s face, <br> A fish leaps forth to light, and then again <br> The dark wave covers it, so sprang and fell <br> The chief. Magnanimous Epeius gave <br> His hands and raised him up; his friends came round <br> And led him thence with dragging feet, and head <br> That drooped from side to side, while from his mouth <br> Came clotted blood. They placed him in the midst, <br> Unconscious still, and sent and took the cup.</p>
<p class="verse">Then, third in order, for the wrestling-match <br> The son of Peleus brought and showed the Greeks <br> Yet other prizes. To the conqueror <br> A tripod for the hearth, of ample size, <br> He offered; twice six oxen, as the Greeks <br> Esteemed it, were its price. And next he placed <br> In view a damsel for the vanquished, trained <br> In household arts; four beeves were deemed her price.</p>
<p class="verse">Then rose Achilles, and addressed the Greeks: <br> “Ye who would try your fortune in this strife, <br> Arise.” He spake, and mighty Ajax rose, <br> The son of Telamon, and after him <br> The wise Ulysses, trained to stratagems. <br> They, girding up their loins, came forth and stood <br> In the mid space, and there with vigorous arms <br> They clasped each other, locked like rafters framed <br> By some wise builder for the lofty roof <br> Of a great mansion proof against the winds. <br> Then their backs creaked beneath the powerful strain <br> Of their strong hands; the sweat ran down their limbs; <br> Large whelks upon their sides and shoulders rose, <br> Crimson with blood. Still eagerly they strove <br> For victory and the tripod. Yet in vain <br> Ulysses labored to supplant his foe, <br> And throw him to the ground, and equally <br> Did Ajax strive in vain, for with sheer strength <br> Ulysses foiled his efforts. When they saw <br> That the Greeks wearied of the spectacle, <br> The mighty Telamonian Ajax said:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Son of Laertes, nobly born and trained <br> To wise expedients, lift me up, or I <br> Will lift up thee; and leave the rest to Jove.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake, and raised Ulysses from the ground, <br> Who dealt, with ready stratagem, a blow <br> Upon the ham of Ajax, and the limb <br> Gave way; the hero fell upon his back, <br> And on his breast Ulysses, while the host <br> Stood wondering and amazed. Ulysses strove, <br> In turn, to lift his rival, but prevailed <br> Only to move him from his place; he caught <br> The knee of Ajax in his own, and both <br> Came to the ground together, soiled with dust. <br> They rose to wrestle still, but from his seat <br> Achilles started, and forbade them thus:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Contend no longer, nor exhaust your strength <br> With struggling; there is victory for both, <br> And equal prizes. Now depart, and leave <br> The field of contest to the other Greeks.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake: they listened and obeyed, and wiped <br> The dust away, and put their garments on. <br> And then the son of Peleus placed in sight <br> Prizes of swiftness⁠—a wrought silver cup <br> That held six measures, and in beauty far <br> Excelled all others known; the cunning hands <br> Of the Sidonian artisans had given <br> Its graceful shape, and over the dark sea <br> Men of Phoenicia brought it, with their wares, <br> To the Greek harbors; they bestowed it there <br> On Thoas. Afterward Euneüs, son <br> Of Jason, gave it to the hero-chief, <br> Patroclus, to redeem a captive friend, <br> Lycaon, Priam’s son. Achilles now <br> Brought it before the assembly as a prize, <br> For which, in honor of the friend he loved, <br> The swiftest runners of the host should strive. <br> Next, for the second in the race, he showed <br> A noble fatling ox; and for the last, <br> Gold, half a talent. Then he stood and said <br> To the Achaians: “Those who would contend <br> For these rewards, rise up.” And then arose <br> Oilean Ajax, fleet of foot; and next <br> Ulysses the sagacious; last upstood <br> Antilochus, the son of Nestor, known <br> As swiftest of the youths. In due array <br> They stood; Achilles showed the goal. At once <br> Forward they sprang. Oilean Ajax soon <br> Gained on the rest, but close behind him ran <br> The great Ulysses. As a shapely maid <br> Flinging the shuttle draws with careful hand <br> The thread that fills the warp, and so brings near <br> The shuttle to her bosom, just so near <br> To Ajax ran Ulysses, in the prints <br> Made by his rival’s feet, before the dust <br> Fell back upon them. As he ran, his breath <br> Smote on the head of Ajax. All the Greeks <br> Shouted applause to him, encouraging <br> His ardor for the victory; but when now <br> They neared the goal, Ulysses silently <br> Prayed thus to Pallas: “Goddess, hear my prayer, <br> And help these feet to win.” The goddess heard, <br> And lightened all his limbs, his feet, his hands; <br> And just as they were rushing on the prize, <br> Ajax, in running, slipped and fell⁠—the work <br> Of Pallas⁠—where in heaps the refuse lay <br> From entrails of the bellowing oxen slain <br> In honor of Patroclus by the hand <br> Of swift Achilles. Mouth and nostrils both <br> Were choked with filth. The much-enduring man <br> Ulysses, coming first, received the cup, <br> While Ajax took the ox, and as he stood <br> Holding the animal’s horn and spitting forth <br> The dirt, he said to those around: “ ’Tis plain <br> The goddess caused my feet to slide; she aids <br> Ulysses like a mother.” So he said, <br> And the Greeks laughed. And then Antilochus <br> Received the third reward, and with a smile <br> Said to the Greeks: “I tell you all, my friends, <br> What you must know already, that the gods <br> Honor the aged ever. Ajax stands <br> Somewhat in years above me, but this chief <br> Who takes the prize is of a former age <br> And earlier race of men; they call him old, <br> But hard it were for any Greek to vie <br> With him in swiftness, save Achilles here.”</p>
<p class="verse">Such praise he gave Pelides, fleet of foot, <br> Who answered: “Thy good word, Antilochus, <br> Shall not be vainly spoken. I will add <br> Yet half a talent to thy gold.” He said, <br> And gave the gold; Antilochus, well pleased, <br> Received it. Then Pelides brought a spear <br> Of ponderous length into the middle space, <br> And laid it down, and placed a buckler near <br> And helmet, which had been Sarpedon’s arms, <br> And which Patroclus won of him in war. <br> Then stood Achilles and addressed the Greeks:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“I call on two, the bravest of the host, <br> To arm themselves and take their spears in hand, <br> And in a contest for these weapons put <br> Each other to the proof. Whoever first <br> Shall wound his adversary, piercing through <br> The armor to the delicate skin beneath, <br> And draw the crimson blood, to him I give <br> This beautiful sword of Thrace, with silver studs, <br> Won from Asteropaeus. And let both <br> Bear off these arms, a common gift, and both <br> Shall sit and banquet nobly in my tent.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake, and Telamonian Ajax rose, <br> The large of limb; Tydides Diomed, <br> The strong, rose also. When they had put on <br> Their arms apart from all the host, they came, <br> All eager for the combat, to the lists, <br> And fearful was their aspect. All the Greeks <br> Looked on with dread and wonder, and when now <br> Stood face to face the warriors, thrice they rushed <br> Against each other; thrice they dealt their blows. <br> Then Ajax thrust through Diomed’s round shield <br> His weapon, but it wounded not; the mail <br> Beyond it stopped the stroke. Tydides aimed <br> Over his adversary’s mighty shield <br> A blow to reach his neck. The Greeks, alarmed <br> For Ajax, shouted that the strife should cease, <br> And both divide the prize. Achilles heard, <br> But gave to Diomed the ponderous sword, <br> Its sheath, and the fair belt from which it hung.</p>
<p class="verse">Again Pelides placed before the host <br> A mass of iron, shapeless from the forge, <br> Which once the strong Eëtion used to hurl; <br> But swift Achilles, when he took his life, <br> Brought it with other booty in his ships <br> To Troas. Rising, he addressed the Greeks:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Stand forth, whoever will contend for this, <br> And if broad fields and rich be his, this mass <br> Will last him many years. The man who tends <br> His flocks, or guides his plough, need not be sent <br> To town for iron; he will have it here.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake, and warlike Polypoetes rose. <br> Uprose the strong Leonteus, who in form <br> Was like a god. The son of Telamon <br> Rose also, and Epeius nobly born; <br> Each took his place. Epeius seized the mass, <br> And sent it whirling. All the Achaians laughed. <br> The loved of Mars, Leonteus, flung it next, <br> And after him the son of Telamon, <br> The large-limbed Ajax, from his vigorous arm <br> Sent it beyond the mark of both. But when <br> The sturdy warrior Polypoetes took <br> The mass in hand, as far as o’er his beeves <br> A herdsman sends his whirling staff, so far <br> This cast outdid the rest. A shout arose; <br> The friends of sturdy Polypoetes took <br> The prize, and bore it to the hollow ships.</p>
<p class="verse">Achilles for the archers brought forth steel, <br> Tempered for arrow-heads⁠—ten axes, each <br> With double edge, and single axes ten⁠— <br> And from a galley’s azure prow took off <br> A mast, and reared it on the sands afar, <br> And, tying to its summit by the foot <br> A timorous dove, he bade them aim at her: <br> “Whoever strikes the bird shall bear away <br> The double axes to his tent; while he <br> Who hits the cord, but not the bird, shall take <br> The single axes, as the humbler prize.”</p>
<p class="verse">He ceased, and then arose the stalwart king, <br> Teucer; then also rose Meriones, <br> The valiant comrade of Idomeneus. <br> The lots were shaken in a brazen helm, <br> And Teucer’s lot was first. He straightway sent <br> A shaft with all his strength, but made no vow <br> Of a choice hecatomb of firstling lambs <br> To Phoebus, monarch-god. He missed the bird, <br> Such was the will of Phoebus, but he struck, <br> Close to her foot, the cord that made her fast. <br> The keen shaft severed it; the dove flew up <br> Into the heavens; the fillet dropped to earth <br> Amid the loud applauses of the Greeks. <br> And then Meriones made haste to take <br> The bow from Teucer’s hand. Long time he held <br> The arrow aimed, the while he made a vow <br> To Phoebus, the great archer, promising <br> A chosen hecatomb of firstling lambs; <br> Then, looking toward the dove, as high in air <br> She wheeled beneath the clouds, he pierced her breast <br> Beneath the wing; the shaft went through and fell, <br> Fixed in the ground, beside Meriones, <br> While the bird settled on the galley’s mast <br> With drooping head and open wings. The breath <br> Forsook her soon, and down from that high perch <br> She fell to earth. The people all looked on, <br> Admiring and amazed. Meriones <br> Took up the double axes as his prize, <br> While Teucer bore the others to the fleet.</p>
<p class="verse">And then Pelides brought into the midst <br> A ponderous spear, and laid a cauldron down <br> Which never felt the fire, inwrought with flowers, <br> Its price an ox. And then the spearmen rose. <br> Atrides Agamemnon, mighty king, <br> First rose, and after him Meriones, <br> The brave companion of Idomeneus; <br> And thus to both the swift Achilles said:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“O son of Atreus, for we know how far <br> Thou dost excel all others, and dost cast <br> The spear with passing strength and skill, bear thou <br> This prize, as victor, to the roomy ships, <br> And if it please thee, let us, as I wish, <br> Give to our brave Meriones the spear.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake, and Agamemnon, king of men, <br> Complied, and gave Meriones in hand <br> The brazen spear, while to Talthybius, <br> The herald, he consigned the greater prize.</p>
