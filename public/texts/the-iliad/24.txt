<p class="lede">A council of the gods⁠—Thetis sent to make Achilles willing to restore the body of Hector to his friends⁠—Iris sent to Priam, bidding him go in person to Achilles and ask for the body⁠—Visit of Priam made by night to the tent of Achilles, who is moved by his entreaties and magnificent presents to deliver up the remains of his son⁠—Departure of Priam by night with the body from the tent of Achilles⁠—Lament of Andromache, Hector, and Helen over the dead⁠—The funeral of Hector, with which the poem closes.</p>
<p class="verse">The assembly was dissolved, the people all <br> Dispersed to their swift galleys, and prepared <br> With food and gentle slumber to refresh <br> Their wearied frames. But still Achilles wept, <br> Remembering his dear comrade. Sleep, whose sway <br> Is over all, came not; he turned and tossed, <br> Still yearning for his strong and valiant friend <br> Patroclus. All that they had ever done <br> Together, all the hardships they had borne, <br> The battles fought with heroes, the wild seas <br> O’erpassed, came thronging on his memory. <br> He shed warm tears, as now upon his sides, <br> Now on his back, now on his face he lay. <br> Then, starting from his couch, he wandered forth <br> In sorrow by the margin of the deep. <br> Nor did the morn that rose o’er sea and shore <br> Dawn unperceived by him; for then he yoked <br> His fleet steeds to the chariot, and made fast <br> The corse of Hector, that it might be dragged <br> After the wheels. Three times around the tomb <br> Of Menoetiades he dragged the slain, <br> Then turned and sought his tent, again to rest, <br> And left him there stretched out amid the dust <br> With the face downward. Yet Apollo, moved <br> With pity for the hero, kept him free <br> From soil or stain, though dead, and o’er him held <br> The golden aegis, lest, when roughly dragged <br> Along the ground, the body might be torn.</p>
<p class="verse">So in his anger did Achilles treat <br> Unworthily the noble Hector’s corse. <br> The blessed gods themselves with pity looked <br> Upon the slain, and bade the vigilant one, <br> The Argus-queller, bear him thence by stealth. <br> This counsel pleased the immortals all, except <br> Juno and Neptune and the blue-eyed maid, <br> And these persisted in their wrath. To them <br> Ilium, the hallowed city, and its king, <br> Priam, and all his people, from the first <br> Were hateful; ’twas for Alexander’s fault, <br> Affronting the two goddesses what time <br> They sought his cottage, and preferring her <br> Who ministered to his calamitous love. <br> But now, when the twelfth morning from that day <br> Arose, Apollo spake among the gods:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Cruel are ye, O gods, and prone to wrong. <br> For was not Hector wont before your shrines <br> To burn the thighs of chosen bulls and goats? <br> And now that he is dead ye venture not <br> To rescue him, and let his wife and son <br> And mother and King Priam look again <br> Upon his face. Soon would they light the pile, <br> And burn the dead, and pay the funeral rite. <br> Ye seek to favor, O ye gods, that pest <br> Achilles, in whose breast there dwells no love <br> Of justice, nor a temper to be moved <br> By prayers, but who delights in savage deeds. <br> And as a lion, conscious of vast strength <br> And scornful of resistance, falls upon <br> The shepherd’s flock, and slays for his repast, <br> Thus with Achilles neither mercy dwells <br> Nor shame, which often profits, often harms <br> Mankind. For when another man has met <br> A greater grief than he⁠—has lost, perchance, <br> A brother or a son⁠—he dries at length <br> His tears, and ceases to lament; for fate <br> Bestows the power to suffer patiently. <br> But this Achilles, after he has spoiled <br> The godlike Hector of his life in war, <br> Hath bound him to his chariot, and hath dragged <br> The corse around his dear companion’s tomb. <br> Unseemly is the deed, and small will be <br> The good it brings him. Brave although he be, <br> We may be angry with him when he thus <br> Insults a portion of insensible earth.”</p>
<p class="verse">The white-armed Juno was incensed, and spake: <br> “So mightst thou say, God of the silver bow, <br> Were equal honor to Achilles due <br> And Hector. Hector is a mortal man, <br> And suckled at a woman’s breast. Not so <br> Achilles; he was born of one of us, <br> A goddess whom I nurtured and brought up <br> And gave to Peleus. Ye were present all, <br> Ye gods, when they were wedded. Thou wert there <br> To share the marriage banquet, harp in hand, <br> Thou plotter with the vile, thou faithless one!”</p>
<p class="verse">Then answered cloud-compelling Jove, and said: <br> “Let not thy anger rise against the gods, <br> O Juno, for the honor of the chiefs <br> Shall not be equal. Yet of all the race <br> Of mortals dwelling in the city of Troy <br> Was Hector dearest to the gods; to me <br> He ever was; and never did he fail <br> To offer welcome gifts. My altar ne’er <br> Lacked fitting feast, libation, and the fume <br> Of incense⁠—hallowed rites which are our due. <br> Yet seek we not to steal away the corse <br> Of valiant Hector; that we could not do <br> Without his slayer’s knowledge, who by night <br> And day is ever near to him and keeps <br> Watch o’er him like a mother. Let some god <br> Call hither Thetis. I will counsel her <br> Prudently, that Achilles may receive <br> Ransom from Priam, and restore his son.”</p>
<p class="verse">He ceased, and with the swiftness of the storm <br> Rose Iris up, to be his messenger. <br> Half way ’twixt Samos and the rugged coast <br> Of Imbrus down she plunged to the dark sea, <br> Entering the deep with noise. Far down she sank <br> As sinks the ball of lead, that, sliding o’er <br> A wild bull’s horn, bears into ocean’s depths <br> Death to the greedy fishes. There she found <br> Thetis within her roomy cave, among <br> The goddesses of ocean, seated round <br> In full assembly. Thetis in the midst <br> Bewailed the fate of her own blameless son, <br> About to perish on the fertile soil <br> Of Troy, and far from Greece. The swift of wing, <br> Iris, approached her and addressed her thus:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Arise, O Thetis. Father Jupiter, <br> Whose counsel stands forever, sends for thee.”</p>
<p class="verse">And silver-footed Thetis answered him: <br> “Why should that potent deity require <br> My presence, who have many griefs, and shrink <br> From mingling with immortals? Yet I go, <br> Perforce, for never doth he speak in vain.”</p>
<p class="verse">So spake the goddess-queen, and, speaking, took <br> Her mantle⁠—darker web was never worn⁠— <br> And onward went. Wind-footed Iris led <br> The way; the waters of the sea withdrew <br> On either side. They climbed the steepy shore, <br> And took their way to heaven. They found the son <br> Of Saturn, him of the far-sounding voice, <br> With all the blessed, ever-living gods <br> Assembled round him. Close to Father Jove <br> She took her seat, for Pallas yielded it, <br> And Juno put a beautiful cup of gold <br> Into her hand, and spake consoling words. <br> She drank and gave it back, and thus began <br> The father of immortals and of men:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Thou comest to Olympus, though in grief, <br> O goddess Thetis, and I know the cause <br> That makes thee sad and will not from thy thoughts; <br> Yet let me now declare why I have called <br> Thee hither. For nine days the immortal gods <br> Have been at strife concerning Hector’s corse <br> And Peleus’ son, the spoiler. They have asked <br> The vigilant Argus-queller to remove <br> The dead by stealth. But I must yet bestow <br> Fresh honor on Achilles, and thus keep <br> Thy love and reverence. Now descend at once <br> Into the camp and carry to thy son <br> My message: say that it offends the gods, <br> And me the most, that in his spite he keeps <br> The corse of Hector at the beaked ships, <br> Refusing to restore it. He perchance <br> Will listen, and, revering me, give back <br> The slain. And I will send a messenger, <br> Iris, to large-souled Priam, bidding him <br> Hasten in person to the Grecian fleet, <br> To ransom his beloved son, and bring <br> Achilles gifts that shall appease his rage.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake: the goddess of the silver feet, <br> Thetis, obeyed, and with precipitate flight <br> Descended from the mountain-peaks. She came <br> To her son’s tent, and found him uttering moans <br> Continually, while his beloved friends <br> Were busy round him; they prepared a feast, <br> And had just slain within the tent a ewe <br> Of ample size and fleece. She took her seat <br> Beside her son, and smoothed his brow, and said:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“How long, my son, wilt thou lament and grieve <br> And pine at heart, abstaining from the feast <br> And from thy couch? Yet well it is to seek <br> A woman’s love. Thy life will not be spared <br> Long time to me, for death and cruel fate <br> Stand near thee. Listen to me; I am come <br> A messenger from Jove, who bids me say <br> The immortals are offended, and himself <br> The most, that thou shouldst in thy spite detain <br> The corse of Hector at the beaked ships, <br> Refusing its release. Comply thou then, <br> And take the ransom and restore the dead.”</p>
<p class="verse">And thus Achilles, swift of foot, replied: <br> “Let him who brings the ransom come and take <br> The body, if it be the will of Jove.”</p>
<p class="verse">Thus did the mother and the son confer <br> Among the galleys, and between them passed <br> Full many a wingèd word, while Saturn’s son <br> Bade Iris go with speed to sacred Troy:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Fleet Iris, haste thee. Leave the Olympian seats, <br> And send magnanimous Priam to the fleet, <br> To ransom his dear son, and bear him back <br> To Ilium. Let him carry gifts to calm <br> The anger of Achilles. He should go <br> Alone, no Trojan with him, save a man <br> In years, a herald, who may guide the mules <br> And strong-wheeled chariot, harnessed to bear back <br> Him whom the great Achilles has o’erthrown; <br> And let him fear not death nor other harm, <br> For we will send a guide to lead him safe, <br> The Argus-queller, till he stand beside <br> Achilles; and when once he comes within <br> The warrior’s tent, Achilles will not raise <br> His hand to slay, but will restrain the rest. <br> Nor mad, nor rash, nor criminal is he, <br> And will humanely spare a suppliant man.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake, and Iris, the swift messenger, <br> Whose feet are like the wind, went forth with speed, <br> And came to Priam’s palace, where she found <br> Sorrow and wailing. Round the father sat <br> His sons within the hall, and steeped with tears <br> Their garments. In the midst the aged man <br> Sat with a cloak wrapped round him, and much dust <br> Strewn on his head and neck, which, when he rolled <br> Upon the earth, he gathered with his hands. <br> His daughters and the consorts of his sons <br> Filled with their cries the mansion, sorrowing <br> For those, the many and brave, who now lay slain <br> By Grecian hands. The ambassadress of Jove <br> Stood beside Priam, and in soft, low tones, <br> While his limbs shook with fear, addressed him thus:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Be comforted, and have no fear; for I <br> Am come, Dardanian Priam, not to bring <br> Mischief, but blessing. I am sent to thee <br> A messenger from Jove, who, though afar, <br> Pities thee and will aid thee. He who rules <br> Olympus bids thee ransom thy slain son, <br> The noble Hector, carrying gifts to calm <br> The anger of Achilles. Thou shouldst go <br> Alone, no Trojan with thee, save a man <br> In years, a herald, who shall guide the mules <br> And strong-wheeled chariot, harnessed to bring back <br> Him whom the great Achilles has o’erthrown. <br> And have no fear of death or other harm; <br> A guide shall go with thee to lead thee safe, <br> The Argus-queller, till thou stand beside <br> Achilles, and when once thou art within <br> The warrior’s tent, Achilles will not raise <br> His hand to slay, but will restrain the rest. <br> He is not mad, nor rash, nor prone to crime, <br> And will humanely spare a suppliant man.”</p>
<p class="verse">Thus the swift-footed Iris spake, and then <br> Departed. Priam bade his sons prepare <br> The strong-wheeled chariot, drawn by mules, and bind <br> A coffer on it. He descended next <br> Into a fragrant chamber, cedar-lined, <br> High-roofed, and stored with many things of price, <br> And calling Hecuba, his wife, he said:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Dear wife, a message from Olympian Jove <br> Commands that I betake me to the fleet, <br> And thence redeem my slaughtered son with gifts <br> That may appease Achilles. Tell me now <br> How this may seem to thee? for I am moved <br> By a strong impulse to approach the ships, <br> And venture into the great Grecian camp.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake: his consort wept, and answered thus: <br> “Ah me! The prudence which was once so praised <br> By strangers and by those who own thy sway, <br> Where is it now? Why wouldst thou go alone <br> To the Greek fleet, to meet the eye of him <br> Who slew so many of thy gallant sons? <br> An iron heart is thine. If that false man, <br> Remorseless as he is, should see thee there <br> And seize thee, neither pity nor respect <br> Hast thou to hope from him. Let us lament <br> Our Hector in these halls. A cruel fate <br> Spun, when I brought him forth, his thread of life⁠— <br> That far from us his corse should feed the hounds <br> Near that fierce man, whose liver I could tear <br> From out his bosom. Then the indignities <br> Done to my son would be repaid, for he <br> Was slain, not shunning combat, coward-like, <br> But fighting to defend the men of Troy <br> And the deep-bosomed Trojan dames. He fell <br> Without a thought of flight or of retreat.”</p>
<p class="verse">And thus the aged, godlike king rejoined: <br> “Keep me not back from going, nor be thou <br> A bird of evil omen in these halls, <br> For thou shalt not persuade me. This I say: <br> If any of the dwellers of the earth, <br> Soothsayer, seer, or priest, had said to me <br> What I have heard, I well might deem the words <br> A lie, and heed them not. But since I heard <br> Myself the mandate from a deity, <br> And saw her face to face, I certainly <br> Will go, nor shall the message be in vain. <br> And should it be my fate to perish there <br> Beside the galleys of the mail-clad Greeks, <br> So be it; for Achilles will forthwith <br> Put me to death embracing my poor son, <br> And satisfying my desire to weep.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake, and, raising the fair coffer-lids, <br> Took out twelve robes of state most beautiful, <br> Twelve single cloaks, as many tapestried mats, <br> And tunics next and mantles twelve of each, <br> And ten whole talents of pure gold, which first <br> He weighed. Two burnished tripods from his store <br> He added, and four goblets and a cup <br> Of eminent beauty, which the men of Thrace <br> Gave him when, as an envoy to their coast, <br> He came from Troy⁠—a sumptuous gift, and yet <br> The aged king reserved not even this <br> To deck his palace, such was his desire <br> To ransom his dear son. And then he drave <br> Away the Trojans hovering round his porch, <br> Rebuking them with sharp and bitter words:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Hence with you, worthless wretches! Have ye not <br> Sorrow enough at home, that ye are come <br> To vex me thus? Or doth it seem to you <br> Of little moment, that Saturnian Jove <br> Hath sent such grief upon me in the loss <br> Of my most valiant son? Ye yet will know <br> How great that loss has been; for it will be <br> A lighter task for the beleaguering Greeks <br> To work our ruin, now that he is dead. <br> But I shall sink to Hades ere mine eyes <br> Behold the city sacked and made a spoil.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake, and with his staff he chased away <br> The loiterers; forth before the aged man <br> They went. With like harsh words he chid his sons. <br> Helenus, Paris, noble Agathon, <br> Pammon, Antiphonus, Deïphobus, <br> Polites, great in war, Hippothoüs, <br> And gallant Dios, nine in all he called, <br> And thus bespake them with reproachful words:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Make haste, ye idle fellows, my disgrace! <br> Would ye had all been slain beside the fleet <br> Instead of Hector! Woe is me! The most <br> Unhappy of mankind am I, who had <br> The bravest sons in all the town of Troy, <br> And none of them, I think, are left to me. <br> Mestor, divine in presence, Troilus, <br> The gallant knight, and Hector, he who looked <br> A god among his countrymen⁠—no son <br> Of man he seemed, but of immortal birth⁠— <br> Those Mars has slain, but these who are my shame <br> Remain⁠—these liars, dancers, excellent <br> In choirs, whose trade is public robbery <br> Of lambs and kids. Why haste ye not to get <br> My chariot ready, and bestow these things <br> Within it, that my journey may begin?”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake, and they, in fear of his rebuke, <br> Lifted from out its place the strong-wheeled car, <br> Framed to be drawn by mules, and beautiful, <br> And newly built, and on it they made fast <br> The coffer. From its pin they next took down <br> The boxwood mule-yoke, fitted well with rings, <br> And carved with a smooth boss. With this they brought <br> A yoke-band nine ells long, which carefully <br> Adjusting to the polished pole’s far end, <br> They cast the ring upon the bolt, and thrice <br> Wound the long band on each side of the bolt <br> Around the yoke, and made it fast, and turned <br> The loose ends under. Then they carried forth <br> The treasures that should ransom Hector’s corse; <br> And having piled them in the polished car, <br> They yoked the hardy, strong-hoofed mules which once <br> The Mysians gave to Priam, princely gifts. <br> To bear the yoke of Priam they led forth <br> The horses which the aged man himself <br> Fed at the polished manger. These the king <br> Yoked, aided by the herald, while in mind, <br> Within the palace court, they both revolved <br> Their prudent counsels. Hecuba, the queen, <br> Came to them in deep sorrow. In her hand <br> She bore a golden cup of delicate wine, <br> That they might make libations and depart. <br> She stood before the steeds, and thus she spake:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Take this, and pour to Father Jove, and pray <br> That thou mayst safely leave the enemy’s camp <br> For home, since ’tis thy will, though I dissuade, <br> To go among the ships. Implore thou then <br> The god of Ida and the gatherer <br> Of the black tempest, Saturn’s son, who looks <br> Down on all Troy, to send his messenger, <br> His swift and favorite bird, of matchless strength, <br> On thy right hand, that, with thine eye on him, <br> Thou mayst with courage journey to the ships <br> Of the Greek horsemen. But if Jupiter <br> All-seeing should withhold his messenger, <br> I cannot bid thee, eager as thou art, <br> Adventure near the galleys of the Greeks.”</p>
<p class="verse">And thus the godlike Priam made reply: <br> “Dear wife, indeed, I will not disobey <br> Thy counsel; meet it is to raise our hands <br> To Jove, and ask him to be merciful.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake, and bade the attendant handmaid pour <br> Pure water on his hands, for near him stood <br> A maid who came and held a basin forth <br> And ewer. When his hands were washed, he took <br> The goblet from the queen, and then, in prayer, <br> Stood in the middle of the court, and poured <br> The wine, and, looking heavenward, spake aloud:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“O Father Jove, most glorious and most great, <br> Who rulest all from Ida, let me find <br> Favor and pity with Achilles. Send <br> A messenger, thy own swift, favorite bird, <br> Of matchless strength, on my right hand, that I, <br> Beholding him, may confidently pass <br> To where the fleet of the Greek horsemen lies!”</p>
<p class="verse">Thus in his prayer he spake, and Jupiter, <br> The All-disposer, hearkened, and sent forth <br> An eagle, bird of surest augury, <br> Named the Black Chaser, and by others called <br> Percnos, with wings as broad as is the door <br> Skilfully fashioned for the lofty hall <br> Of some rich man, and fastened with a bolt. <br> Such ample wings he spread on either side <br> As townward on the right they saw him fly. <br> They saw and they rejoiced; their hearts grew light <br> Within their bosoms. Then the aged king <br> Hastened to mount the polished car, and drave <br> Through vestibule and echoing porch. The mules, <br> Harnessed to draw the four-wheeled car, went first, <br> Driven by the sage Idaeus; after them, <br> The horses, urged by Priam with the lash <br> Rapidly through the city. All his friends <br> Followed lamenting, as for one who went <br> To meet his death. And now when they had reached <br> The plain descending from the town, the sons <br> And sons-in-law of Priam all returned <br> To Ilium, and the twain proceeded on, <br> Yet not unmarked by all-beholding Jove, <br> Who, moved with pity for the aged man, <br> Turned to his well-beloved son and said:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Hermes, who more than any other god <br> Delightest to consort with human kind, <br> And willingly dost listen to their prayers, <br> Haste, guide King Priam to the Grecian fleet, <br> Yet so that none may see him, and no Greek <br> Know of his coming, till he stand before <br> Pelides.” Thus he spake: the messenger <br> Who slew the Argus hearkened and obeyed; <br> And hastily beneath his feet he bound <br> The fair, ambrosial, golden sandals worn <br> To bear him over ocean like the wind, <br> And o’er the boundless land. His wand he took <br> Wherewith he seals in sleep the eyes of men, <br> And opens them at will. With this in hand, <br> The mighty Argus-queller flew, and soon <br> Was at the Troad and the Hellespont. <br> Like to some royal stripling seemed the god, <br> In youth’s first prime, when youth has most of grace. <br> And there the Trojans twain, when they had passed <br> The tomb of Ilus, halted with their mules <br> And horses, that the beasts might drink the stream; <br> For twilight now was creeping o’er the earth. <br> The herald looked, and saw that Mercury <br> Was near, and thus, addressing Priam, said:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Be on thy guard, O son of Dardanus, <br> For here is cause for wariness. I see <br> A warrior, and I think he seeks our lives. <br> Now let us urge our steeds and fly, or else <br> Descend and clasp his knees, and sue for grace.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake, and greatly was the aged king <br> Bewildered by his words; with hair erect <br> He stood, and motionless, while Mercury <br> Drew near, and took the old man’s hand, and asked:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Whither, O father, guidest thou thy mules <br> And steeds in the dim night, while others sleep? <br> Fearest thou nothing from the warlike Greeks, <br> Thy foes, who hate thee, and are near at hand? <br> Should one of them behold thee bearing off <br> These treasures in the swiftly darkening night, <br> What wouldst thou do? Thou art not young, and he <br> Who comes with thee is old; ye could not make <br> Defence against the foe. Fear nought from me, <br> And I will save thee, since thou art so like <br> To my own father, from all other harm.”</p>
<p class="verse">Priam, the godlike ancient, answered thus: <br> “Thou sayest true, dear son; but sure some god <br> Holds over me his kind, protecting hand, <br> Who sends a guide like thee to join me here, <br> So noble art thou both in form and air, <br> And gracious are thy thoughts, and blessed they <br> Who gave thee birth.” With that the messenger, <br> The Argus-queller, spake again, and said: <br> “Most wisely hast thou spoken, aged man. <br> But tell, and truly, why thou bearest hence <br> This store of treasures among stranger men? <br> Is it that they may be preserved for thee? <br> Or are ye all deserting in alarm <br> Your hallowed Troy? for such a man of might <br> Was thy brave son who died, that I may say <br> The Greeks in battle had no braver man.”</p>
<p class="verse">And Priam, godlike ancient, spake in turn: <br> “Who then art thou, and of what parents born, <br> Excellent youth, who dost in such kind words <br> Speak of the death of my unhappy son?”</p>
<p class="verse">The herald, Argus-queller, answered him: <br> “I see that thou wouldst prove me, aged man, <br> By questions touching Hector, whom I oft <br> Have seen with mine own eyes in glorious fight, <br> Putting the Greeks to rout and slaying them <br> By their swift ships with that sharp spear of his. <br> We stood and marvelled, for Achilles, wroth <br> With Agamemnon, would not suffer us <br> To join the combat. I attend on him; <br> The same good galley brought us to this shore, <br> And I am one among his Myrmidons. <br> Polyctor is my father, who is rich, <br> And now as old as thou. Six are his sons <br> Beside me, I the seventh. In casting lots <br> With them, it fell to me that I should come <br> To Ilium with Achilles. I am here <br> In coming from the fleet, for with the dawn <br> The dark-eyed Greeks are planning to renew <br> The war around the city. They have grown <br> Impatient of long idleness; their chiefs <br> Seek vainly to restrain their warlike rage.”</p>
<p class="verse">Then spake the godlike ancient, Priam, thus: <br> “If thou indeed dost serve Pelides, tell, <br> And truly tell me, whether yet my son <br> Is at the fleet, or has Achilles cast, <br> Torn limb from limb, his body to the hounds?”</p>
<p class="verse">The herald, Argus-queller, thus replied: <br> “O aged monarch, neither have the hounds <br> Devoured thy son, nor yet the birds of prey; <br> But near the galleys of Achilles still <br> He lies neglected and among the tents. <br> Twelve mornings have beheld him lying there, <br> Nor hath corruption touched him, nor the worms <br> That make the slain their feast begun to feed. <br> ’Tis true that, when the holy morning dawns <br> Achilles drags him fiercely round the tomb <br> Of his dear friend; yet that disfigures not <br> The dead. Shouldst thou approach him, thou wouldst see <br> With marvelling eyes how fresh and dewy still <br> The body lies, the blood all cleansed away, <br> Unsoiled in every part, and all the wounds <br> Closed up wherever made; for many a spear <br> Was thrust into his sides. Thus tenderly <br> The blessed gods regard thy son, though dead, <br> For dearly was he loved by them in life.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake; the aged man was comforted, <br> And said: “ ’Tis meet, O son, that we should pay <br> Oblations to the immortals; for my son <br> While yet alive neglected not within <br> His palace the due worship of the gods <br> Who dwell upon Olympus; therefore they <br> Are mindful of him, even after death. <br> Take this magnificent goblet; be my guard, <br> And guide me, by the favor of the gods, <br> Until I reach Pelides in his tent.”</p>
<p class="verse">Again the herald, Argus-queller, spake: <br> “Thou seekest yet to try me, aged man, <br> Who younger am than thou. Yet think thou not <br> That I, without the knowledge of my chief, <br> Will take thy gifts; for in my heart I fear <br> Achilles, nor would wrong him in the least, <br> Lest evil come upon me. Yet I go <br> Willingly with thee, as thy faithful guide. <br> Were it as far as Argos the renowned, <br> In a swift galley, or on foot by land, <br> Yet none would dare to harm thee while with me.”</p>
<p class="verse">So Hermes spake, and leaped into the car, <br> And took into his hands the lash and reins, <br> And breathed into the horses and the mules <br> Fresh vigor. Coming to the wall and trench <br> About the ships, they found the guard engaged <br> With their night-meal. The herald Argicide <br> Poured sleep upon them all, and quickly flung <br> The gates apart, and pushed aside the bars, <br> And led in Priam, with the costly gifts <br> Heaped on the car. They went until they reached <br> The lofty tent in which Achilles sat, <br> Reared by the Myrmidons to lodge their king, <br> With timbers of hewn fir, and over-roofed <br> With thatch, for which the meadows had been mown, <br> And fenced for safety round with rows of stakes. <br> One fir-tree bar made fast its gate, which three <br> Strong Greeks were wont to raise aloft, and three <br> Were needed to take down the massive beam. <br> Achilles wielded the vast weight alone; <br> Beneficent Hermes opened it before <br> The aged man, and brought the treasures in, <br> Designed for swift Achilles. Then he left <br> The car and stood upon the ground, and said:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“O aged monarch, I am Mercury, <br> An ever-living god; my father, Jove, <br> Bade me attend thy journey. I shall now <br> Return, nor must Achilles look on me; <br> It is not meet that an immortal god <br> Should openly befriend a mortal man. <br> Enter, approach Pelides, clasp his knees; <br> Entreat him by his father, and his son, <br> And fair-haired mother; so shall he be moved.”</p>
<p class="verse">Thus having spoken, Hermes took his way <br> Back to the Olympian summit. Priam then <br> Sprang from the chariot to the ground. He left <br> Idaeus there to guard the steeds and mules, <br> And, hastening to the tent where, dear to Jove, <br> Achilles lodged, he found the chief within, <br> While his companions sat apart, save two⁠— <br> Automedon the brave, and Alcimus, <br> Who claimed descent from Mars. These stood near by, <br> And ministered to Peleus’ son, who then <br> Was closing a repast, and had just left <br> The food and wine, and still the table stood. <br> Unmarked the royal Priam entered in, <br> And, coming to Achilles, clasped his knees, <br> And kissed those fearful slaughter-dealing hands, <br> By which so many of his sons had died. <br> And as, when some blood-guilty man, whose hand <br> In his own land has slain a fellow-man, <br> Flees to another country, and the abode <br> Of some great chieftain, all men look on him <br> Astonished⁠—so, when godlike Priam first <br> Was seen, Achilles was amazed, and all <br> Looked on each other, wondering at the sight. <br> And thus King Priam supplicating spake:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Think of thy father, an old man like me, <br> Godlike Achilles! On the dreary verge <br> Of closing life he stands, and even now <br> Haply is fiercely pressed by those who dwell <br> Around him, and has none to shield his age <br> From war and its disasters. Yet his heart <br> Rejoices when he hears thou yet dost live, <br> And every day he hopes that his dear son <br> Will come again from Troy. My lot is hard, <br> For I was father of the bravest sons <br> In all wide Troy, and none are left me now. <br> Fifty were with me when the men of Greece <br> Arrived upon our coast; nineteen of these <br> Owned the same mother, and the rest were born <br> Within my palaces. Remorseless Mars <br> Already had laid lifeless most of these, <br> And Hector, whom I cherished most, whose arm <br> Defended both our city and ourselves, <br> Him didst thou lately slay while combating <br> For his dear country. For his sake I come <br> To the Greek fleet, and to redeem his corse <br> I bring uncounted ransom. O, revere <br> The gods, Achilles, and be merciful, <br> Calling to mind thy father! Happier he <br> Than I; for I have borne what no man else <br> That dwells on earth could bear⁠—have laid my lips <br> Upon the hand of him who slew my son.” <br> He spake: Achilles sorrowfully thought <br> Of his own father. By the hand he took <br> The suppliant, and with gentle force removed <br> The old man from him. Both in memory <br> Of those they loved were weeping. The old king, <br> With many tears, and rolling in the dust <br> Before Achilles, mourned his gallant son. <br> Achilles sorrowed for his father’s sake, <br> And then bewailed Patroclus, and the sound <br> Of lamentation filled the tent. At last <br> Achilles, when he felt his heart relieved <br> By tears, and that strong grief had spent its force, <br> Sprang from his seat; then lifting by the hand <br> The aged man, and pitying his white head <br> And his white chin, he spake these wingèd words:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Great have thy sufferings been, unhappy king! <br> How couldst thou venture to approach alone <br> The Grecian fleet, and show thyself to him <br> Who slew so many of thy valiant sons? <br> An iron heart is thine. But seat thyself, <br> And let us, though afflicted grievously, <br> Allow our woes to sleep awhile, for grief <br> Indulged can bring no good. The gods ordain <br> The lot of man to suffer, while themselves <br> Are free from care. Beside Jove’s threshold stand <br> Two casks of gifts for man. One cask contains <br> The evil, one the good, and he to whom <br> The Thunderer gives them mingled sometimes falls <br> Into misfortune, and is sometimes crowned <br> With blessings. But the man to whom he gives <br> The evil only stands a mark exposed <br> To wrong, and, chased by grim calamity, <br> Wanders the teeming earth, alike unloved <br> By gods and men. So did the gods bestow <br> Munificent gifts on Peleus from his birth, <br> For eminent was he among mankind <br> For wealth and plenty; o’er the Myrmidons <br> He ruled, and, though a mortal, he was given <br> A goddess for a wife. Yet did the gods <br> Add evil to the good, for not to him <br> Was born a family of kingly sons <br> Within his house, successors to his reign. <br> One short-lived son is his, nor am I there <br> To cherish him in his old age; but here <br> Do I remain, far from my native land, <br> In Troy, and causing grief to thee and thine. <br> Of thee too, aged king, they speak, as one <br> Whose wealth was large in former days, when all <br> That Lesbos, seat of Macar, owns was thine, <br> And all in Phrygia and the shores that bound <br> The Hellespont; men said thou didst excel <br> All others in thy riches and thy sons. <br> But since the gods have brought this strife on thee <br> War and perpetual slaughter of brave men <br> Are round thy city. Yet be firm of heart, <br> Nor grieve forever. Sorrow for thy son <br> Will profit nought; it cannot bring the dead <br> To life again, and while thou dost afflict <br> Thyself for him fresh woes may fall on thee.”</p>
<p class="verse">And thus the godlike Priam, aged king, <br> Made answer: “Bid me not be seated here, <br> Nursling of Jove, while Hector lies among <br> Thy tents unburied. Let me ransom him <br> At once, that I may look on him once more <br> With my own eyes. Receive the many gifts <br> We bring thee, and mayst thou possess them long, <br> And reach thy native shore, since by thy grace <br> I live and yet behold the light of day.”</p>
<p class="verse">Achilles heard, and, frowning, thus rejoined: <br> “Anger me not, old man; ’twas in my thought <br> To let thee ransom Hector. To my tent <br> The mother came who bore me, sent from Jove, <br> The daughter of the Ancient of the Sea, <br> And I perceive, nor can it be concealed, <br> O Priam, that some god hath guided thee <br> To our swift galleys; for no mortal man, <br> Though in his prime of youthful strength, would dare <br> To come into the camp; he could not pass <br> The guard, nor move the beams that bar our gates. <br> So then remind me of my griefs no more, <br> Lest, suppliant as thou art, I leave thee not <br> Unharmed, and thus transgress the laws of Jove.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake: the aged man in fear obeyed. <br> And then Pelides like a lion leaped <br> Forth from the door, yet not alone he went; <br> For of his comrades two⁠—Automedon, <br> The hero, and his comrade Alcimus, <br> He whom Achilles held in most esteem <br> After the slain Patroclus⁠—followed him. <br> The mules and horses they unyoked, and led <br> The aged monarch’s clear-voiced herald in, <br> And bade him sit. Then from the polished car <br> They took the costly ransom of the corse <br> Of Hector, save two cloaks, which back they laid <br> With a fair tunic, that their chief might give <br> The body shrouded to be borne to Troy. <br> And then he called the maidens, bidding them <br> Wash and anoint the dead, yet far apart <br> From Priam, lest, with looking on his son, <br> The grief within his heart might rise uncurbed <br> To anger, and Achilles in his rage <br> Might stay him and transgress the laws of Jove. <br> And when the handmaids finished, having washed <br> The body and anointed it with oil, <br> And wrapped a sumptuous cloak and tunic round <br> The limbs, Achilles lifted it himself <br> And placed it on a bier. His comrades gave <br> Their aid, and raised it to the polished car. <br> When all was done, Achilles groaned, and called <br> By name the friend he dearly loved, and said:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“O my Patroclus, be not wroth with me <br> Shouldst thou in Hades hear that I restore <br> Hector to his dear father, since I take <br> A ransom not unworthy; but of this <br> I yield to thee the portion justly thine.”</p>
<p class="verse">So spake the godlike warrior, and withdrew <br> Into his tent, and took the princely seat <br> From which he had arisen, opposite <br> To that of Priam, whom he thus bespake:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Behold thy son is ransomed, aged man, <br> As thou hast asked, and lies upon his bier. <br> Thou shalt behold him with the early dawn, <br> And bear him hence. Now let us break our fast, <br> For even Niobe, the golden-haired, <br> Refrained not from her food, though children twelve <br> Perished within her palace⁠—six young sons <br> And six fair daughters. Phoebus slew the sons <br> With arrows from his silver bow, incensed <br> At Niobe, while Dian, archer queen, <br> Struck down the daughters; for the mother dared <br> To make herself the peer of rosy-cheeked <br> Latona, who, she boastfully proclaimed, <br> Had borne two children only, while herself <br> Had brought forth many. Yet, though only two, <br> The children of Latona took the lives <br> Of all her own. Nine days the corses lay <br> In blood, and there was none to bury them, <br> For Jove had changed the dwellers of the place <br> To stone; but on the tenth the gods of heaven <br> Gave burial to the dead. Yet Niobe, <br> Though spent with weeping long, did not refrain <br> From food. And now forever mid the rocks <br> And desert hills of Sipylus, where lie, <br> Fame says, the couches of the goddess-nymphs, <br> Who lead the dance where Acheloüs flows, <br> Although she be transformed to stone, she broods <br> Over the woes inflicted by the gods. <br> But now, O noble Ancient, let us sit <br> At our repast, and thou mayst afterward <br> Mourn thy beloved son, while bearing him <br> Homeward, to be bewailed with many tears.”</p>
<p class="verse">Achilles, the swift-footed, spake, and left <br> His seat, and, slaying a white sheep, he bade <br> His comrades flay and dress it. Then they carved <br> The flesh in portions which they fixed on spits, <br> And roasted carefully, and drew them back. <br> And then Automedon distributed <br> The bread in shapely canisters around <br> The table, while Achilles served the flesh, <br> And all put forth their hands and shared the feast. <br> But when their thirst and hunger were appeased, <br> Dardanian Priam fixed a wondering look <br> Upon Achilles, who in nobleness <br> Of form was like the gods. Achilles fixed <br> A look of equal wonder on his guest, <br> Dardanian Priam, for he much admired <br> His gracious aspect and his pleasant speech. <br> And when at length they both withdrew their gaze, <br> Priam, the godlike Ancient, spake, and said:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Nursling of Jove, dismiss me speedily <br> To rest, that we may lie, and be refreshed <br> With gentle slumbers. Never have these eyes <br> Been closed beneath their lids, since by thy hand <br> My Hector lost his life; and evermore <br> I mourn and cherish all my griefs, and writhe <br> Upon the ground within my palace courts; <br> But I have taken food at last, and drunk <br> Draughts of red wine, untasted till this hour.”</p>
<p class="verse">Achilles bade the attending men and maids <br> Place couches in the porch, and over them <br> Draw sumptuous purple mats on which to lay <br> Embroidered tapestries, and on each of these <br> Spread a broad, fleecy mantle, covering all. <br> Forth went the train with torches in their hands, <br> And quickly spread two couches. Then the swift <br> Achilles pleasantly to Priam said:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Sleep, excellent old man, without the tent, <br> Lest some one of our counsellors arrive, <br> Such as oft come within my tent to sit <br> And talk of warlike matters. Seeing thee <br> In the dark hours of night, he might relate <br> The tale to Agamemnon, king of men, <br> And hinder thus the ransom of thy son. <br> But say, and truly say, how many days <br> Requirest thou to pay the funeral rites <br> To noble Hector, so that I may rest <br> As many, and restrain the troops from war.”</p>
<p class="verse">Then answered godlike Priam, aged king: <br> “Since, then, thou wilt, Achilles, that we pay <br> The rites of burial to my noble son, <br> I own the favor. Well thou knowest how <br> We Trojans are constrained to keep within <br> The city walls, for it is far to bring <br> Wood from the mountains, and we fear to dare <br> The journey. Nine days would we mourn the dead <br> Within our dwellings, and upon the tenth <br> Would bury him, and make a solemn feast, <br> And the next day would rear his monument, <br> And on the twelfth, if needful, fight again.”</p>
<p class="verse">And swift Achilles, godlike chief, rejoined: <br> “Be it, O reverend Priam, as thou wilt, <br> And for that space will I delay the war.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake, and that the aged king might feel <br> No fear, he grasped his right hand at the wrist; <br> And then King Priam and the herald went <br> To sleep within the porch, but wary still. <br> Achilles slumbered in his stately tent, <br> The rosy-cheeked Briseis at his side, <br> And all the other gods and men who fought <br> In chariots gave themselves to slumber, save <br> Beneficent Hermes; sleep came not to him, <br> For still he meditated how to bring <br> King Priam back from the Achaian fleet <br> Unnoticed by the watchers at the gate. <br> So at the monarch’s head he stood, and spake:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“O aged king, thou givest little heed <br> To danger, sleeping thus amid thy foes, <br> Because Achilles spares thee. Thou hast paid <br> Large ransom for thy well-beloved son, <br> And yet the sons whom thou hast left in Troy <br> Would pay three times that ransom for thy life, <br> Should Agamemnon, son of Atreus, learn⁠— <br> Or any of the Greeks⁠—that thou art here.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake: the aged king in fear awaked <br> The herald. Hermes yoked the steeds and mules, <br> And drave them quickly through the camp unmarked <br> By any there. But when they reached the ford <br> Where Xanthus, progeny of Jupiter, <br> Rolls the smooth eddies of his stream, the god <br> Deputed for the Olympian height, and Morn <br> In saffron robes o’erspread the Earth with light. <br> Townward they urged the steeds, and as they went <br> Sorrowed and wailed: the mules conveyed the dead, <br> And they were seen by none of all the men <br> And graceful dames of Troy save one alone. <br> Cassandra, beautiful as Venus, stood <br> On Pergamus, and from its height discerned <br> Her wither, standing on the chariot-seat, <br> And knew the herald, him whose voice so oft <br> Summoned the citizens, and knew the dead <br> Stretched on a litter drawn by mules. She raised <br> Her voice, and called to all the city thus:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“O Trojan men and women, hasten forth <br> To look on Hector, if ye e’er rejoiced <br> To see him coming from the field alive, <br> The pride of Troy, and all who dwell in her.”</p>
<p class="verse">She spake, and suddenly was neither man <br> Nor woman left within the city bounds. <br> Deep grief was on them all; they went to meet, <br> Near to the gates, the monarch bringing home <br> The dead. And first the wife whom Hector loved <br> Rushed with his reverend mother to the car <br> As it rolled on, and, plucking out their hair, <br> Touched with their hands the forehead of the dead, <br> While round it pressed the multitude, and wept, <br> And would have wept before the gates all day, <br> Even to the set of sun, in bitter grief <br> For Hector’s loss, had not the aged man <br> Addressed the people from his chariot-seat: <br> “Give place to me, and let the mules pass on, <br> And ye may weep your fill when once the dead <br> Is laid within the palace.” As he spake, <br> The throng gave way and let the chariot pass; <br> And having brought it to the royal halls, <br> On a fair couch they laid the corse, and placed <br> Singers beside it, leaders of the dirge, <br> Who sang a sorrowful, lamenting strain, <br> And all the women answered it with sobs. <br> White-armed Andromache in both her hands <br> Took warlike Hector’s head, and over it <br> Began the lamentation midst them all:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Thou hast died young, my husband, leaving me <br> In this thy home a widow, and one son, <br> An infant yet. To an unhappy pair <br> He owes his birth, and never will, I fear, <br> Bloom into youth; for ere that day will Troy <br> Be overthrown, since thou, its chief defence, <br> Art dead, the guardian of its walls and all <br> Its noble matrons and its speechless babes, <br> Yet to be carried captive far away, <br> And I among them, in the hollow barques; <br> And thou, my son, wilt either go with me, <br> Where thou shalt toil at menial tasks for some <br> Pitiless master; or perhaps some Greek <br> Will seize thy little arm, and in his rage <br> Will hurl thee from a tower and dash thee dead, <br> Remembering how thy father, Hector, slew <br> His brother, son, or father; for the hand <br> Of Hector forced full many a Greek to bite <br> The dust of earth. Not slow to smite was he <br> In the fierce conflict; therefore all who dwell <br> Within the city sorrow for his fall. <br> Thou bringest an unutterable grief, <br> O Hector, on thy parents, and on me <br> The sharpest sorrows. Thou didst not stretch forth <br> Thy hands to me, in dying, from thy couch, <br> Nor speak a word to comfort me, which I <br> Might ever think of night and day with tears.”</p>
<p class="verse">So spake the weeping wife: the women all <br> Mingled their wail with hers, and Hecuba <br> Took up the passionate lamentation next:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“O Hector, thou who wert most fondly loved <br> Of all my sons! While yet thou wert alive, <br> Dear wert thou to the gods, who even now, <br> When death has overtaken thee, bestow <br> Such care upon thee. All my other sons <br> Whom swift Achilles took in war he sold <br> At Samos, Imbrus, by the barren sea, <br> And Lemnos harborless. But as for thee, <br> When he had taken with his cruel spear <br> Thy life, he dragged thee round and round the tomb <br> Of his young friend, Patroclus, whom thy hand <br> Had slain, yet raised he not by this the dead; <br> And now thou liest in the palace here, <br> Fresh and besprinkled as with early dew, <br> Like one just slain with silent arrows aimed <br> By Phoebus, bearer of the silver bow.”</p>
<p class="verse">Weeping she spake, and woke in all who heard <br> Grief without measure. Helen, last of all, <br> Took up the lamentation, and began:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“O Hector, who wert dearest to my heart <br> Of all my husband’s brothers⁠—for the wife <br> Am I of godlike Paris, him whose fleet <br> Brought me to Troy⁠—would I had sooner died! <br> And now the twentieth year is past since first <br> I came a stranger from my native shore, <br> Yet have I never heard from thee a word <br> Of anger or reproach. And when the sons <br> Of Priam, and his daughters, and the wives <br> Of Priam’s sons, in all their fair array, <br> Taunted me grievously, or Hecuba <br> Herself⁠—for Priam ever was to me <br> A gracious father⁠—thou didst take my part <br> With kindly admonitions, and restrain <br> Their tongues with soft address and gentle words. <br> Therefore my heart is grieved, and I bewail <br> Thee and myself at once⁠—unhappy me! <br> For now I have no friend in all wide Troy⁠— <br> None to be kind to me: they hate me all.”</p>
<p class="verse">Weeping she spake: the mighty throng again <br> Answered with wailing. Priam then addressed <br> The people: “Now bring wood, ye men of Troy, <br> Into the city. Let there be no fear <br> Of ambush from the Greeks, for when of late <br> I left Achilles at the dark-hulled barques, <br> He gave his promise to molest no more <br> The men of Troy till the twelfth morn shall rise.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake, and speedily they yoked the mules <br> And oxen to the wains, and came in throngs <br> Before the city walls. Nine days they toiled <br> To bring the trunks of trees, and when the tenth <br> Arose to light the abodes of men, they brought <br> The corse of valiant Hector from the town <br> With many tears, and laid it on the wood <br> High up, and flung the fire to light the pile.</p>
<p class="verse">Now when the early rosy-fingered Dawn <br> Looked forth, the people gathered round the pile <br> Of glorious Hector. When they all had come <br> Together, first they quenched the funeral fires, <br> Wherever they had spread, with dark-red wine, <br> And then his brothers and companions searched <br> For the white bones. In sorrow and in tears, <br> That streaming stained their cheeks, they gathered them, <br> And placed them in a golden um. O’er this <br> They drew a covering of soft purple robes, <br> And laid it in a hollow grave, and piled <br> Fragments of rock above it, many and huge. <br> In haste they reared the tomb, with sentries set <br> On every side, lest all too soon the Greeks <br> Should come in armor to renew the war. <br> When now the tomb was built, the multitude <br> Returned, and in the halls where Priam dwelt, <br> Nursling of Jove, were feasted royally. <br> Such was the mighty Hector’s burial rite.</p>
