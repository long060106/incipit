<p class="lede">Agamemnon’s distress at the obstinacy of Achilles⁠—Consults with Menelaus, Nestor, Ulysses, and Diomed⁠—A council⁠—Diomed and Ulysses set out for the enemy’s camp to learn his designs⁠—Death of Dolon, the Trojan Spy⁠—Rhesus the Thracian killed in his tent and his horses taken.</p>
<p class="verse">All the night long the captains of the Greeks <br> Slept at the ships, and pleasant was their sleep⁠— <br> Save only Agamemnon, Atreus’ son, <br> The shepherd of the people. Not to him⁠— <br> Vexed with a thousand cares⁠—came gentle sleep. <br> As when the husband of the light-haired queen <br> Of heaven sends forth his thunders, ushering in <br> Some wide-involving shower⁠—rain, hail, or snow <br> Whitening the fields⁠—or opening o’er some land <br> The ravenous jaws of unrelenting war⁠— <br> So frequent were the groans which from his heart <br> Atrides uttered; for within his breast <br> His heart was troubled. Looking toward the plain <br> Of Troy, he wondered at the many fires <br> Blazing before the city, and the sound <br> Of flutes and fifes, and tumult of the crowd. <br> But when he turned him toward the fleet and host <br> Of Greece, he tore his hair, and flung it up <br> To Jove, and vented his great heart in groans. <br> And now at length it seemed to him most wise <br> To seek Neleian Nestor, and with him <br> Devise some plan by which to turn aside <br> The threatened evil from the Greeks. He rose, <br> And drew his tunic o’er his breast, and laced <br> The graceful sandals to his well-shaped feet; <br> And o’er his shoulders threw the blood-stained hide <br> Of a huge tawny lion, that reached down <br> Even to the ground; and took in hand his spear. <br> Meantime with like uneasy thoughts oppressed <br> Was Menelaus, to whose eyes there came <br> No slumber⁠—dreading lest calamity <br> Should light upon the Greeks, who for his sake <br> Had crossed the sea to carry war to Troy. <br> And first he threw a leopard’s spotted hide <br> O’er his broad back, and placed the brazen helm <br> Upon his head, and took in his strong grasp <br> A spear, and went to bid his brother wake⁠— <br> His brother, the chief ruler over all <br> The men of Greece, and honored like a god. <br> He found him at his galley’s prow in act <br> To sheath his shoulders in the shining mail, <br> And pleased to greet his coming. To the king <br> Thus Menelaus, great in battle, spake:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Why arm thyself, my brother? Wouldst thou send <br> A warrior to explore the Trojan camp? <br> None will accept the task, I fear, to creep <br> Alone at dead of night, a spy, within <br> The hostile lines;⁠—a bold man must he be.”</p>
<p class="verse">Then answered Agamemnon, king of men:⁠— <br> “Most noble Menelaus, much we need <br> Wise counsel⁠—thou and I⁠—to save our men <br> And galleys from destruction, since the will <br> Of Jove is changed. Now hath the God respect <br> To Hector’s sacrifices; for in truth <br> I never saw⁠—I never heard of one <br> Who in one day performed such mighty deeds <br> As Hector, dear to Jove, just now hath wrought, <br> Though not the son of goddess or of god. <br> Those deeds will be, I deem, for many a day <br> A cause of bitter sorrow to the Greeks⁠— <br> Such evil hath he wrought. Now go at once, <br> And from their galleys call Idomeneus <br> And Ajax; while to noble Nestor’s tent <br> I go, and pray that he will rise and give <br> Their orders to the sacred band of guards;⁠— <br> For they will hearken to him, since his son <br> Commands them jointly with Meriones, <br> The armor-bearer of Idomeneus⁠— <br> Both named by us to that important trust.”</p>
<p class="verse">Then Menelaus, great in battle, said:⁠— <br> “What wilt thou, then, and what dost thou command⁠— <br> That I remain with them until thou come, <br> Or, having given the message, seek thee here?”</p>
<p class="verse">Again the monarch Agamemnon spake:⁠— <br> “Wait there, lest as we go I meet thee not, <br> For many ways are through the camp. But thou, <br> In going, shout aloud and bid them all <br> Be vigilant, accosting everyone <br> By his paternal name, and giving each <br> Due honor: bear thyself not haughtily: <br> We too must labor; for when we were born <br> Jove laid this hard condition on us all.”</p>
<p class="verse">So spake he, and, dismissing with that charge <br> His brother, hastened to where Nestor lay, <br> The shepherd of his people. Him he found <br> On his soft couch within his tent beside <br> His dark-brown ship. Around him scattered shone <br> His arms⁠—a shield, two spears, a gleaming helm, <br> And pliant belt, with which the ancient man <br> Girded himself when arming to lead on <br> His men to murderous fight;⁠—for not to age <br> The warrior yielded yet. He raised his head, <br> And, leaning on his elbow, questioned thus <br> Atrides: “Who art thou that traversest <br> The camp beside the fleet at dead of night, <br> Alone, while others sleep? Com’st thou to find <br> One of the guardsmen, or a comrade? Speak; <br> Come not in silence thus: what wouldst thou have?”</p>
<p class="verse">Then answered Agamemnon, king of men:⁠— <br> “O Nestor, son of Neleus, whom the Greeks <br> All glory in! Thou certainly wilt know <br> Atrides Agamemnon, whom the will <br> Of Jove hath visited with hardships great <br> Beyond what others bear, to last while breath <br> Is in my lungs, and while my knees can move. <br> I wander thus abroad because sweet sleep <br> Comes not to close my eyelids, and the war <br> And slaughter of the Greeks distress me sore. <br> For them I greatly fear, my heart is faint, <br> My mind confounded. In my breast the heart <br> Pants, and my limbs all tremble. If thou wilt⁠— <br> For, as I see, thou also dost not sleep⁠— <br> Come with me to the guards, that we may know <br> Whether, o’ercome by toil and weariness, <br> They give themselves to slumber and forget <br> Their watch. The foe is near us in his camp, <br> And how know we that even now by night <br> He plans not, to attack us in our tents?”</p>
<p class="verse">Then Nestor, the Gerenian knight, replied:⁠— <br> “Atrides Agamemnon, glorious king <br> Of men, almighty Jove will not perform <br> For Hector all that Hector plans and hopes; <br> And heavier cares, I think, will yet be his <br> When once Achilles’ wrath is turned away. <br> Yet willingly I join thee. Let us call <br> The other chiefs⁠—Ulysses, Diomed, <br> Both mighty spearmen; Ajax, swift of foot; <br> And the brave son of Phyleus. It were well <br> To send and bid the mightier Ajax come, <br> And King Idomeneus, for farthest off <br> The ships of both are stationed. I shall chide <br> Thy brother Menelaus⁠—though he be <br> Honored and dear, and though it please thee not⁠— <br> For sleeping, while he leaves such toils as these <br> To thee alone. He should be here among <br> The chiefs, exhorting them to valiant deeds; <br> For now the hour of bitter need is come.”</p>
<p class="verse">Again spake Agamemnon, king of men:⁠— <br> “At other times, old chief, I would have begged <br> That thou shouldst blame him: he is oft remiss, <br> And late to act; but not because of sloth, <br> Or want of spirit, but he looks to me <br> And waits for my example. Yet tonight <br> He rose before me, sought me, and is sent <br> To call the chiefs whom thou hast named; and now <br> Let us go on, and meet them where they wait, <br> Among the guards and just before the gates⁠— <br> For I appointed that the trysting-place.”</p>
<p class="verse">And Nestor, the Gerenian knight, replied:⁠— <br> “Then let no Greek condemn him, or refuse <br> To heed and to obey when he shall speak.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake, and drew his tunic o’er his breast, <br> Laced the fair sandals to his shapely feet, <br> And round him fastened, with a clasp, his cloak⁠— <br> A double web of purple, with full folds <br> And flowing pile. He grasped a massive spear, <br> Its blade of trenchant brass. And first he sought <br> The galleys of the Achaians brazen-mailed. <br> There shouted Nestor the Gerenian knight, <br> To raise Ulysses, best of counsellors, <br> Jove-like in wisdom; who perceived the voice, <br> And issued from his tent in haste, and said:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“What brings you forth to walk the camp at night, <br> Beside the ships alone; what urgent cause?”</p>
<p class="verse">Then answered Nestor, the Gerenian knight:⁠— <br> “Son of Laertes, nobly born, and skilled <br> In wise devices, be thou not displeased: <br> A fearful woe impends above the Greeks: <br> Come, then, and call the other chiefs, to give <br> Their counsel whether we shall flee or fight.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake; and wise Ulysses, entering <br> His tent again, upon his shoulders laid <br> His well-wrought shield, and joined them as they went, <br> Till, coming to Tydides Diomed, <br> They found him by his tent among his arms, <br> His comrades sleeping round him with their shields <br> Beneath their heads. Their spears were set upright, <br> The nether points in earth. The polished brass <br> Gleamed like the lightnings of All-Father Jove. <br> In sleep the hero lay; a wild bull’s hide <br> Was spread beneath him, and a carpet dyed <br> With glowing colors propped his head. The knight, <br> Gerenian Nestor, touched him with his foot <br> And roused him, and addressed him chidingly:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“O son of Tydeus! Wilt thou calmly sleep <br> All the night long? And hast thou, then, not heard <br> That on a height amidst the plain the sons <br> Of Troy are stationed, near the ships, and small <br> The space that parts the enemy’s camp from ours?”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake. The son of Tydeus sprang from sleep <br> At once, and answered him with wingèd words:</p>
<p class="verse">“Thy labors are too constant, aged man; <br> Thou shrinkest from no hardship. Are there not <br> Young men among the Greeks to walk the camp <br> And call the kings? Thou never takest rest.”</p>
<p class="verse">And Nestor, the Gerenian knight, replied:⁠— <br> “Well hast thou said, my friend, for I have sons <br> Without reproach, and I have many troops; <br> And any one of these might walk the camp <br> And give the summons. But tonight there lies <br> A hard necessity upon the Greeks, <br> And their destruction and their rescue hang <br> Balanced on a knife’s edge. Come then, since thou <br> Art younger, call swift Ajax and the son <br> Of Phyleus, if thou wouldst relieve my age.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake; and Diomed around him flung <br> A tawny lion’s ample hide, that reached <br> Down to his feet, and took his spear and went <br> And summoned the two kings, and brought them forth.</p>
<p class="verse">Now when they came among the assembled guard, <br> Its leaders were not slumbering; every man <br> Sat watchful and in arms. As dogs that guard <br> Flocks in a sheepfold hear some savage beast <br> That comes through thickets down the mountainside; <br> Loud is the clamor of the dogs and men, <br> And sleep is frightened thence⁠—so gentle sleep <br> Fled from the eyes of those who watched, that night, <br> Sadly, with eyes turned ever toward the plain, <br> Intently listening for the foe’s approach. <br> The aged Nestor saw them, and rejoiced, <br> And thus encouraged them with wingèd words:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Watch thus, dear youths, let no one yield to sleep, <br> Lest we become the mockery of the foe.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake, and crossed the trench; and with him went <br> The Grecian leaders, they who had been called <br> To council. With them went Meriones <br> And Nestor’s eminent son, for they had both <br> Been summoned. Crossing to the other side <br> Of that deep trench, they found an open space <br> Clear of the dead, in which they sat them down⁠— <br> Just where the fiery Hector, having slain <br> Many Achaians, turned him back when night <br> Came o’er him. There they sat to hold debate <br> And thus spake Nestor the Gerenian knight:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Friends! Is there none among you who so far <br> Trusts his own valor that he will tonight <br> Venture among the Trojans? He perchance <br> Might capture on the borders of the camp <br> Some foeman wandering, or might bring report <br> Of what they meditate, and whether still <br> They mean to keep their station far from Troy <br> And near our ships, or, since their late success, <br> Return to Ilium. Could he safely bring <br> This knowledge back to us, his meed were great, <br> Glory among all men beneath the sky, <br> And liberal recompense. As many chiefs <br> As now command our galleys, each would give <br> A black ewe with a suckling lamb⁠—such gifts <br> No one hath yet received⁠—and he should sit <br> A guest at all our banquets and our feasts.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake; and all were silent for a space. <br> Then Diomed, the great in battle, said:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Nestor, my resolute spirit urges me <br> To explore the Trojan camp, that lies so near; <br> Yet, were another warrior by my side, <br> I should go forth with a far surer hope, <br> And greater were my daring. For when two <br> Join in the same adventure, one perceives <br> Before the other how they ought to act; <br> While one alone, however prompt, resolves <br> More tardily and with a weaker will.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake; and many a chief made suit to share <br> The risk with Diomed. The ministers <br> Of Mars, the chieftains Ajax, asked to go; <br> Meriones desired it; Nestor’s son <br> Greatly desired to join the enterprise; <br> Atrides Menelaus, skilled to wield <br> The spear, desired it; and that hardy chief, <br> Ulysses, longed to explore the Trojan camp, <br> For full of daring aims was the great soul <br> Within his bosom. Agamemnon then, <br> The king of men, took up the word and said:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Tydides Diomed, most dear of men, <br> Choose from the many chiefs, who ask to bear <br> A part with thee, the bravest. Be not moved <br> By deference to take the worse and leave <br> The abler warrior. Pay no heed to rank, <br> Or race, or wide extent of kingly rule.”</p>
<p class="verse">Thus spake the king; for in his heart he feared <br> For fair-haired Menelaus. Diomed, <br> The great in battle, then addressed them all:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Ye bid me choose: how, then, can I o’erlook <br> Godlike Ulysses, prudent in resolve, <br> And firm in every danger, well beloved <br> By Pallas. Give me him, and our return <br> Is sure, though from consuming flames; for he <br> Is wise to plan beyond all other men.”</p>
<p class="verse">Ulysses, nobly born and hardy, spake <br> In turn: “Tydides, praise me not too much, <br> Nor blame me, for thou speakest to the Greeks, <br> Who know me. Meantime let us haste to go, <br> For the night wears away, and morn is near. <br> The stars are high, two thirds of night are past⁠— <br> The greater part⁠—and scarce a third remains.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake; and both arrayed themselves for fight. <br> The mighty warrior Thrasymedes gave <br> The two-edged sword he wore to Diomed⁠— <br> Whose own was at the galleys⁠—and a shield. <br> The hero then put on his helmet, made <br> Of tough bull-hide, with neither cone nor crest⁠— <br> Such as is worn by beardless youths. A bow, <br> Quiver, and sword Meriones bestowed <br> Upon Ulysses, placing on his brows <br> A leathern helmet, firmly laced within <br> By many a thong, and on the outer side <br> Set thickly with a tusky boar’s white teeth, <br> Which fenced it well and skilfully. A web <br> Of woollen for the temples lined the work. <br> This helm Autolycus once bore away <br> From Eleon, the city where he sacked <br> The stately palace of Amyntor, son <br> Of Ormenus. The captor gave the prize <br> To the Cytheran chief, Amphidamas, <br> Who bore it to Scandeia, and in turn <br> Bestowed it upon Molus as his guest, <br> And Molus gave it to Meriones, <br> His son, to wear in battle. Now at last <br> It crowned Ulysses’ temples. When the twain <br> Were all accoutred in their dreadful arms, <br> Forward they went, and left the assembled chiefs, <br> While, sent by Pallas forth, upon their right <br> A heron flew beside their path. The bird <br> They saw not, for the night was dark, but heard <br> Its rustling wings. Ulysses at the sound <br> Rejoiced, and supplicated Pallas thus:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Hear! daughter of the Aegis-bearer Jove! <br> Thou who art near me in all dangers, thou <br> Whose eye is on me wheresoe’er I go, <br> Befriend me, Pallas, yet again, and grant <br> That, laden with great glory, we return <br> Safe to the galleys, mighty deeds performed, <br> And woe inflicted on the Trojan race.”</p>
<p class="verse">Next Diomed, the great in battle, prayed:⁠— <br> “Daughter invincible of Jove, give ear <br> Also to me. Be with me now, as once <br> Thou didst attend on Tydeus nobly born, <br> My father, when he bore an embassy <br> To Thebé from the Achaians. He beside <br> The Asopus left the Achaians mailed in brass, <br> And bore a friendly message to the sons <br> Of Cadmus, and on his return performed <br> Full many a mighty deed with aid from thee, <br> Great goddess! for thou stoodest by his side. <br> Stand now by me; be thou my shield and guard; <br> And I, in turn, will offer up to thee <br> A yearling heifer, broad between the horns, <br> Which never ploughman yet hath tamed to bear <br> The yoke. Her to thine altar will I bring, <br> With gilded horns, to be a sacrifice.”</p>
<p class="verse">So prayed they. Pallas listened to their prayers; <br> And, having supplicated thus the child <br> Of Jove Almighty, the two chiefs went on Like lions through the darkness of the night, Through slaughter, heaps of corses, and black blood.</p>
<p class="verse">Nor now had Hector suffered the brave sons <br> Of Troy to sleep, but summoned all the chiefs, <br> Leaders, and princes of the host, and thus <br> Addressed the assembly with well-ordered words:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Who of you all will promise to perform <br> The task I set him, for a large reward? <br> For ample shall his meed be. I will give <br> A chariot and two steeds with lofty necks, <br> Swifter than the swift galleys of the Greeks. <br> Great glory will be his whoever dares <br> Approach those ships and bring the knowledge thence <br> Whether the fleet is guarded as before, <br> Or whether, yielding to our arms, the foe <br> Is meditating flight, and, through the night <br> O’ercome with weariness, keeps watch no more.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake; and all were silent for a space. <br> Now there was one, among the Trojan chiefs, <br> Whose father was Eumedes, of the train <br> Of reverend heralds. Dolon was his name, <br> And he was rich in gold and brass, deformed <br> In face but swift of foot, an only son <br> Among five sisters. He stood forth among <br> The Trojans, and replied to Hector thus:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“My daring spirit, Hector, urges me <br> To visit the swift ships and learn the state <br> Of the Greek host. But hold thy sceptre forth, <br> And solemnly attest the gods that thou <br> Wilt give to me the horses, and the car <br> Engrailed with brass, which bear the illustrious son <br> Of Peleus. I shall not explore in vain, <br> Nor balk thy hope of me; for I will pass <br> Into the camp until I reach the ship <br> Of Agamemnon, where the chiefs are now <br> Debating whether they shall fly or fight.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake; and Hector held the sceptre forth, <br> And swore: “Be Jupiter the Thunderer, <br> Husband of Juno, witness, that those steeds <br> Shall bear no other Trojan than thyself. <br> That honor I confirm to thee alone.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake. It was an idle oath, yet gave <br> New courage to the spy, who instantly <br> Upon his shoulders hung his crooked bow, <br> And round him flung a gray wolf’s hide, and placed <br> A casque of otter-skin upon his head, <br> And took his pointed javelin, and made haste <br> To reach the Grecian fleet. Yet was he doomed <br> Never to leave that fleet again, nor bring <br> Tidings to Hector. Soon was he beyond <br> The crowd of men and steeds, and eagerly <br> Held on his way. Ulysses first perceived <br> His coming, and thus spake to Diomed:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Someone, Tydides, from the enemy’s camp <br> Is coming, either as a spy, or else <br> To spoil the dead. First let us suffer him <br> To pass us by a little on the plain, <br> Then let us rush and seize him. Should his speed <br> Be greater than our own, let us attack <br> The fugitive with spears, and drive him on <br> To where our ships are lying, from his camp, <br> Lest, flying townward, he escape our hands.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake; and both lay down without the path <br> Among the dead, while he unwarily <br> Passed by them. When he now had gone as far <br> As two yoked mules might at the furrow’s end <br> Precede a pair of oxen⁠—for by mules <br> The plough is drawn more quickly through the soil <br> Of the deep fallow⁠—then they rose, and rushed <br> To seize him. As he heard their steps he stopped. <br> In hope that his companions had been sent <br> From Troy by Hector to conduct him back. <br> But when they came within a javelin’s cast, <br> Or haply less, he saw that they were foes, <br> And moved his nimble knees, and turned to flee, <br> While rapidly they followed. As two hounds, <br> Sharp-toothed, and trained to track their prey, pursue <br> Through forest-grounds some fawn or hare that runs <br> Before them panting, so did Diomed <br> And terrible Ulysses without stop <br> Follow the fugitive, to cut him off <br> From his own people. In his flight he came <br> Where soon he would have mingled with the guards, <br> Close to the fleet. Then Pallas breathed new strength <br> Into Tydides, that no other Greek <br> Might boast that he had wounded Dolon first, <br> And steal the honor. Therefore, with his spear <br> Uplifted, Diomed rushed on and spake:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Stop, or my spear o’ertakes thee, nor wilt thou <br> Escape a certain death from this right hand.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake, and hurled his spear⁠—but not to smite⁠— <br> At Dolon, over whose right shoulder passed <br> The polished weapon, and, descending, pierced <br> The ground. Then Dolon, pale and fear-struck, stopped, <br> And quaked, with chattering teeth and stammering speech. <br> They, breathless with the chase, came up and seized <br> His hands, while, bursting into tears, he spake:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Take me alive, and ye shall have from me <br> A ransom: there is store of brass and gold <br> And well-wrought steel, of which a princely share <br> My father will bestow when he shall hear <br> Of me alive and at the Grecian fleet.”</p>
<p class="verse">The crafty chief Ulysses answered thus:⁠— <br> “Take heart, and cease to think of death, but tell, <br> And truly, why thou camest to our fleet: <br> Was it to strip the bodies of the dead? <br> Camest thou, sent by Hector, as a spy <br> Among our ships, or of thine own accord?”</p>
<p class="verse">And Dolon answered, trembling still with fear:⁠— <br> “Hector, against my will and to my hurt, <br> Persuaded me. He promised to bestow <br> On me the firm-paced coursers, and the car <br> Engrailed with brass, which bear the illustrious son <br> Of Peleus, and enjoined me by the aid <br> Of darkness to approach the foe and learn <br> Whether ye guard your galleys as before, <br> Or, overcome by us, consult on flight, <br> And, wearied with the hardships of the day, <br> Have failed to set the accustomed nightly watch.”</p>
<p class="verse">The man of craft, Ulysses, smiled, and said:⁠— <br> “Truly, thy hope was set on princely gifts⁠— <br> The steeds of war-renowned Aeacides, <br> Hard to be reined by mortal hands, or driven as <br> By any, save by Peleus’ son himself, <br> Whom an immortal mother bore. But come, <br> Tell me⁠—and tell the truth⁠—where hast thou left <br> Hector, the leader of the host, and where <br> Are laid his warlike arms; where stand his steeds; <br> Where are the sentinels, and where the tents <br> Of other chiefs? On what do they consult? <br> Will they remain beside our galleys here, <br> Or do they meditate, since, as they say, <br> The Greeks are beaten, a return to Troy?”</p>
<p class="verse">Dolon, Eumedes’ son, made answer thus:⁠— <br> “What thou requirest I will truly tell. <br> Hector is with his counsellors, and now, <br> Apart from all the bustle, at the tomb <br> Of Ilus the divine, he plans the war. <br> Sentries, of whom thou speakest, there are none; <br> No chosen band, O hero! has in charge <br> To guard the camp. By all their blazing fires, <br> Constrained by need, the Trojans keep awake, <br> And each exhorts his fellow to maintain <br> The watch: not so the auxiliar troops who came <br> From far: they sleep, and since they have no wives <br> Nor children near, they let the Trojans watch.”</p>
<p class="verse">Then thus the man of wiles, Ulysses, spake:⁠— <br> “How sleep they⁠—mingled with the knights of Troy <br> Or by themselves? Tell me, that I may know.”</p>
<p class="verse">Dolon, Eumedes’ son, made answer thus:⁠— <br> “What thou requirest I will truly tell. <br> On one hand, toward the sea, the bowmen lie <br> Of Caria and Paeonia, and with them <br> Lelegans, Caucons, and the gallant tribe <br> Of the Pelasgians. On the other hand, <br> Toward Thymbra, are the Lycians, the proud race <br> Of Mysia, Phrygia’s knights, and cavalry <br> Of the Maeonians. Why should ye inquire <br> The place of each? If ye design tonight <br> To penetrate into the Trojan camp, <br> There are the Thracians, newly come, apart <br> From all the others: with them is their king, <br> Rhesus, the son of Eioneus; his steeds <br> Are far the largest and most beautiful <br> I ever saw⁠—the snow is not so white, <br> The wind is not so swift. His chariot shines <br> With gold and silver, and the coat of mail <br> In which he came to Troy is all of gold, <br> And gloriously and marvellously bright, <br> Such as becomes not mortal men to wear, <br> But the gods only. Now to your swift Ships <br> Lead me; or bind me fast with thongs, and here <br> Leave me till your return; and ye shall know <br> Whether the words I speak be true or false.”</p>
<p class="verse">Then sternly spake the gallant Diomed:⁠— <br> “Once in our hands a prisoner, do not think, <br> O Dolon! to escape, though thou hast told <br> Things that shall profit us. For if we now <br> Release thee thou wilt surely come again <br> To the Greek fleet, a spy, or openly <br> To fight against us. If I take thy life, <br> ’Tis certain thou wilt harm the Greeks no more.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake. And as the suppliant took his chin <br> In his large hand, and had begun a prayer, <br> He smote him with his sword at the mid-neck, <br> And cut the tendons both; the severed head, <br> While yet he spake, fell, rolling in the dust. <br> And then they took his helm of otter-skin <br> The wolf’s-hide, sounding bow, and massive spear. <br> The nobly born Ulysses in his hand <br> Lifted the trophies high, devoting them <br> To Pallas, deity of spoil, and prayed:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Delight thyself, O goddess, in these arms, <br> For thee we first invoke, of all the gods <br> Upon Olympus. Guide us now to find <br> The camp and coursers of the sons of Thrace.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake; and, raising them aloft, he hung <br> The spoils upon a tamarisk, and brake <br> Reeds and the spreading branches of the tree <br> To form a mark, that so on their return <br> They might not, in the darkness, miss the spot. <br> Then onward, mid strewn arms and pools of blood, <br> They went, and soon were where the Thracians lay. <br> There slept the warriors, overpowered with toil; <br> Their glittering arms were near them, fairly ranged <br> In triple rows, and by each suit of arms <br> Two coursers. Rhesus slumbered in the midst. <br> Near him were his fleet horses, which were made <br> Fast to the chariot’s border by the reins. <br> Ulysses saw them first, and, pointing, said:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“This is the man, O Diomed, and these <br> The steeds, described by Dolon whom we slew. <br> Come, then; put forth thy strength of arm, for ill <br> Doth it become thee to stand idle here, <br> Armed as thou art. Loose thou the steeds; or else <br> Slay thou the men, and leave the steeds to me.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake. The blue-eyed Pallas straightway gave <br> Strength to Tydides, who on every side <br> Dealt slaughter. From the smitten by the sword <br> Rose fearful groans; the ground was red with blood. <br> As when a ravening lion suddenly <br> Springs on a helpless flock of goats or sheep, <br> So fell Tydides on the Thracian band, <br> Till twelve were slain. Whomever Diomed <br> Approached and smote, the sage Ulysses seized, <br> And drew him backward by the feet, that thus <br> The flowing-maned coursers might pass forth <br> Unhindered, nor, by treading on the dead, <br> Be startled; for they yet were new to war. <br> Now when the son of Tydeus reached the king⁠— <br> The thirteenth of his victims⁠—him he slew <br> As he breathed heavily; for on that night <br> A fearful dream, in shape Oenides’ son, <br> Stood o’er him, sent by Pallas. Carefully <br> Ulysses meantime loosed the firm-paced steeds, <br> And, fastening them together, drave them forth, <br> Urging them with his bow: he had not thought <br> To take the showy lash that lay in sight <br> On the fair chariot-seat. In going thence <br> He whistled, as a sign to Diomed, <br> Who lingered, pondering on his next exploit⁠— <br> Whether to seize the chariot where was laid <br> The embroidered armor, dragging it away; <br> Or, lifting it aloft, to bear it thence; <br> Or take more Thracian lives. As thus his thoughts <br> Were busy, Pallas, standing near him, spake:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“O son of large-souled Tydeus, think betimes <br> Of thy return to where the galleys lie; <br> Else may some god arouse the sons of Troy, <br> And thou be forced to reach the ships by flight.”</p>
<p class="verse">She spake. He knew the goddess by her voice, <br> And leaped upon a steed. Ulysses lashed <br> The horses with his bow, and on they flew <br> Toward the swift galleys of the Grecian host.</p>
<p class="verse">Apollo, bearer of the silver bow, <br> Kept no vain watch, and, angry when he saw <br> Minerva at the side of Diomed, <br> Down to the mighty host of Troy he came, <br> And roused from sleep a Thracian counsellor⁠— <br> Hippocoön, a kinsman of the house <br> Of Rhesus. Leaping from his couch, he saw <br> The vacant spot where the swift steeds had stood, <br> And, weltering in their blood, the dying chiefs, <br> He saw, and wept aloud, and called by name <br> His dear companion. Then a clamor rose, <br> And boundless tumult, as the Trojans came <br> All rushing to the spot, and marvelling <br> At what the daring warriors, who were now <br> Returning to the hollow ships, had done.</p>
<p class="verse">And when these warriors now had reached the spot <br> Where Hector’s spy was slain, Ulysses, dear <br> To Jupiter, reined in the fiery steeds, <br> And Diomed leaped down and took the spoil <br> Blood-stained, and gave it to Ulysses’ hands, <br> And mounted. Then again they urged the steeds, <br> Which, not unwilling, flew along the way. <br> First Nestor heard the approaching sound, and said:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Friends, chiefs and princes of the Greeks, my heart⁠— <br> Truly or falsely⁠—urges me to speak. <br> The trampling of swift steeds is in my ears. <br> O that Ulysses and the gallant son <br> Of Tydeus might be bringing at this hour <br> Firm-footed coursers from the enemy’s camp! <br> Yet must I fear that these, our bravest chiefs, <br> Have met disaster from the Trojan crew.”</p>
<p class="verse">While he was speaking yet, the warriors came. <br> They sprang to earth; their friends, rejoicing, flocked <br> Around them, greeting them with grasp of hands <br> And with glad words, while the Gerenian knight, <br> Nestor, inquired: “Declare, illustrious chief, <br> Glory of Greece, Ulysses, how ye took <br> These horses: from the foe;⁠—or did some god <br> Bestow them? They are glorious as the sun. <br> Oft am I midst the Trojans, for, though old, <br> I lag not idly at the ships; yet ne’er <br> Have my eyes looked on coursers like to these. <br> Some god, no doubt, has given them, for to Jove, <br> The God of storms, and Pallas, blue-eyed child <br> Of aegis-bearing Jove, ye both are dear.”</p>
<p class="verse">Then sage Ulysses answered: “Pride of Greece! <br> Neleian Nestor, truly might a god <br> Have given us nobler steeds than even these. <br> All power is with the gods. But these of which <br> Thou askest, aged man, are brought from Thrace, <br> And newly come. Brave Diomed hath slain <br> Their lord, and twelve companions by his side⁠— <br> All princes. Yet another victim fell⁠— <br> A spy whom, near our ships, we put to death⁠— <br> A man whom Hector and his brother chiefs <br> Sent forth by midnight to explore our camp.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake, and gayly caused the firm-paced steeds <br> To pass the trench; the other Greeks, well pleased, <br> Went with him. When they reached the stately tent <br> Of Diomed, they led the coursers on <br> To stalls where Diomed’s fleet horses stood <br> Champing the wholesome corn, and bound them there <br> With halters neatly shaped. Ulysses placed <br> Upon his galley’s stern the bloody spoil <br> Of Dolon, to be made an offering <br> To Pallas. Then, descending to the sea, <br> They washed from knees and neck and thighs the grime <br> Of sweat; and when in the salt wave their limbs <br> Were cleansed, and all the frame refreshed, they stepped <br> Into the polished basins of the bath, <br> And, having bathed and rubbed with fragrant oil <br> Their limbs, they sat them down to a repast, <br> And from a brimming jar beside them drew, <br> And poured to Pallas first, the pleasant wine.</p>
