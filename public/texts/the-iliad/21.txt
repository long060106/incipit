<p class="lede">Flight of the Trojans before Achilles, some toward Troy, and the rest toward the river Scamander⁠—Twelve Trojan youths made captive in the river, to be butchered at the funeral pile of Patroclus⁠—Insult offered by Achilles to the god of the river, who causes his waters to rush against him, and forces him to flee for his life⁠—Interference of Vulcan, who is summoned by Juno to the aid of Achilles, and who, by drying up the waters of the river, compels it to submit⁠—Combat of Mars and Minerva, and of the other gods⁠—Achilles decoyed away from the gates of Troy by Apollo disguised in the form of Agenor, while the Trojans enter the city.</p>
<p class="verse">Now when they reached the pleasant banks <br> The eddying Xanthus runs, the river sprung <br> From deathless Jove, Achilles drave his foes through which <br> Asunder. Part he chased across the plain <br> Townward, along the way by which the Greek <br> In terror fled the day before, pursued <br> By glorious Hector. Panic-struck they ran <br> Along that way, while, to restrain their flight, <br> Before them Juno hung a veil of cloud <br> And darkness. Meanwhile half the flying crowd <br> Leaped down to that deep stream and rolled among <br> Its silver eddies. With a mighty noise <br> They plunged; the torrent dashed; the banks around <br> Remurmured shrilly to the cries of those <br> Who floated struggling in the current’s whirl, <br> As when before the fierce, devouring flames <br> A swarm of locusts, springing into air, <br> Fly toward a river, while the fire behind <br> Crackles with sudden fierceness, and in fright <br> They fall into the waves, the roaring stream <br> Of the deep-eddied Xanthus thus was filled <br> Before Achilles with a mingled crowd <br> Of steeds and men. The Jove-descended man <br> Left leaning on the tamarisks his spear <br> Upon the river’s border, and leaped in, <br> Armed only with his sword, intent to deal <br> Death on the fugitives; on every side <br> He smote, and from the smitten by the sword <br> Rose lamentable cries; the waves around <br> Grew crimson with their blood. As when before <br> A dolphin of huge bulk the fishes flee <br> In fear, and crowd the creeks that lie around <br> The sheltered haven⁠—for their foe devours <br> All that he overtakes⁠—the Trojans thus <br> Hid from his sight among the hollow rocks <br> Beside the rushing river. When his hand <br> Was weary with the work of death, he took <br> Twelve youths alive, whose blood was yet to pay <br> The penalty for Menoetiades, <br> His slaughtered friend. He led them from the stream, <br> Passive with fear like fawns, and tied their hands <br> Behind them with the well-twined cords that bound <br> Their tunics. Then he gave them to his friends, <br> Who led the captives to the roomy ships.</p>
<p class="verse">Again Achilles rushed upon the foe <br> Intent on slaughter. One he met who climbed <br> The river’s bank, Dardanian Priam’s son, <br> Lycaon, whom in former days he made <br> His captive, by surprise, when in the night <br> He found him lopping with an axe the boughs <br> Of a wild fig-tree, that the trunk might form <br> The circle of a wheel. Achilles came, <br> An unexpected foe, and bore him off <br> To sea, and sold him in the populous isle <br> Of Lemnos. He was bought by Jason’s son, <br> The Imbrian prince, Eëtion, who had been <br> His host, and now redeemed him with large gifts, <br> And sent him to Arisba’s noble town. <br> Yet thence he stole, and reached his father’s house <br> Again, and there made merry with his friends <br> Eleven days, but on the twelfth a god <br> Delivered him again into the hands <br> Of Peleus’ son, who now would send his soul <br> Repining down to Hades. When the chief, <br> The swift of foot, beheld him stand unarmed, <br> With neither helm nor shield nor spear⁠—for these <br> He had thrown down⁠—faint with the sweaty toil <br> Of clambering up the bank, and every limb <br> Unstrung with weariness, then wrathfully <br> Thus said Achilles to his mighty soul:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“O strange! My eyes behold a miracle. <br> Sure, the brave sons of Troy whom I have slain <br> Will rise up from the nether darkness yet, <br> Since this man, whom I once reprieved from death <br> And sold in Lemnos the divine, comes back. <br> Nor could the ocean’s gray abyss of brine, <br> Beyond which many long in vain to pass, <br> Detain him in that isle. But he shall taste <br> The sharpness of my spear, that I may prove <br> Whether he after that will reappear, <br> And whether the kind earth, which holds so well <br> The valiant dead, can keep him in her womb.”</p>
<p class="verse">So pondered he and stood. The Trojan drew <br> Close to him, with intent to clasp his knees, <br> Fear-struck, yet hoping to avoid the doom <br> Of bitter death. The great Achilles raised <br> His ponderous spear to strike. Lycaon stooped, <br> And, darting underneath the weapon, seized <br> The hero’s knees; behind him in the ground <br> The spear stood fixed, though eager yet for blood; <br> One arm was round his adversary’s knees, <br> The other held⁠—and would not let it go⁠— <br> The spear, while thus with wingèd words he prayed:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“I clasp thy knees, Achilles; look on me <br> Kindly and pity me, O foster-child <br> Of Jove. I am thy suppliant, and may claim <br> Thy mercy. I partook with thee the fruits <br> Of Ceres, when amid my fruitful fields <br> Thou madest me a captive, carrying me <br> From friends and kindred to the sacred isle <br> Of Lemnos. Thou didst sell me there⁠—my price <br> A hundred beeves⁠—and thou shalt now receive, <br> For ransom, thrice as many. It is yet <br> But the twelfth morning since I came to Troy <br> After much hardship, and a pitiless fate <br> Betrays me to thy hands. I must believe <br> That Father Jove in wrath delivers me <br> To thee again. Laothoe brought me forth <br> To a brief life; that mother was the child <br> Of aged Altes⁠—Altes ruling o’er <br> The warlike Leleges, by whom are tilled <br> The heights of Pedasus, where Satnio flows⁠— <br> And Priam wedded her with other maids. <br> She bore two children to be slain by thee; <br> One was the godlike Polydore, whom thou <br> Didst smite with thy keen spear, in the front rank <br> Of those who fought on foot. His evil fate <br> Must overtake me now, for, since a god <br> Has brought me near thee, there is no escape. <br> Yet let me tell thee this, and weigh it well, <br> And let it save my life. I came not forth <br> From the same womb with Hector, by whose hand <br> Thy brave and gentle friend, Patroclus, died.”</p>
<p class="verse">The illustrious son of Priam ended here <br> His prayer, and heard a merciless reply:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Fool! Never talk of ransom⁠—not a word. <br> Before the evil day on which my friend <br> Was slain, it pleased me oftentimes to spare <br> The Trojans. Many a one I took alive <br> And sold; but now no man of all their race, <br> Whom any god may bring within my reach, <br> Shall leave the field alive, and least of all <br> The sons of Priam. Die thou, then; and why <br> Shouldst thou, my friend, lament? Patroclus died, <br> And greatly he excelled thee. Seest thou not <br> How eminent in stature and in form <br> Am I, whom to a prince renowned for worth <br> A goddess mother bore; yet will there come <br> To me a violent death at morn, at eve, <br> Or at the midday hour, whenever he <br> Whose weapon is to take my life shall cast <br> The spear or send an arrow from the string.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake: the Trojan’s heart and knees grew faint; <br> His hand let go the spear; he sat and cowered <br> With outstretched arms. Achilles drew his sword, <br> And smote his neck just at the collar-bone; <br> The two-edged blade was buried deep. He fell <br> Prone on the earth; the black blood spouted forth <br> And steeped the soil. Achilles by the foot <br> Flung him to float among the river-waves, <br> And uttered, boastfully, these wingèd words:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Lie there among the fishes, who shall feed <br> Upon thy blood unscared. No mother there <br> Shall weep thee lying on thy bier; thy corpse <br> Scamander shall bear down to the broad sea, <br> Where, as he sees thee darkening its face, <br> Some fish shall hasten, darting through the waves, <br> To feed upon Lycaon’s fair white limbs. <br> So perish ye, till sacred Troy be ours, <br> You fleeing, while I follow close and slay. <br> This river cannot aid you⁠—this fair stream <br> With silver eddies, to whose deity <br> Ye offer many beeves in sacrifice, <br> And fling into its gulfs your firm-paced steeds; <br> But thus ye all shall perish, till I take <br> Full vengeance for Patroclus of the Greeks, <br> Whom, while I stood aloof from war, ye slew.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake: and, deeply moved with inward wrath, <br> The River pondered how to render vain <br> The prowess of Achilles, and avert <br> Destruction from the Trojans. Now the son <br> Of Peleus rushed, his ponderous spear in hand, <br> To slay Asteropasus, who was sprung <br> From Pelegon, and Pelegon was born <br> To the broad river Axius, of a maid, <br> The eldest-born of Acessamenus, <br> Named Periboea; for the river-god <br> Was joined with her in love. Achilles sprang <br> To meet the youth, as, rising from the stream, <br> Armed with two spears, he stood, his heart made strong <br> And resolute by Xanthus, who had seen <br> Indignantly so many Trojans die⁠— <br> Youths whom Achilles slaughtered in his stream, <br> And had no pity on them. When the twain <br> Were near each other, standing face to face, <br> The swift Achilles was the first to speak:⁠— <br> “Who and whence art thou that dost venture thus <br> To meet me? They who seek to measure strength <br> With me are sons of most unhappy men.”</p>
<p class="verse">And thus the illustrious son of Pelegon <br> Made answer: “Brave Pelides, why inquire <br> My lineage? I am from a distant coast⁠— <br> Paeonia’s fertile fields; I lead to war <br> Paeonia’s warriors with long spears, and this <br> Is now the eleventh morning since I came <br> To join the war at Troy. I claim descent <br> From Axius, the broad Axius, who pours forth <br> The fairest river on the earth. His son <br> Was Pelegon, expert to wield the spear, <br> And I was born to Pelegon. And now, <br> Illustrious son of Peleus, let us fight.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake: Achilles raised the Pelian ash <br> To smite; Asteropaeus aimed at him <br> Both lances, for he used both hands alike. <br> One struck the Grecian’s shield, yet passed not through, <br> Stopped by the god-given gold; the other gashed <br> Lightly the elbow of his dexter arm; <br> The black blood spouted forth, the spear passed on <br> Beyond him, and, still eager for its prey, <br> Stood fixed in earth. Achilles then, intent <br> To slay Asteropaeus, hurled at him <br> His trusty spear. The weapon missed its mark, <br> And, striking the high bank, was buried there <br> Up to the middle of its ashen staff. <br> Achilles drew the keen sword from his thigh, <br> And flew with fury toward his foe, who toiled <br> In vain with sinewy arm to pluck that spear <br> From out the bank; and thrice he shook the beam <br> Fiercely, and thrice desisted, lacking strength, <br> And last he sought, by bending it, to break <br> The ashen weapon of Aeacides. <br> But ere it snapped Achilles took his life, <br> Smiting him at the navel with the sword. <br> Forth gushed the entrails to the ground, and o’er <br> His dying eyes the darkness came; and then <br> Achilles, leaping on his breast, tore off <br> The armor, and exultingly exclaimed:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Lie there! A perilous task it was for thee <br> To combat with a son of Jove, though born <br> Thyself to a great River. I can boast <br> Descent from sovereign Jove. I owe my birth <br> To Peleus, ruler of the Myrmidons. <br> His father was Aeacus, who was born <br> To Jupiter, a god more potent far <br> Than all the rivers flowing to the sea. <br> And mightier is the race of Jupiter <br> Than that of any stream. Here close at hand <br> Is a great river, if such aid can aught <br> Avail thee; but to strive with Jupiter <br> Is not permitted. Acheloüs, king <br> Of rivers, cannot vie with him, nor yet <br> The great and mighty deep from which proceed <br> All streams and seas and founts and watery depths. <br> He trembles at the bolt of mighty Jove <br> And his hoarse thunder crashing in the sky.”</p>
<p class="verse">As thus he spake he plucked from out the bank <br> His brazen spear, and left the lifeless chief <br> Stretched in the sand, where the dark water steeped <br> His limbs, and eels and fishes came and gnawed <br> The warrior’s reins. Achilles hastened on, <br> Pursuing the Pseonian knights, who now, <br> When they beheld their bravest overthrown <br> In desperate battle by the mighty arm <br> And falchion of Pelides, took to flight <br> Along the eddying river. There he slew <br> Mydon, Thersilochus, Astypylus, <br> Mnesus, and Thrasius, and struck down in death <br> Aenius and Ophelestes. Many more <br> Of the Pseonians the swift-footed Greek <br> Had slain, had not the eddying River, roused <br> To anger, put a human semblance on, <br> And uttered from its whirling deeps a voice:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“O son of Peleus! Thou who dost excel <br> All other men in might and dreadful deeds⁠— <br> For the gods aid thee ever⁠—if the son <br> Of Saturn gives thee to destroy the race <br> Of Trojans, drive them from me to the plain, <br> And there perform thy terrible exploits. <br> For now my pleasant waters, in their flow, <br> Are choked with heaps of dead, and I no more <br> Can pour them into the great deep, so thick <br> The corpses clog my bed, while thou dost slay <br> And sparest not. Now then, withhold thy hand, <br> Prince of the people! I am horror-struck.”</p>
<p class="verse">Achilles the swift-footed made reply: <br> “Be it as thou commandest, foster-child <br> Of Jove, Scamander! Yet I shall not cease <br> To slay these treaty-breakers till at length <br> I shut them up within their town, and force <br> Hector to meet me, that we may decide <br> Which shall o’ercome the other⁠—he or I.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake, and rushed upon the men of Troy, <br> Terrible as a god, while from his bed <br> The eddying River called to Phoebus thus:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Why this, thou bearer of the silver bow, <br> Thou son of Jove? Thou heedest not the will <br> Of Saturn’s son, who strictly bade that thou <br> Shouldst aid the Trojans till the latest gleam <br> Of sunset, and till night is on the fields.”</p>
<p class="verse">And then Achilles, mighty with the spear, <br> From the steep bank leaped into the mid-stream, <br> While, foul with ooze, the angry River raised <br> His waves, and pushed along the heaps of dead <br> Slain by Achilles. These, with mighty roar <br> As of a bellowing ox, Scamander cast <br> Aground; the living with his whirling gulfs <br> He hid, and saved them in his friendly streams. <br> In tumult terribly the surges rose <br> Around Achilles, beating on his shield, <br> And made his feet to stagger, till he grasped <br> A tall, fair-growing elm upon the bank. <br> Down came the tree, and in its loosened roots <br> Brought the earth with it; the fair stream was checked <br> By the thick branches, and the prostrate trunk <br> Bridged it from side to side. Achilles sprang <br> From the deep pool, and fled with rapid feet <br> Across the plain in terror. Nor did then <br> The mighty river-god refrain, but rose <br> Against him with a darker crest, to drive <br> The noble son of Peleus from the field, <br> And so deliver Troy. Pelides sprang <br> A spear’s cast backward⁠—sprang with all the speed <br> Of the black eagle’s wing, the hunter-bird, <br> Fleetest and strongest of the fowls of air. <br> Like him he darted; clashing round his breast, <br> The brazen mail rang fearfully. Askance <br> He fled; the water with a mighty roar <br> Followed him close. As, when a husbandman <br> Leads forth, from some dark spring of earth, a rill <br> Among his planted garden-beds, and clears <br> Its channel, spade in hand, the pebbles there <br> Move with the current, which runs murmuring down <br> The sloping surface and outstrips its guide⁠— <br> So rushed the waves where’er Achilles ran, <br> Swift as he was; for mightier are the gods <br> Than men. As often as the noble son <br> Of Peleus made a stand in hope to know <br> Whether the deathless gods of the great heaven <br> Conspired to make him flee, so often came <br> A mighty billow of the Jove-born stream <br> And drenched his shoulders. Then again he sprang <br> Away; the rapid torrent made his knees <br> To tremble, while it swept, where’er he trod, <br> The earth from underneath his feet. He looked <br> To the broad heaven above him, and complained:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Will not some god, O Father Jove, put forth <br> His power to save me in my hour of need <br> From this fierce river? Any fate but this <br> I am resigned to suffer. None of all <br> The immortal ones is more in fault than she <br> To whom I owe my birth; her treacherous words <br> Deluded me to think that I should fall <br> Beneath the walls of Troy by the swift shafts <br> Of Phoebus. Would that Hector, the most brave <br> Of warriors reared upon the Trojan soil, <br> Had slain me; he had slain a brave man then, <br> And a brave man had stripped me of my arms. <br> But now it is my fate to perish, caught <br> In this great river, like a swineherd’s boy, <br> Who in the time of rains attempts to pass <br> A torrent, and is overwhelmed and drowned.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake, and Neptune and Minerva came <br> Quickly and stood beside him. In the form <br> Of men they came, and took his hand, and cheered <br> His spirit with their words. And thus the god <br> Neptune, who makes the earth to tremble, said:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Fear not, Pelides, neither let thy heart <br> Be troubled, since thou hast among the gods, <br> By Jove’s consent, auxiliars such as I <br> And Pallas. It is not thy doom to be <br> Thus vanquished by a river. Soon its rage <br> Will cease, as thou shalt see. Meantime we give <br> This counsel; heed it well: let not thy hand <br> Refrain from slaughter till the Trojan host <br> Are all shut up⁠—all that escape thy arm⁠— <br> Within the lofty walls of Troy. Then take <br> The life of Hector, and return on board <br> Thy galleys; we will make that glory thine.”</p>
<p class="verse">Thus having spoken, they withdrew and joined <br> The immortals, while Achilles hastened on, <br> Encouraged by the mandate of the gods, <br> Across the plain. The plain was overflowed <br> With water; sumptuous arms were floating round, <br> And bodies of slain youths. Achilles leaped, <br> And stemmed with powerful limbs the stream, and still <br> Went forward; for Minerva mightily <br> Had strengthened him. Nor did Scamander fail <br> To put forth all his power, enraged the more <br> Against the son of Peleus; higher still <br> His torrent swelled and tossed with all its waves, <br> And thus he called to Simoïs with a shout:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“O brother, join with me to hold in check <br> This man, who threatens soon to overthrow <br> King Priam’s noble city; for no more <br> The Trojan host resist him. Come at once <br> And aid me; fill thy channel from its springs, <br> And summon all thy brooks, and lift on high <br> A mighty wave, and roll along thy bed, <br> Mingled in one great torrent, trees and stones, <br> That we may tame this savage man, who now <br> In triumph walks the field, and bears himself <br> As if he were a god. His strength, I deem, <br> Will not avail him, nor his noble form, <br> Nor those resplendent arms, which yet shall lie <br> Scattered along the bottom of my gulfs, <br> And foul with ooze. Himself too I shall wrap <br> In sand, and pile the rubbish of my bed <br> In heaps around him. Never shall the Greeks <br> Know where to gather up his bones, o’erspread <br> By me with river-slime, for there shall be <br> His burial-place; no other tomb the Greeks <br> Will need when they perform his funeral rites.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake, and wrathfully he rose against <br> Achilles⁠—rose with turbid waves, and noise, <br> And foam, and blood and bodies of the dead. <br> One purple billow of the Jove-born stream <br> Swelled high and whelmed Achilles. Juno saw, <br> And trembled lest the hero should be whirled <br> Downward by the great river, and in haste <br> She called to Vulcan, her beloved son:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Vulcan, my son, arise! We deemed that thou <br> And eddying Xanthus were of equal might <br> In battle. Come with instant aid, and bring <br> Thy vast array of flames, while from the deep <br> I call a tempest of the winds⁠—the West <br> And the swift South⁠—and they shall sweep along <br> A fiery torrent to consume the foe, <br> Warriors and weapons. Thou meantime lay waste <br> The groves along the Xanthus; hurl at him <br> Thy fires, nor let him with soft words or threats <br> Avert thy fury. Pause not from the work <br> Of ruin till I shout and give the sign, <br> And then shalt thou restrain thy restless fires.”</p>
<p class="verse">She spake, and Vulcan at her word sent forth <br> His fierce, devouring flames. Upon the plain <br> They first were kindled, and consumed the dead <br> That strewed it, where Achilles struck them down. <br> The ground was dried; the glimmering flood was stayed. <br> As when the autumnal north-wind, breathing <br> A newly watered garden, quickly dries <br> The clammy mould, and makes the tiller glad, <br> So did the spacious plain grow dry on which <br> The dead were turned to ashes. Then the god <br> Seized on the river with his glittering fires. <br> The elms, the willows, and the tamarisks <br> Fell, scorched to cinders, and the lotus-herbs, <br> Rushes, and reeds that richly fringed the banks <br> Of that fair-flowing current were consumed. <br> The eels and fishes, that were wont to glide <br> Hither and thither through the pleasant depths <br> And eddies, languished in the fiery breath <br> Of Vulcan, mighty artisan. The strength <br> Of the great River withered, and he spake:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“O Vulcan, there is none of all the gods <br> Who may contend with thee. I combat not <br> With fires like thine. Cease then. With my consent <br> The noble son of Peleus may drive out <br> The Trojans from their city. What have I <br> To do with war⁠—the attack or the defence?”</p>
<p class="verse">Thus in that fiery glow he spake, while seethed <br> His pleasant streams. As over a strong fire <br> A cauldron filled with fat of pampered swine <br> Glows bubbling on all sides, while underneath <br> Burns the dry fuel, thus were his fair streams <br> Scorched by the heat, and simmered, while the blast <br> Sent forth by Vulcan, the great artisan, <br> Tormented him, and he besought the aid <br> Of Juno with these supplicating words:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Why should thy son, O Juno, wreak on me <br> His fury, more than on the other gods? <br> My fault is less than theirs who give their aid <br> To Troy; and I will cease, if thou command. <br> Bid him desist, and here I pledge my oath <br> Not to attempt to save the Trojan race <br> From ruin, though their city sink in flames <br> Before the torches of the warlike Greeks.”</p>
<p class="verse">This when the white-armed goddess Juno heard, <br> She said to Vulcan, her beloved son:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Dear son, refrain; it is not well that thus <br> A god should suffer for the sake of men.”</p>
<p class="verse">She spake, and Vulcan quenched his dreadful fires, <br> And back the pleasant waters to their bed <br> Went gliding. Xanthus had been made to yield, <br> And the two combatants no longer strove <br> Since Juno, though offended, bade them cease,</p>
<p class="verse">Yet was the conflict terrible among <br> The other gods, as zeal for different sides <br> Impelled them. With a loud uproar they met <br> Each other in the field; the spacious earth <br> Rebellowed to the noise, and the great heaven <br> Returned it. To the ear of Jove it rose, <br> Who, sitting on Olympus, laughed within <br> His secret heart as he beheld the gods <br> Contending, for not long they stood apart. <br> Shield-breaking Mars began the assault; he rushed <br> Toward Pallas, brandishing his brazen spear, <br> And thus accosted her with insolent words:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Thou shameless one, thou whose effrontery <br> Is boundless, why wilt thou provoke the gods <br> To strife? Thy temper is most arrogant. <br> Rememberest thou the time when thou didst prompt <br> Tydides Diomed to strike at me? <br> It was thy hand that held his shining spear, <br> And aimed it well, and gave the wound; but now <br> Will I take vengeance on thee for that wrong.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake, and smote Minerva’s fringèd shield, <br> The dreadful aegis, which not even Jove <br> Could pierce with thunderbolts. The murderous Mars <br> Smote it with his huge spear. She only stepped <br> Backward a space, and with her powerful hand <br> Lifted a stone that lay upon the plain, <br> Black, huge, and jagged, which the men of old <br> Had placed there for a landmark. This she hurled <br> At Mars, and struck him on the neck; he fell <br> With nerveless limbs, and covered, as he lay, <br> Seven acres of the field: his armor clashed <br> Around him in his fall; his locks all soiled <br> Lay in the trodden dust. The goddess stood <br> O’er him, and boasted thus with wingèd words:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Fool that thou art, hast thou not learned how much <br> The might I boast excels thine own, that thus <br> Thou measurest strength with me? Now dost thou feel <br> Thy mother’s curse fulfilled, who meditates <br> Thy chastisement, since thou hast left the Greeks <br> And joined the treaty-breaking sons of Troy.”</p>
<p class="verse">She spake, and turned away her glorious eyes. <br> Jove’s daughter, Venus, took the hand of Mars, <br> And led him groaning thence, while hardly yet <br> His strength came back. The white-armed Juno saw, <br> And spake to Pallas thus, with wingèd words:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“See, daughter of the Aegis-bearer, Jove, <br> Unconquerable maid! That shameless one, <br> Through all the tumult, from the thick of fight, <br> Leads hence the murderous Mars; but follow her.”</p>
<p class="verse">She spake, and Pallas gladly hastened forth, <br> And, overtaking Venus, dealt at her <br> A mighty buffet on the breast; her heart <br> Fainted, her knees gave way; and, as she lay <br> Prostrate with Mars upon the fruitful earth, <br> Exulting Pallas spake these wingèd words:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Would that all those who aid the cause of Troy <br> And combat with the mailèd Greeks were thus! <br> Would that they were as hardy and as brave <br> As Venus here, who ventured to the help <br> Of Mars, and met the force of my right arm! <br> Then had the stately Ilium been o’erthrown <br> Long since, and we had rested from the war.”</p>
<p class="verse">She spake: the white-armed Juno gently smiled. <br> And then King Neptune to Apollo said:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Why, Phoebus, stand we thus aloof? it ill <br> Becomes us, while the other gods engage <br> In conflict. ’Twere a shame should we return <br> Up to Olympus and the brazen halls <br> Of Jove with no blow struck. Begin, for thou <br> Art younger born, and I, who both in years <br> And knowledge am before thee, must not make <br> The assault. O silly god, and slow of thought! <br> Hast thou indeed forgotten all the wrongs <br> We suffered once in Troy, and only we <br> Of all the gods, when, sent to earth by Jove, <br> We served a twelvemonth for a certain hire <br> The proud Laomedon, by whom our tasks <br> Were set? I built a city and a wall <br> Of broad extent, and beautiful, and strong <br> To stand assault; and, Phoebus, thou didst feed <br> His stamping oxen, with curved horns, among <br> The lawns of woody Ida seamed with glens. <br> But when the welcome hours had brought the day <br> Of our reward, the ruffian king refused <br> The promised wages, and dismissed us both <br> With menaces; to bind thee hand and foot <br> He threatened, and to sell thee as a slave <br> In distant isles, and to cut off the ears <br> Of both of us. So we returned to heaven, <br> Incensed at him who thus withheld the hire <br> He promised. Dost thou favor Troy for this? <br> Wilt thou not rather act with us until <br> These treaty-breakers, with their children all <br> And their chaste matrons, perish utterly?”</p>
<p class="verse">Then thus the archer-king, Apollo, spake: <br> “Thou wouldst not deem me wise, should I contend <br> With thee, O Neptune, for the sake of men, <br> Who flourish like the forest-leaves awhile, <br> And feed upon the fruits of earth and then <br> Decay and perish. Let us quit the field, <br> And leave the combat to the warring hosts.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake, and turned, afraid to meet in arms <br> His uncle; but the sylvan Dian heard⁠— <br> His sister, mistress of the beasts that range <br> The wilds⁠—and harshly thus upbraided him:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“O mighty Archer, dost thou flee and yield <br> The victory to Neptune, who bears off <br> A glory cheaply earned? Why dost thou bear <br> That idle bow, thou coxcomb? I shall hope <br> No more to hear thee in our father’s halls. <br> And in the presence of the immortals, boast <br> That thou wilt fight with Neptune hand to bard.”</p>
<p class="verse">The archer-god, Apollo, answered not; <br> But thus the imperial wife of Jupiter, <br> Indignantly and with reproachful words, <br> Rebuked the quivered goddess of the chase:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“How is it that thou darest, shameless one, <br> Resist me? Thou wilt find it hard, though trained <br> In archery, to match thy strength with mine, <br> Though Jove has made thee among womankind <br> A lioness, and though he gives thee power <br> To slay whomever of thy sex thou wilt; <br> Yet wilt thou find it easier to strike down <br> The mountain beasts of prey, and forest deer, <br> Than combat with thy betters. If thou choose <br> To try the event of battle, then put forth <br> Thy strength against me, and thou shalt be taught <br> How greatly I excel in might of arm.”</p>
<p class="verse">Thus Juno spake, and grasped in her left hand <br> Both Dian’s wrists, and, plucking with her right <br> The quiver from her shoulders, beat with it <br> Her ears, and smiled as under her quick blows <br> The sufferer writhed. To earth the arrows fell, <br> And Dian weeping fled. As when a dove, <br> Not fated to be overtaken yet, <br> Flees from a hawk to find her hiding-place, <br> The hollow rock, so Dian fled in tears, <br> And left her arrows. To Latona, then, <br> Heaven’s messenger, the Argus-queller, spake:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Far be it from me to contend with thee, <br> Latona; perilous it were to meet <br> A consort of the Cloud-compeller, Jove, <br> In combat. Go and freely make thy boast <br> Among the gods that thou hast vanquished me.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake: Latona gathered from the ground <br> The bow and shafts which in that whirl of dust <br> Had fallen here and there, and, bearing them, <br> Followed her daughter, who meantime had reached <br> Olympus and the brazen halls of Jove. <br> And there, a daughter at her father’s knees, <br> She sat her down, while, as she wept, her robe <br> Of heavenly texture trembled. Graciously <br> Jove smiled, and drew her toward him and inquired: <br> “What dweller of the sky has dared do this, <br> Dear child, as though some flagrant guilt were thine?”</p>
<p class="verse">And thus replied the mistress of the chase <br> Crowned with the crescent: “Father, ’twas thy queen, <br> The white-armed Juno; she who causes strife <br> And wrath among the gods has done me wrong.”</p>
<p class="verse">So talked they, while to sacred Ilium came <br> Phoebus Apollo; ’twas his charge to watch <br> The well-built city’s ramparts, lest the Greeks <br> That day should lay it waste against the will <br> Of fate. The other gods went back to heaven, <br> Some angry, some exulting. They sat down <br> Beside the All-Father, him who darkens heaven <br> With gathered clouds. Meantime Achilles chased <br> And slew the Trojans and their firm-paced steeds. <br> As, when the smoke rolls heavenward from a town <br> Given by the angry gods a prey to fire, <br> Toil is the lot of all, and bitter woe <br> The fate of many, such the woe and toil <br> Caused by Achilles to the sons of Troy.</p>
<p class="verse">The aged Priam from a lofty tower <br> Beheld the large-limbed son of Peleus range <br> The field, and all the Trojans helplessly <br> Fleeing in tumult. With a cry of grief <br> He came from that high station to the ground, <br> And gave commandment to the sturdy men <br> Who stood to watch the gates along the wall:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Hold the gates open while the flying host <br> Enter the city; for Achilles comes, <br> Routing them, near at hand, and we may see <br> Terrible havoc. But when all our troops <br> Are once within the walls, and breathe again, <br> Shut the close-fitting portals; for I dread <br> Lest that fierce warrior rush into our streets.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake: they drew the bolts and opened wide <br> The gates, and gave a refuge to the host. <br> Then leaped Apollo forth to meet their flight <br> And rescue them. All faint with burning thirst, <br> And grimed with dust, they hurried o’er the plain, <br> And toward the city and its lofty walls, <br> While eagerly Achilles on their track <br> Pressed with his spear; his heart was full of rage, <br> And all on fire his spirit with desire <br> For glory. Then the Greeks had overthrown <br> The towery Troy, if Phoebus had not moved <br> Agenor, a young hero, nobly born, <br> Blameless, and brave, Antenor’s son, to meet <br> Achilles. Phoebus breathed into his heart <br> Courage, as, standing by the youth, he leaned <br> Against a beechen tree, and, wrapped from sight <br> In darkness, watched to rescue him from death. <br> Agenor stood as he beheld approach <br> The mighty spoiler, and, perplexed in mind, <br> Sighed heavily, and said to his great soul:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Ah me! If with the routed troops I flee <br> From fierce Achilles, he will overtake <br> And slay me; I shall die as cowards die. <br> But if I leave the host to be pursued <br> By Peleus’ son, and by another way <br> Flee from the wall across the plain, until <br> I reach the lawns of Ida, and am hid <br> Among its thickets, then I may at eve <br> Bathe in the river and return refreshed <br> To Troy. But why give way to thoughts like these? <br> For he may yet observe me as I haste <br> From Ilium o’er the plain, and his swift feet <br> May follow; there will then be no escape <br> From death and fate, since he in might of arm <br> Excels all other men. If now I here <br> Confront him before Troy, I cannot think <br> That he is weapon-proof; one life alone <br> Dwells in him, though Saturnian Jupiter <br> Bestows on him the glory of the day.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake, and firmly waited for the son <br> Of Peleus; eagerly his fearless heart <br> Longed for the combat. As a panther leaves <br> The covert of the wood and comes to meet <br> A huntsman, nor is scared nor put to flight <br> By noise of baying hounds, not even though <br> A spear’s thrust or a javelin flung from far <br> Have wounded him, yet, wounded, he fights on, <br> Until he grapples with his enemy <br> Or perishes⁠—thus did the noble son <br> Of the renowned Antenor press to try <br> His prowess with Achilles, and disdained <br> To flee before him. Holding his round shield <br> Before his face, and with his lifted spear <br> Aimed at the Greek, he shouted thus aloud:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Renowned Achilles! Thou dost fondly know <br> That thou today wilt overthrow the town <br> Of the magnanimous Trojans. Many toils, <br> Thou fool! must be endured ere that can be; <br> For we are many and are brave who dwell <br> Within it, and shall well defend the town <br> For our beloved parents and our wives <br> And little ones. Here shall thou meet thy doom, <br> Brave as thou art, and terrible in war.”</p>
<p class="verse">As thus he spake, his powerful hand dismissed <br> The keen-edged spear, nor missed his aim; it struck <br> The son of Peleus just below the knee. <br> The tin of which the greave was newly forged <br> Rang shrilly, and sent back the brazen point; <br> It could not pierce the armor which a god <br> Had given. And then the son of Peleus aimed <br> His weapon at Agenor. Phoebus came <br> And snatched away his triumph, bearing off <br> The godlike youth, Agenor, in a veil <br> Of darkness from the perils of the war. <br> Then he decoyed Achilles from the host <br> Of Troy; the archer of the skies put on <br> Agenor’s perfect semblance, and appeared <br> Before the Greek, and fled; his hasty flight <br> Was followed close. Achilles chased the god <br> Ever before him, yet still near, across <br> The fruitful fields, to the deep-eddied stream <br> Of Xanthus; for Apollo artfully <br> Made it to seem that he should soon o’ertake <br> His flying foe, and thus beguiled him on. <br> Meantime the routed Trojans gladly thronged <br> Into the city, filled the streets, and closed <br> The portals. None now dared without the walls <br> To wait for others, or remain to know <br> Who had escaped with life, and who were slain <br> In battle; eagerly they flung themselves <br> Into the city⁠—everyone whose feet <br> And knees had borne him from the field alive.</p>
