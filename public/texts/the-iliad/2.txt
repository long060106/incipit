<p class="lede">A treacherous dream sent by Jupiter to Agamemnon, who assembles the army in the hope to take Troy⁠—Debate of the chiefs in council⁠—Agamemnon pretends a desire to return to Greece, in order to try the disposition of the army⁠—Insolent speech of Thersites, and his punishment by Ulysses⁠—Advice of Nestor to review the troops⁠—Catalogue of the troops and ships⁠—Enumeration of the Trojan forces.</p>
<p class="verse">All other deities, all mortal men, <br> Tamers of war-steeds, slept the whole night through; <br> But no sweet slumber came to Jove; his thoughts <br> Were ever busy with the anxious care <br> To crown with honor Peleus’ son, and cause <br> Myriads to perish at the Grecian fleet. <br> At last, this counsel seemed the best⁠—to send <br> A treacherous dream to Agamemnon, son <br> Of Atreus. Then he called a Dream, and thus <br> Addressing it with wingèd words, he said:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Go, fatal Vision, to the Grecian fleet, <br> And, entering Agamemnon’s tent, declare <br> Faithfully what I bid thee. Give command <br> That now he arm, with all the array of war, <br> The long-haired Greeks, for lo, the hour is come <br> That gives into his hands the city of Troy <br> With all its spacious streets. The powers who dwell <br> In the celestial mansions are no more <br> At variance; Juno’s prayers have moved them all, <br> And o’er the Trojans hangs a fearful doom.”</p>
<p class="verse">So spake the God; the Vision heard, and went <br> At once to where the Grecian barques were moored, <br> And entered Agamemnon’s tent and found <br> The king reposing, with the balm of sleep <br> Poured all around him. At his head the Dream <br> Took station in the form of Neleus’ son, <br> Nestor, whom Agamemnon honored most <br> Of all the aged men. In such a shape <br> The heaven-sent Dream to Agamemnon spake:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“O warrior-son of Atreus, sleepest thou? <br> Tamer of steeds! It ill becomes a chief, <br> Who has the charge of nations and sustains <br> Such mighty cares, to sleep the livelong night. <br> Give earnest heed to me, for I am come <br> A messenger from Jove, who, though far off, <br> Takes part in thy concerns and pities thee. <br> He bids thee arm, with all the array of war, <br> The long-haired Greeks, for now the hour is come <br> Which gives into thy hands the city of Troy <br> With all its spacious streets. The powers that dwell <br> In the celestial mansions are no more <br> At variance; Juno’s prayers have moved them all, <br> And o’er the Trojans hangs a fearful doom, <br> Decreed by Jove. Bear what I say in mind, <br> And when thy sleep departs forget it not.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake, and, disappearing, left the king <br> Musing on things that never were to be; <br> For on that very day he thought to take <br> The city of Priam. Fool! who little knew <br> What Jupiter designed should come to pass, <br> And little thought by his own act to bring <br> Great woe and grief on Greeks and Trojans both <br> In hard-fought battles. From his sleep he woke, <br> The heavenly voice still sounding in his ears, <br> And sat upright, and put his tunic on, <br> Soft, fair, and new, and over that he cast <br> His ample cloak, and round his shapely feet <br> Laced the becoming sandals. Next, he hung <br> Upon his shoulders and his side the sword <br> With silver studs, and took into his hand <br> The ancestral sceptre, old, but undecayed, <br> And with it turned his footsteps toward the fleet <br> Of the Achaian warriors brazen-mailed.</p>
<p class="verse">Now Dawn, the goddess, climbed the Olympian height, <br> Foretelling Day to Jupiter and all <br> The immortal gods, when Agamemnon bade <br> The shrill-voiced heralds call the long-haired Greeks <br> Together; they proclaimed his will, and straight <br> The warriors came in throngs. But first he bade <br> A council of large-minded elders meet <br> On Pylian Nestor’s royal barque, and there <br> Laid his well-pondered thought before them:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“My friends, give ear: a Vision from above <br> Came to me sleeping in the balmy night; <br> Most like to noble Nestor was its look, <br> Its face, its stature, and its garb. It stood <br> Beside me at my head, and thus it spake:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“O warrior-son of Atreus, sleepest thou? <br> Tamer of steeds! It ill becomes a chief, <br> Who has the charge of nations and sustains <br> Such mighty cares, to sleep the livelong night. <br> Give earnest heed to me, for I am come <br> A messenger from Jove, who, though far off, <br> Takes part in thy concerns and pities thee. <br> He bids thee arm, with all the array of war, <br> The long-haired Greeks, for now the hour is come <br> Which gives into thy hands the city of Troy <br> With all its spacious streets. The powers who dwell <br> In the celestial mansions are no more <br> At variance; Juno’s prayers have moved them all, <br> And o’er the Trojans hangs a fearful doom, <br> Decreed by Jove. Bear what I say in mind.”</p>
<p class="verse">“It spake and passed away, and with it fled <br> My slumbers. Now must we devise a way <br> To bring into the field the sons of Greece. <br> I first will try, as best I may, with words, <br> And counsel flight from Troy with all our ships. <br> Ye each, with different counsels, do your part.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake, and took his seat, and after him <br> Nestor, the king of sandy Pylus, rose, <br> With well-considered words. “O friends,” he said, <br> “Leaders and princes of the Grecian race, <br> Had any other of the Argive host <br> Related such a dream, we should have said <br> The tale is false, and spurned the counsel given, <br> But he has seen it who in rank and power <br> Transcends us all, and ours it is to see <br> How we may arm for war the sons of Greece.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake, and left the council, and the rest, <br> All sceptred kings, arose, prepared to obey <br> The shepherd of the people. All the Greeks <br> Meanwhile came thronging to the appointed place. <br> As, swarming forth from cells within the rock, <br> Coming and coming still, the tribe of bees <br> Fly in a cluster o’er the flowers of spring, <br> And some are darting out to right and left, <br> So from the ships and tents a multitude <br> Along the spacious beach, in mighty throngs, <br> Moved toward the assembly. Rumor went with them, <br> The messenger of Jove, and urged them on. <br> And now, when they were met, the place was stunned <br> With clamor; earth, as the great crowd sat down, <br> Groaned under them; a din of mingled cries <br> Arose; nine shouting heralds strove to hush <br> The noisy crowd to silence, that at length <br> The heaven-descended monarchs might be heard.</p>
<p class="verse">And when the crowd was seated and had paused <br> From clamor, Agamemnon rose. He held <br> The sceptre; Vulcan’s skill had fashioned it, <br> And Vulcan gave it to Saturnian Jove, <br> And Jove bestowed it on his messenger, <br> The Argus-queller Hermes. He in turn <br> Gave it to Pelops, great in horsemanship; <br> And Pelops passed the gift to Atreus next, <br> The people’s shepherd. Atreus, when he died, <br> Bequeathed it to Thyestes, rich in flocks; <br> And last, Thyestes left it to be borne <br> By Agamemnon, symbol of his rule <br> O’er many isles and all the Argive realm. <br> Leaning on this, he spake these wingèd words:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Friends, Grecian heroes, ministers of Mars, <br> Saturnian Jove hath in an evil net <br> Entangled me most cruelly. He gave <br> His promise and his nod, that, having razed <br> Troy with her strong defences, I should see <br> My home again; but now he meditates <br> To wrong me, and commands me to return, <br> With lessened glory and much people lost, <br> To Argos. Thus hath it seemed good to Jove <br> The mighty, who hath overthrown the towers <br> Of many a city, and will yet o’erthrow. <br> The ages yet to come will hear with shame <br> That such a mighty army of the Greeks <br> Have waged a fruitless war, and fought in vain <br> A foe less numerous; yet no end appears <br> To this long strife. Should Greeks and Trojans make <br> A treaty, faithfully to number each, <br> And should the Trojans count their citizens, <br> And we the Greeks, disposed in rows of tens, <br> Should call the Trojans singly to pour out <br> The wine for us, full many a company <br> Of ten would lack its cup-bearer; so far, <br> I judge, the sons of Greece outnumber those <br> Who dwell in Troy. But they have yet allies <br> From many a city, men who wield the spear, <br> Withstanding my attempt to overthrow <br> That populous town. Nine years of mighty Jove <br> Have passed already, and the planks that form <br> Our barques are mouldering, and the cables drop <br> In pieces, and our wives within their homes, <br> With their young children, sit expecting us; <br> Yet is the enterprise for which we came <br> Still unperformed. Now let us all obey <br> The mandate I reveal, and hasten hence, <br> With all our fleet, to our beloved homes; <br> For Troy with her broad streets we cannot take.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake, and in the bosoms of the crowd <br> Stirred every heart; even those who heard him not <br> Were moved: the assembly wavered to and fro <br> Like the long billows of the Icarian Sea, <br> Roused by the East wind and the South, that rush <br> Forth from the cloudy seat of Father Jove; <br> Or like the harvest-field, when west winds stoop <br> Suddenly from above, and toss the wheat. <br> So was the whole assembly swayed; they ran <br> With tumult to the ships; beneath their feet <br> Rose clouds of dust, and each exhorted each <br> To seize the ships and drag them to the deep. <br> They cleared the channels mid the clamorous cries <br> Of multitudes, who hastened to return, <br> And drew the props from underneath their barques.</p>
<p class="verse">Then had the Greeks returned before their time <br> If Juno had not to Minerva said:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Unconquerable child of Jove! What change <br> Is this? Shall then the Argive army thus <br> Flee to their homes across the deep and leave <br> Glory to Priam, and to Ilium’s sons <br> The Argive Helen, for whose sake have died <br> So many Greeks upon the Trojan strand, <br> Far from the land they loved? But hasten thou <br> To the host of Argive warriors mailed in brass, <br> And with persuasive words restrain their men. <br> Nor let them launch their barques upon the sea.”</p>
<p class="verse">She spake; nor did the blue-eyed Pallas fail <br> To heed the mandate, but with quick descent <br> She left the Olympian height and suddenly <br> Stood by the swift ships of the Grecian host. <br> She found Ulysses there, the man endowed <br> With wisdom like to Jove’s; he had not touched <br> His well-appointed barque, for grief had seized <br> The hero’s heart. The blue-eyed goddess took <br> Her place beside him, and addressed him thus:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Son of Laertes, nobly born and sage <br> Ulysses, will ye, entering your good ships, <br> Return in flight to your own land and leave <br> Glory to Priam, and to Ilium’s sons <br> The Argive Helen, for whose sake have died <br> So many Greeks upon the Trojan strand, <br> Far from the land they loved? Go thou at once <br> And seek the Argive warriors and restrain <br> With thy persuasive words the impatient men, <br> Nor let them launch their well-appointed ships.”</p>
<p class="verse">She spake; Ulysses knew the heavenly voice, <br> And hastened back, and as he ran cast by <br> His cloak. Eurybates of Ithaca, <br> The herald, caught it as he followed him. <br> And now before Atrides, king of men, <br> The warrior stood, and from his hand received <br> The ancestral sceptre, old, but undecayed; <br> And bearing this, he went among the ships <br> Which brought the Achaian army, mailed in brass; <br> And whomsoe’er he met upon his way, <br> Monarch or eminent among the host, <br> He stopped him, and addressed him blandly, thus:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Good friend, this eager haste as if from fear <br> Befits thee not. Sit down, and cause the rest <br> To sit. What Agamemnon’s will may be <br> Thou canst not yet be certain; he intends <br> To try the Greeks, and soon will punish those <br> Who act amiss. We cannot all have heard <br> What he has said; beware, then, lest his wrath <br> Fall heavily upon the sons of Greece. <br> The monarch, foster-child of Jupiter, <br> Is terrible enraged. Authority <br> Is given by Jove, all-wise, who loves the king.”</p>
<p class="verse">But when he found one of the lower sort <br> Shouting and brawling, with the royal wand <br> He smote him, and reproved him sharply, thus:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Friend, take thy seat in quiet, and attend <br> To what thy betters say; thou art not strong <br> Nor valiant, and thou art of mean repute <br> In combat and in council. We, the Greeks, <br> Cannot be all supreme in power. The rule <br> Of the many is not well. One must be chief <br> In war, and one the king, to whom the son <br> Of Saturn gives the sceptre, making him <br> The lawgiver, that he may rule the rest.”</p>
<p class="verse">Thus did he act the chief, and make the host <br> Obey his word; they to the council ground <br> Came rushing back from all the ships and tents <br> With tumult, as, on the long-stretching shore <br> Of ocean many-voiced, his billows fling <br> Themselves in fury, and the deep resounds.</p>
<p class="verse">All others took their seats and kept their place; <br> Thersites only, clamorous of tongue, <br> Kept brawling. He, with many insolent words, <br> Was wont to seek unseemly strife with kings, <br> Uttering whate’er it seemed to him might move <br> The Greeks to laughter. Of the multitude <br> Who came to Ilium, none so base as he⁠— <br> Squint-eyed, with one lame foot, and on his back <br> A lump, and shoulders curving towards the chest; <br> His head was sharp, and over it the hairs <br> Were thinly scattered. Hateful to the chiefs <br> Achilles and Ulysses, he would oft <br> Revile them. He to Agamemnon now <br> Called with shrill voice and taunting words. The Greeks <br> Heard him impatiently, with strong disgust <br> And vehement anger, yet he shouted still <br> To Agamemnon, and kept railing on:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Of what dost thou complain; what wouldst thou more, <br> Atrides? In thy tents are heaps of gold; <br> Thy tents are full of chosen damsels, given <br> To thee before all others, by the Greeks, <br> Whene’er we take a city. Dost thou yet <br> Hanker for gold, brought by some Trojan knight, <br> A ransom for his son, whom I shall lead⁠— <br> I, or some other Greek⁠—a captive bound? <br> Or dost thou wish, for thy more idle hours, <br> Some maiden, whom thou mayst detain apart? <br> Ill it beseems a prince like thee to lead <br> The sons of Greece, for such a cause as this, <br> Into new perils. O ye coward race! <br> Ye abject Greeklings, Greeks no longer, haste <br> Homeward with all the fleet, and let us leave <br> This man at Troy to win his trophies here, <br> That he may learn whether the aid we give <br> Avails him aught or not, since he insults <br> Achilles, a far braver man than he, <br> And takes from him by force and holds his prize. <br> And yet, Achilles is not moved by this <br> To anger: he is spiritless, or else, <br> Atrides, this injustice were thy last.”</p>
<p class="verse">Taunting the shepherd of the people thus, <br> Thersites shouted to the king of men. <br> But great Ulysses, coming quickly up, <br> Rebuked him with a frown: “Thou garrulous wretch! <br> Glib as thou art of tongue, Thersites, cease, <br> Nor singly dare to seek dispute with kings. <br> There came, I deem, no viler wretch than thou <br> To Troy with Agamemnon. Prate no more <br> Of kings, reviling them, and keeping watch <br> For pretexts to return. We know not yet <br> Whether to go or to remain were best. <br> Thou railest at the shepherd of the host, <br> Atrides Agamemnon, for thou seest <br> The Grecian heroes load him with rewards, <br> While thou insultest him with scurrilous words. <br> I tell thee now⁠—and I shall keep my word⁠— <br> If e’er again I find thee railing on, <br> As now thou dost, then let Ulysses wear <br> His head no longer, let me not be called <br> The father of Telemachus, if I <br> Shall fail to seize thee, and to strip thee bare <br> Of cloak and tunic, and whatever else <br> Covers thy carcass, and to send thee forth, <br> Howling, to our swift barques upon the shore, <br> Scourged from the council with a storm of blows.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake, and with his sceptre smote the back <br> And shoulders of the scoffer, who crouched low <br> And shed a shower of tears. A bloody whelk <br> Rose where the golden sceptre fell. He took <br> His seat, dismayed, and still in pain wiped off <br> The tears from his smutched face. The multitude <br> Around him, though in anxious mood, were moved <br> To smiles, and one addressed his neighbor thus:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Strange that Ulysses does a thousand things <br> So well⁠—so wise in council, and in war <br> So brave; and for the Grecian army now <br> He does the best of all, in silencing <br> The chatter of this saucy slanderer, <br> Whose acrid temper will not soon again <br> Move him to rail with insolent speech at kings.”</p>
<p class="verse">So talked the multitude. Ulysses then, <br> Holding the sceptre, rose, and by his side <br> The blue-eyed Pallas, in a herald’s form, <br> Commanded silence, that the Argive host⁠— <br> The mightiest and the meanest⁠—might attend <br> To what should now be said, and calmly weigh <br> The counsel given them. With a prudent art <br> Ulysses framed his speech, and thus he spake:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“The Greeks, O Atreus’ son, would bring on thee <br> Dishonor in the eyes and speech of men, <br> Breaking the promise made when first they came <br> From Argos, famed for steeds, that, having spoiled <br> This well-defended Troy, thou shouldst return <br> A conqueror. And now, like tender boys <br> Or widowed women, all give way to grief <br> And languish to return. ’Twere hard to bear <br> If, after all our sufferings and our toils, <br> We go back now. And yet, whoe’er remains <br> A single month away from wife and home <br> Chafes if the winter storms and angry sea <br> Detain him still on board his well-oared barque; <br> And we have seen the ninth full year roll round <br> Since we came hither. Therefore blame I not <br> The Greeks if they in their beaked ships repine <br> At this delay. But then it were disgrace <br> To linger here so long and journey home <br> With empty hands. Bear with us yet, and wait <br> Till it be certain whether Calchas speaks <br> Truly or not. For we remember well, <br> And all of you whom cruel death has spared <br> Are witnesses with me, that when the ships <br> Of Greece⁠—it seems as if but yesterday⁠— <br> Mustered in Aulis on their way to bring <br> Woe upon Priam and the town of Troy, <br> And we, beside a fountain, offered up <br> On sacred altars chosen hecatombs, <br> Under a shapely plane-tree, from whose root <br> Flowed the clear water, there appeared to us <br> A wondrous sign. A frightful serpent, marked <br> With crimson spots, which Jupiter sent forth <br> To daylight from beneath the altar-stone, <br> Came swiftly gliding toward the tree, whereon <br> A sparrow had her young⁠—eight unfledged birds⁠— <br> Upon the topmost bough and screened by leaves; <br> The mother was the ninth. The serpent seized <br> The helpless brood and midst their piteous cries <br> Devoured them, while the mother fluttered round, <br> Lamenting, till he caught her by the wing; <br> And when he had destroyed the parent bird <br> And all her brood, the god who sent him forth <br> Made him a greater marvel still. The son <br> Of crafty Saturn changed the snake to stone; <br> And we who stood around were sore amazed. <br> Such was the awful portent which the gods <br> Showed at that sacrifice. But Calchas thus <br> Instantly spake, interpreting the sign:⁠— <br> “ ‘O long haired Greeks,’ he said, ‘why stand ye thus <br> In silence? All-foreseeing Jupiter <br> Hath sent this mighty omen; late it comes <br> And late will be fulfilled, yet gloriously, <br> And with a fame that never shall decay. <br> For as the snake devoured the sparrow’s brood, <br> Eight nestlings, and the mother-bird the ninth⁠— <br> So many years the war shall last; the tenth <br> Shall give into our hands the stately Troy.’</p>
<p class="verse">“So spake the seer; thus far his words are true. <br> Bide ye then here, ye well-greaved sons of Greece, <br> Until the city of Priam shall be ours.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake, and loud applause thereon ensued <br> From all the Greeks, and fearfully the ships <br> Rang with the clamorous voices uttering <br> The praises of Ulysses and his words. <br> Then Nestor, the Gerenian knight, arose <br> And thus addressed them: “Strangely ye behave, <br> Like boys unwonted to the tasks of war. <br> Where now are all your promises and oaths? <br> Shall all our counselings and all our cares, <br> Leagues made with wine, religiously outpoured, <br> And plightings of the strong right hand, be cast <br> Into the flames? Idly we keep alive <br> A strife of words, which serves no end though long <br> We loiter here! But thou, Atrides, firm <br> Of purpose, give command that now the Greeks <br> Move to the war, and leave to meet their fate <br> Those⁠—one or more⁠—who, parting from our host, <br> Meditate⁠—but I deem in vain⁠—to flee <br> Homeward to Argos ere they are assured <br> Whether the word of Jove omnipotent <br> Be false or true. For when the Greeks embarked <br> In their swift ships, to carry death and fate <br> To Ilium’s sons, almighty Jupiter <br> Flung down his lightnings on the right and gave <br> Propitious omens. Therefore let no Greek <br> Go home till he possess a Trojan wife <br> And ye have signally avenged the wrongs <br> And griefs of Helen. Yet, if one be here <br> Who longs to go, let him but lay his hand <br> On his black ship, prepared to cross the deep, <br> And he shall die before the rest. But thou, <br> O king, be wisely counselled, lend an ear <br> To others, nor neglect what I propose. <br> Marshal the Greeks by tribes and brotherhoods, <br> That tribe may stand by tribe, and brotherhoods <br> Succor each other; if thou thus command <br> And they obey, thou shalt discern which chief <br> Or soldier is faint-hearted, which is brave, <br> For each will fight his best, and thou shalt know <br> Whether through favor of the gods to Troy, <br> Or our own cowardice and shameful lack <br> Of skill in war, the town is not o’erthrown.”</p>
<p class="verse">In turn the monarch Agamemnon spake:⁠— <br> “O aged warrior, thou excellest all <br> The Greeks in council. Would to Jupiter, <br> To Pallas and Apollo, that with me <br> There were but ten such comrades. Priam’s town <br> Would quickly fall before us and be made <br> A desolation. But the god who bears <br> The aegis, Saturn’s son, hath cast on me <br> Much grief, entangling me in idle strifes <br> And angry broils. Achilles and myself <br> Have quarrelled for a maid with bitter words, <br> And I was first incensed. But if again <br> We meet and act as friends, the overthrow <br> That threatens Ilium will not be delayed⁠— <br> Not for an hour. Now all to your repast! <br> And then prepare for battle. First let each <br> See that his spear be sharp, and put his shield <br> In order, give to his swift-footed steeds <br> Their ample forage, and o’erlook his car <br> That it be strong for war; for all the day <br> Shall we maintain the stubborn fight, nor cease <br> Even for a moment, till the night come down <br> To part the wrathful combatants. The band <br> Of each broad buckler shall be moist with sweat <br> On every breast, and weary every arm <br> That wields the spear, and every horse that drags <br> The polished chariot o’er the field shall smoke <br> With sweat. But whosoever shall be found <br> By the beaked ships and skulking from the fray <br> Shall be the feast of birds of prey and dogs!”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake; the Argives raised a mighty shout, <br> Loud as when billows lash the beetling shore, <br> Rolled by the south-wind toward some jutting rock <br> On which the waves, whatever wind may blow, <br> Beat ceaselessly. In haste the people rose <br> And went among the ships, and kindled fires <br> Within their tents and took their meal. And one <br> Made offerings to one god; another paid <br> Vows to another of the immortal race; <br> And all implored deliverance from death <br> And danger. Agamemnon, king of men, <br> Offered a fatted ox of five years old <br> To Jupiter Almighty, summoning <br> The elder princes of the Grecian host⁠— <br> Nestor the first, the king Idomeneus, <br> And then the warriors Ajax and the son <br> Of Tydeus, with Ulysses, like to Jove <br> In council, sixth and last. Unbidden came <br> The valiant Menelaus, for he knew <br> The cares that weighed upon his brother’s heart. <br> Then, as they stood around the fatted ox <br> And took in hand the salted barley-meal, <br> King Agamemnon in the circle prayed:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“O Jove, most great and glorious! who dost rule <br> The tempest⁠—dweller of the ethereal space! <br> Let not the sun go down and night come on <br> Ere I shall lay the halls of Priam waste <br> With fire, and give their portals to the flames, <br> And hew away the coat of mail that shields <br> The breast of Hector, splitting it with steel. <br> And may his fellow-warriors, many a one, <br> Fall round him to the earth and bite the dust.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake; the son of Saturn hearkened not, <br> But took the sacrifice and made more hard <br> The toils of war. And now when they had prayed, <br> And strown the salted meal, they drew the neck <br> Of the victim back and cut the throat and flayed <br> The carcass, hewed away the thighs and laid <br> The fat upon them in a double fold, <br> On which they placed raw strips of flesh, and these <br> They burned with leafless billets. Then they fixed <br> The entrails on the spits and held them forth <br> Above the flames, and when the thighs were burned <br> And entrails tasted, all the rest was carved <br> Into small portions and transfixed with spits <br> And roasted carefully and drawn away. <br> And when these tasks were finished and the board <br> Was spread, they feasted; from that equal feast <br> None went unsated. When they had appeased <br> Their thirst and hunger, the Gerenian knight <br> Nestor stood forth and spake: “Most glorious son <br> Of Atreus, Agamemnon, king of men! <br> Waste we no time in prattle, nor delay <br> The work appointed by the gods, but send <br> The heralds of the Achaians, brazen-mailed, <br> To call the people to the fleet, while we <br> Pass in a body through their vast array <br> And wake the martial spirit in their breasts.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake, and Agamemnon, king of men, <br> Followed the counsel. Instantly he bade <br> The loud-voiced herald summon to the war <br> The long-haired Argives. At the call they came, <br> Quickly they came together, and the kings, <br> Nurslings of Jupiter, who stood beside <br> Atrides, hastened through the crowd to form <br> The army into ranks. Among them walked <br> The blue-eyed Pallas, bearing on her arm <br> The priceless aegis, ever fair and new, <br> And undecaying; from its edge there hung <br> A hundred golden fringes, fairly wrought, <br> And every fringe might buy a hecatomb. <br> With this and fierce, defiant looks she passed <br> Through all the Achaian host, and made their hearts <br> Impatient for the march and strong to endure <br> The combat without pause⁠—for now the war <br> Seemed to them dearer than the wished return, <br> In their good galleys, to the land they loved.</p>
<p class="verse">As when a forest on the mountaintop <br> Is in a blaze with the devouring flame <br> And shines afar, so, while the warriors marched, <br> The brightness of their burnished weapons flashed <br> On every side and upward to the sky.</p>
<p class="verse">And as when water-fowl of many tribes⁠— <br> Geese, cranes, and long-necked swans⁠—disport themselves <br> In Asia’s fields beside Cayster’s streams, <br> And to and fro they fly with screams, and light, <br> Flock after flock, and all the fields resound; <br> So poured, from ships and tents, the swarming tribes <br> Into Scamander’s plain, where fearful’y <br> Earth echoed to the tramp of steeds and men; <br> And there they mustered on the river’s side, <br> Numberless as the flowers and leaves of spring, <br> And as when flies in swarming myriads haunt <br> The herdsman’s stalls in spring-time, when new milk <br> Has filled the pails⁠—in such vast multitudes <br> Mustered the long-haired Greeks upon the plain, <br> Impatient to destroy the Trojan race.</p>
<p class="verse">Then, as the goatherds, when their mingled flocks <br> Are in the pastures, know and set apart <br> Each his own scattered charge, so did the chiefs, <br> Moving among them, marshal each his men. <br> There walked King Agamemnon, like to Jove <br> In eye and forehead, with the loins of Mars, <br> And ample chest like him who rules the sea. <br> And as a bull amid the horned herd <br> Stands eminent and nobler than the rest, <br> So Jove to Agamemnon on that day <br> Gave to surpass the chiefs in port and mien.</p>
<p class="verse">O Muses, goddesses who dwell on high, <br> Tell me⁠—for all things ye behold and know, <br> While we know nothing and may only hear <br> The random tales of rumor⁠—tell me who <br> Were chiefs and princes of the Greeks; for I <br> Should fail to number and to name them all⁠— <br> Had I ten tongues, ten throats, a voice unapt <br> To weary, uttered from a heart of brass⁠— <br> Unless the Muses aided me. I now <br> Will sing of the commanders and the ships.</p>
<p class="verse">Peneleus, Prothoenor, Leitus, <br> And Clonius, and Arcesilaus led <br> The warriors of Boeotia, all who dwelt <br> In Hyria and in rocky Aulis, all <br> From Schoenus and from Scolus and the hill <br> Of Eteonus and Thespeia’s fields, <br> And Graia and the Mycalesian plain, <br> All who from Herma and Ilesius came, <br> And Erythrae, and those who had their homes <br> In Eleon, Hyla, and Ocalea, <br> And Peteona, and the stately streets <br> Of Medeon, Copae, Thisbè full of doves, <br> And those whose dwelling-place was Eutresis, <br> And Coronaea, and the grassy lawns <br> Of Haliartus, all the men who held <br> Plataea, or in Glissa tilled the soil, <br> Or dwelt in Hypothebae nobly built, <br> Or in Onchestus with its temple-walls <br> Sacred to Neptune, or inhabited <br> Arnè with fruitful vineyards, Midea <br> And Nyssa the divine, and Anthedon <br> The distant⁠—fifty were their barques, and each <br> Held sixscore youths of the Boeotian race.</p>
<p class="verse">Next, over those who came from Aspledon <br> And from Orchomenus in Minyas <br> Ascalaphus ruled with his brother chief <br> Ialmenus⁠—two sons of mighty Mars. <br> These, in the halls of Actor, Azis’ son, <br> Astyoche bore to the god of war, <br> Who met by stealth the bashful maid, as once <br> She sought the upper palace-rooms. Their ships <br> Were thirty, ranged in order on the shore.</p>
<p class="verse">Then Schedius and Epistrophus, two chiefs <br> Born to Iphitus, son of Naubolus <br> The large of soul, led the Phocean host, <br> Those who in Cyparissus had their homes, <br> In Panopè and Crissa the divine <br> And Daulis, or about Hyampolis <br> Anemoreia, and upon the banks of broad <br> Cephissus, and with them the race <br> Who held Lilaea by Cephissus’ springs. <br> With these came forty ships. Their leaders went <br> Among them, ranging them in due array <br> And close to the Boeotians on the left.</p>
<p class="verse">Ajax the swift of foot, Oileus’ son, <br> Was leader of the Locrians⁠—less in limb <br> And stature than the other Ajax⁠—nay, <br> Much smaller than that son of Telamon, <br> Wearing a linen corselet; but to wield <br> The spear he far excelled all other men <br> Of Hellas and Achaia. Those who dwelt <br> In Cynus, Opus, Bessa, and the fields <br> Of Scarpha and Calliarus and green <br> Augeia, Tarpha, and the meadows where <br> Boagrius waters Thronium, followed him <br> With forty dark-hulled Locrian barques, that came <br> From coasts beyond Eubrea’s sacred isle.</p>
<p class="verse">The Euboeans breathing valor, they who held <br> Chalcis, Eretria, and the vineyard slopes <br> Of Histiaea, and the lofty walls <br> Of Dium and Cerinthus by the sea, <br> And Styra, and Earystus; these obeyed <br> Elphenor of the line of Mars, and son <br> Of the large-souled Chalcodon ruler o’er <br> The Abantes. Him with loosely flowing locks <br> The Abantes followed, swift of foot and fierce <br> In combat, and expert to break the mail <br> Upon the enemies’ breasts with ashen spears; <br> With forty dark-hulled barques they followed him.</p>
<p class="verse">Next they who came from Athens nobly built, <br> The city of Erechtheus, great of soul, <br> Son of the teeming Earth, whom Pallas reared, <br> That daughter of the Highest, and within <br> Her sumptuous temple placed him, where the sons <br> Of Athens, with the circling year’s return, <br> Paid worship at her altars, bringing bulls <br> And lambs to lay upon them; these obeyed <br> Menestheus, son of Peteus, whom no chief <br> On earth could equal in the art to place <br> Squadrons of men and horse in due array <br> For battle. Nestor only sought to share <br> This praise, but Nestor was the elder chief. <br> Fifty dark galleys with Menestheus came.</p>
<p class="verse">Ajax had brought twelve ships from Salamis, <br> And these he stationed near the Athenian host.</p>
<p class="verse">But they who dwelt in Argos, or within <br> The strong-walled Tiryns, or Hermione <br> And Asine with their deep, sheltering bays, <br> Troezenè and Eïonae, and hills <br> Of Epidaurus planted o’er with vines, <br> And they who tilled Aeigina and the coast <br> Of Mases⁠—Grecian warriors⁠—over these <br> Brave Diomed bore sway, with Sthenelus, <br> Beloved son of far-famed Campaneus, <br> And, third in rule, Euryalus, who seemed <br> Like to a god, Mecisteus’ royal son <br> Who sprung from Talaus; yet the chief command <br> Was given to Diomed, the great in war. <br> A fleet of eighty galleys came with them.</p>
<p class="verse">The dwellers of Mycenae nobly built, <br> Of Corinth famed for riches, and the town <br> Of beautiful Cleonae, they who tilled <br> Orneia, Araethyrea’s pleasant land, <br> And Sicyon, where of yore Adrastus reigned, <br> And Hyperesia and the airy heights <br> Of Gonoessa, and Pellene’s fields, <br> And they who came from Aegium and the shores <br> Around it, and broad lands of Helicè⁠— <br> These had a hundred barques, and over them <br> Atrides Agamemnon bore command; <br> And with him came the largest train of troops <br> And bravest. He was cased in gleaming mail, <br> And his heart gloried when he thought how high <br> He stood among the heroes⁠—mightier far <br> In power, and leader of a mightier host.</p>
<p class="verse">Then they who dwelt within the hollow vale <br> Of queenly Lacedsemon, they who held <br> Phare and Sparta, Messa full of doves, <br> Bryseiae, and Augeia’s rich domain, <br> Amyclae and the town of Helos, built <br> Close to the sea, and those who had their homes <br> In Laäs and the fields of Oetylus; <br> All these obeyed the brother of the king, <br> The valiant Menelaus. Sixty ships <br> They brought, but these he ranged apart from those <br> Of Agamemnon. Through the ranks he went, <br> And, trusting in his valor, quickened theirs <br> For battle; for his heart within him burned <br> To avenge the wrongs of Helen and her tears.</p>
<p class="verse">Then came the men who tilled the Pylean coast <br> And sweet Arenè, Thrya at the fords <br> Of Alpheus, and the stately palace homes <br> Of Aepy, or in Cyparissus dwelt, <br> Or in Amphigeneia, Pteleum, <br> Helos and Dorium, where the Muses once <br> Met, journeying from Oecalian Eurytus, <br> The Thracian Thamyris, and took from him <br> His power of voice. For he had made his boast <br> To overcome in song the daughters nine <br> Of Jove the Aegis-bearer. They in wrath <br> Smote him with blindness, took the heavenly gift <br> Of song away, and made his hand forget <br> Its cunning with the harp. All those were led <br> By Nestor, the Gerenian knight, who came <br> To war on Troy with fourscore ships and ten.</p>
<p class="verse">The Arcadians, dwelling by the lofty mount <br> Cyllene, near the tomb of Epytus, <br> Warriors who combat hand to hand, and they <br> Who tilled the fields of Pheneus and possessed <br> Orchomenus with all its flocks, or dwelt <br> In Ripa and in Stratia, and the bleak <br> Enispe, beaten with perpetual winds, <br> And in Tegea, and the lovely land <br> Of Mantinea, and in Stymphalus <br> And in Parrhasia, came in sixty ships <br> To Troy, with Agapenor for their chief, <br> Son of Ancaeus. Every ship was thronged <br> With warriors of Arcadia, for the king <br> Of men, Atrides, gave them well-oared barques <br> To cross the dark blue deep, since not to them <br> Pertained the cares and labors of the sea.</p>
<p class="verse">Then from Buprasium and the sacred coast <br> Of Elis, from Hyrmine and remote <br> Myrsinus and the Olenian precipice, <br> And from Alisium came, with chieftains four, <br> The warriors, ten swift galleys following <br> Each chieftain, crowded with Epean troops. <br> And part obeyed Amphimacus, the son <br> Of Cteatus, and part with Thalpius came, <br> The son of Eurytus Actorides, <br> And part with brave Diores, of the line <br> Of Amarynceus. Last, Polyxenus, <br> The godlike offspring of Agasthenes, <br> Whose father was Augeias, led the rest.</p>
<p class="verse">They from Dulichium and the Echinades, <br> Those holy isles descried from Elis o’er <br> The waters, had for leader Megas, brave <br> As Mars⁠—the son of Phyleus, dearly loved <br> By Jove. He left his father’s house in wrath <br> And dwelt within Dulichium. With the troops <br> Of Megas came a fleet of forty ships.</p>
<p class="verse">Ulysses led the Cephallenian men, <br> Who dwelt in Ithaca, or whose abode <br> Was leafy Neritus, and those who came <br> From Crocyleia, and from Aegilips <br> The craggy, and Zacynthus, and the isle <br> Of Samos, and Epirus, and from all <br> The bordering lands. O’er these Ulysses ruled, <br> A chief like Jove in council, and with him <br> There came twelve galleys with their scarlet prows.</p>
<p class="verse">Then with the Aetolians came Andraemon’s son <br> Thoas, their leader. With him were the men <br> Of Pleuron and Pylene, Olenus, <br> And Chalcis on the sea-coast and the rocks <br> Of Calydon; for now no more the sons <br> Of large-souled Oeneus were alive on earth, <br> Nor lived the chief himself, and in his tomb <br> Was Meleager of the golden hair; <br> And thus the Aetolian rule to Thoas came. <br> A fleet of fourscore galleys followed him.</p>
<p class="verse">Idomeneus, expert to wield the spear, <br> Commanded those of Crete, the men who dwelt <br> In Cnosus or Gortyna, strongly walled <br> Lyctus, Miletus, and the glimmering <br> Lycastus, Phaestus, Rhytium’s populous town, <br> And all the warrior train inhabiting <br> The hundred towns of Crete. Idomeneus <br> The mighty spearman, and Meriones, <br> Fierce as the god of war, commanded these, <br> And came to Troy with eighty dark-ribbed barques.</p>
<p class="verse">Tlepolemus, a warrior of the stock <br> Of Hercules, was leader of the troops <br> Of Rhodes, and brought nine vessels to the war, <br> Manned with the haughty Rhodians. These were ranged <br> In threefold order: those of Lindus, those <br> Who dwell in white Camirus, lastly those <br> Of Ialassa. These Tlepolemus, <br> The valiant spearman, ruled. Astyoche <br> Bore him to mighty Hercules, who led <br> The maid from Ephyra, upon the banks <br> Of Selleis, to be his wife, what time <br> His valor had o’erthrown and made a spoil <br> Of many a city full of noble youths. <br> Tlepolemus, when in the palace-halls <br> He grew to manhood, slew an aged man, <br> An uncle of his father, whom he loved, <br> Lycimnius, of the line of Mars, and straight <br> He rigged a fleet of ships and led on board <br> A numerous host and fled across the sea. <br> For fearful were the threats of other sons <br> And grandsons of the mighty Hercules. <br> In Rhodes they landed after wanderings long <br> And many hardships. There they dwelt in tribes, <br> Three tribes⁠—and were beloved of Jupiter, <br> The ruler over gods and men, who poured <br> Abundant riches on their new abode.</p>
<p class="verse">Nireus with three good ships from Syma came, <br> Nireus, Aglaia’s son by Charopus <br> The monarch⁠—Nireus who in comeliness <br> Surpassed all Greeks that came to Ilium, save <br> The faultless son of Peleus. Yet was he <br> Unwarlike and few people followed him.</p>
<p class="verse">The dwellers of Nisyrus, Crapathus, <br> And Cos, the city of Eurypylus, <br> Casus, and the Calydnian isles, obeyed <br> Phidippus and his brother Antiphus, <br> Sons of the monarch Thessalus, who sprang <br> From Hercules. With thirty ships they came.</p>
<p class="verse">But those who held Pelasgian Argos, those <br> Who dwelt in Alos, Trachys, Alope, <br> Phthia, and Hellas full of lovely dames⁠— <br> Named Myrmidons, Achaians, Hellenes, <br> Achilles led their fifty ships; but they <br> Now heeded not the summons to the war, <br> For there was none to form their ranks for fight. <br> The great Achilles, swift of foot, remained <br> Within his ships, indignant for the sake <br> Of the fair-haired Briscis, whom he brought <br> A captive from Lyrnessus after toils <br> And dangers many. He had sacked and spoiled <br> Lyrnessus, and o’erthrown the walls of Thebes <br> And smitten Mynes and Epistrophus, <br> The warlike sons of King Evenus, sprung <br> From old Selapius. For this cause he kept <br> Within his ships, full soon to issue forth.</p>
<p class="verse">The men of Phylacè, of Pyrasus⁠— <br> Sacred to Ceres and o’erspread with flowers, <br> And of Itona, mother of white flocks, <br> Antrona on the sea, and Pteleum green <br> With herbage⁠—over these while yet he lived <br> The brave Protesilaüs ruled; but now <br> The dark earth covered him, and for his sake <br> His consort, desolate in Phylacè, <br> Tore her fair cheeks, and all unfinished stood <br> His palace, for a Dardan warrior slew <br> Her husband as he leaped upon the land, <br> The foremost of the Achaians. Yet his troops <br> Were not without a leader, though they mourned <br> Their brave old chief. Podarces, loved by Mars⁠— <br> Son of Iphiclus, rich in flocks, who sprang <br> From Phylacus⁠—led them and formed their ranks. <br> A younger brother of the slain was he. <br> The slain was braver. Though the warriors grieved <br> To lose their glorious chief, they did not lack <br> A general. Forty dark ships followed him.</p>
<p class="verse">Then they who dwelt in Pherae, by the lake <br> Boebeis, and in Boebe, Glaphyrae, <br> And nobly built Iolchos, came to Troy, <br> Filling eleven galleys, and obeyed <br> Eumelus, whom Alcestis the divine <br> Bore to Admetus⁠—fairest, she, of all <br> The house of Pelias and of womankind.</p>
<p class="verse">Those from Methone and Olizon’s rocks, <br> And Meliboea and Thaumacia, filled <br> Seven ships, with Philoctetes for their chief, <br> A warrior skilled to bend the bow. Each barque <br> Held fifty rowers, bowmen all, and armed <br> For stubborn battle. But their leader lay <br> Far in an island, suffering grievous pangs⁠— <br> The hallowed isle of Lemnos. There the Greeks <br> Left him, in torture from a venomed wound <br> Made by a serpent’s fangs. He lay and pined. <br> Yet was the moment near when they who thus <br> Forsook their king should think of him again. <br> Meantime his troops were not without a chief; <br> Though greatly they desired their ancient lord, <br> For now the base-born Medon marshalled them, <br> Son of Oileus. Rhene brought him forth <br> To that destroyer of strong fortresses.</p>
<p class="verse">The men of Tricca and Ithome’s hills, <br> And they who held Oechalia and the town <br> Of Eurytus the Aechalian, had for chiefs two <br> sons of Aesculapius, healers both, <br> And skilful⁠—Podalirius one, and one <br> Machaon. Thirty hollow barques were theirs.</p>
<p class="verse">The dwellers of Ormenium, they whose homes <br> Were by the Hyperian fount, and they <br> Who held Asterium and the snowy peaks <br> Of Titanus, obeyed Eurypylus, <br> Evaemon’s son, and far renowned. A fleet <br> Of forty dark-ribbed vessels followed him.</p>
<p class="verse">Those who possessed Argissa, those who held <br> Gyrtonè, Orthè, and Helonè, those <br> Who dwelt in Oloösson with white walls, <br> The sturdy warrior Polypoetes led, <br> Son of Pirithoüs, who derived his birth <br> From deathless Jove. Hippodameia bore <br> The warrior to Pirithoüs on the day <br> When he took vengeance on the shaggy brood <br> Of Centaurs, and from Pelion drove them forth <br> To Aethicae. Yet not alone in rule <br> Was Polypoetes, for Leonteus, sprung <br> From the large-souled Coronus, Caeneus’ son, <br> Shared with him the command. With them a fleet <br> Of forty dark-hulled vessels came to Troy.</p>
<p class="verse">Then Guneus came, with two and twenty ships <br> From Cythus. Under his command he held <br> The Enienes, and that sturdy race, <br> The Periboean warriors, and the men <br> Who built on cold Dodona, or who tilled <br> The fields where pleasant Titaresius flows <br> And into Peneus pours his gentle stream, <br> Yet with its silver eddies mingles not, <br> But floats upon the current’s face like oil⁠— <br> A Stygian stream by which the immortals swear.</p>
<p class="verse">With Prothous, Tenthredon’s son, there came <br> The warriors of Magnesia, who abode <br> By Peneus, and by Pelion hung with woods; <br> Swift-footed Prothous led these. They came <br> With forty dark-hulled galleys to the war.</p>
<p class="verse">These were the chiefs and princes of the Greeks. <br> Say, Muse, who most excelled among the kings, <br> And which the noblest steeds, of all that came <br> With the two sons of Atreus to the war? <br> The noblest steeds were those in Pherae bred, <br> That, guided by Eumelus, flew like birds⁠— <br> Alike in hue and age; the plummet showed <br> Their height the same, and both were mares, and, reared <br> By Phoebus of the silver bow among <br> The meadows of Pieria, they became <br> The terror of the bloody battle-field. <br> The mightiest of the chiefs, while yet in wrath <br> Achilles kept aloof, was Ajax, son <br> Of Telamon; yet was Pelides far <br> The greater warrior, and the steeds which bore <br> That perfect hero were of noblest breed. <br> In his beaked galleys, swift to cut the sea, <br> Achilles lay, meanwhile, and nursed the wrath <br> He bore to Agamemnon, Atreus’ son, <br> The shepherd of the people. On the beach <br> His warriors took their sport with javelins <br> And quoits and bows, while near the chariots tied <br> The horses, standing, browsed on lotus-leaves <br> And parsley from the marshes. But beneath <br> The tents the closely covered chariots stood, <br> While idly through the camp the charioteers, <br> Hither and thither sauntering, missed the sight <br> Of their brave lord and went not to the field.</p>
<p class="verse">The army swept the earth as when a fire <br> Devours the herbage of the plains. The ground <br> Groaned under them as when the Thunderer Jove <br> In anger with his lightnings smites the earth <br> About Typhosus⁠—where they say he lies⁠— <br> In Arimi. So fearfully the ground <br> Groaned under that swift army as it moved.</p>
<p class="verse">Now to the Trojans the swift Iris came <br> A messenger from aegis-bearing Jove, <br> Tidings of bale she brought. They all had met⁠— <br> Old men and youths⁠—in council at the gates <br> Of Priam’s mansion. There did Iris take <br> Her station near the multitude, and spake, <br> In voice and gesture like Polites, son <br> Of Priam, who, confiding in his speed, <br> Had stood a watcher for the sons of Troy <br> On aged Aesyeta’s lofty tomb, <br> To give them warning when the Achaian host <br> Should issue from their galleys. Thus disguised, <br> Swift Iris spake her message from the skies:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Father! Thou art delighted with much speech, <br> As once in time of peace, but now’t is war, <br> Inevitable war, and close at hand. <br> I have seen many battles, yet have ne’er <br> Beheld such armies, and so vast as these⁠— <br> In number like the sands and summer leaves. <br> They march across the plain, prepared to give <br> Battle beneath the city walls. To thee, <br> O Hector, it belongs to heed my voice <br> And counsel. Many are the allies within <br> The walls of this great town of Priam, men <br> Of diverse race and speech. Let every chief <br> Of these array his countrymen for war, <br> And give them orders for the coming fight.”</p>
<p class="verse">She spake, and Hector heeded and obeyed <br> The counsel of the goddess; he dismissed <br> The assembly; all the Trojans rushed to arms, <br> And all the gates were opened. Horse and foot <br> Poured forth together in tumultuous haste.</p>
<p class="verse">In the great plain before the city stands <br> A mound of steep ascent on every side; <br> Men named it Batiea, but the gods <br> Called it the swift Myrinna’s tomb; and here <br> Mustered the sons of Troy and their allies.</p>
<p class="verse">Great Hector of the beamy helm, the son <br> Of Priam, led the Trojan race. The host <br> Of greatest multitude was marshalled there, <br> And there the bravest, mighty with the spear.</p>
<p class="verse">Aeneas marshalled the Dardanian troops⁠— <br> The brave son of Anchises. Venus bore <br> The warrior to Anchises on the heights <br> Of Ida, where the mortal lover met <br> The goddess. Yet he ruled them not alone; <br> Two chiefs, Antenor’s sons Archelochus <br> And Acamas, were with him in command, <br> Expert in all the many arts of war.</p>
<p class="verse">The Trojans from Zeleia, opulent men, <br> Who drank the dark Aesepus⁠—over these <br> Ruled Pandarus, Lycaon’s valiant son, <br> To whom the god Apollo gave his bow.</p>
<p class="verse">The troops from Adrasteia, they who dwelt <br> Within Apaesus’ walls, or tilled the soil <br> Of Pityeia and Tereia’s heights, <br> Were led by Amphius and Adrastus, clad <br> In linen corselets for the war, the sons <br> Of Merops the Percosian, skilled beyond <br> All other men in the diviner’s art. <br> Nor would he that his sons should seek the field <br> Of slaughter. They obeyed him not; the fates <br> Decreed their early death and urged them on.</p>
<p class="verse">The dwellers of Percote, Practium, <br> And Sestus, and Abydus, and divine <br> Arisba, followed Asius, great among <br> The heroes and the son of Hyrtacus⁠— <br> Asius, who came with strong and fiery steeds, <br> Borne from Arisba and from Selleis’ banks.</p>
<p class="verse">Hippothoüs over the Pelasgian tribes⁠— <br> Skilled spearman, who abode among the fields <br> Of the deep-soiled Larissa⁠—bore command⁠— <br> Hippothoüs with Pyteus, who derived <br> Their race from Mars, and for their father claimed <br> Pelasgian Lethus, son of Teutamus.</p>
<p class="verse">And Acamas, and Peiroüs, valiant chief, <br> Were captains of the Thracian men, whose fields <br> Were bounded by the rushing Hellespont. <br> Euphemus led the Cicones, expert <br> To wield the spear in fight. The nobly-born <br> Troezenus was his father. Ceas’ son <br> Pyraechmes with Paeonia’s archers came <br> From the broad Axius in far Amydon⁠— <br> Axius, the fairest river of the earth.</p>
<p class="verse">Pytamenes, a chief of fearless heart, <br> Led from the region of the Eneti, <br> Where first the stubborn race of mules was bred, <br> The Paphlagonian warriors, they who held <br> Cytorus, Sesamus, and fair abodes <br> Built where Parthenius wanders on, and those <br> Who dwelt in Cromna and Aegialus, <br> And on the lofty Erythinian heights.</p>
<p class="verse">And Hodius and Epistrophus led on <br> The Halezonians from the distant land <br> Of Alyba, where ores of silver lie. <br> And Chromis and the augur Ennomus <br> Were leaders of the Mysians; but his skill <br> Saved not the augur from the doom of death, <br> Slain by the swift of foot, Aeacides, <br> With other men of Troy where Xanthus flows. <br> And Phorcys and Ascanius, who was like <br> A god in beauty, led the Phrygian troops <br> From far Ascania, eager for the fray. <br> And Antiphus and Mesthles were the chiefs <br> Of the Maeonian warriors, reared beside <br> The ships of Tmolus. There Gygaea’s lake <br> Brought forth both chieftains to Pylsemenes.</p>
<p class="verse">Nastes was leader of the Carian troops, <br> Who spake in barbarous accents and possessed <br> Miletus and the leafy mountain heights <br> Where dwell the Phthirians, and Maeauder’s stream, <br> And airy peaks of Mycalè. O’er these <br> Amphimachus and Nastes held command⁠— <br> Amphimachus and Nastes, far renowned <br> Sons of Nomion, him who, madly vain, <br> Went to the battle pranked like a young girl <br> In golden ornaments. They spared him not <br> The bitter doom of death; he fell beneath <br> The hand of swift Aeacides within <br> The river’s channel. There the great in war, <br> Achilles, spoiled Nomion of his gold.</p>
<p class="verse">Sarpedon and the noble Glaucus bore <br> Rule o’er the Eycians coming from afar, <br> Where eddying Xanthus runs through Lycia’s meads.</p>
