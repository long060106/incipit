<p class="lede">Thetis brings to Achilles the armor forged by Vulcan⁠—The body of Patroclus preserved by the gods from corruption⁠—An assembly of all the army, before whom Agamemnon and Achilles make speeches, and renounce their enmity⁠—Briseis restored to Achilles, and the presents of Agamemnon accepted by him⁠—Lament of Briseis over Patroclus⁠—Sorrow of Achilles⁠—He arms himself for the war⁠—His speech to the horses of Patroclus whom he upbraids for having suffered their master to be slain⁠—The answer of one of them named Xanthus, warning Achilles of his approaching death.</p>
<p class="verse">In saffron-colored mantle from the tides <br> Of Ocean rose the Morning to bring light <br> To gods and men, when Thetis reached the fleet, <br> Bringing the gift of Vulcan. There she found <br> Her son, who, bending o’er Patroclus, wept <br> Aloud, and all around a troop of friends <br> Lamented bitterly. Beside him stood <br> The glorious goddess, took his hand, and said:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Leave we the dead, my son, since it hath pleased <br> The gods that he should fall; and now receive <br> This sumptuous armor, forged by Vulcan’s hand, <br> Beautiful, such as no man ever wore.”</p>
<p class="verse">The goddess spake, and laid the armor down <br> Before Achilles; as they touched the earth, <br> The well-wrought pieces clanked, and terror seized <br> The Myrmidons. No one among them all <br> Dared fix his gaze upon them; all shrank back. <br> Achilles only, as he saw them, felt <br> His spirit roused within him. In his eyes <br> A terrible brightness flashed, as if of fire. <br> He lifted up the god’s magnificent gift <br> Rejoicing, and, when long his eyes had dwelt <br> Delighted on the marvellous workmanship, <br> Thus to his mother said, in wingèd words:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“A god indeed, my mother, must have given <br> These arms, the work of heavenly hands: no man <br> Could forge them. Now I arm myself for war. <br> But for the valiant Menoetiades <br> I greatly fear that flies will gather round <br> The wounds inflicted by the spear, and worms <br> Be bred within them, to pollute the corpse <br> Now that the life is gone, and taint the whole.”</p>
<p class="verse">And silver-footed Thetis answered thus: <br> “Son, have no care for that. The task be mine <br> To drive away the importunate swarm that feed <br> On heroes slain in battle. Though it lie <br> The whole year long, the body shall remain <br> Even more than uncorrupted. Call thou now <br> To council all the Achaian chiefs; renounce <br> Thy feud with Agamemnon, king of men, <br> And arm for war, and put on all thy might.”</p>
<p class="verse">She spake, and called a fiery courage up <br> Within the hero’s breast. The goddess then <br> Infused ambrosia and the ruddy juice <br> Of nectar through the nostrils of the dead <br> Into the frame, to keep it from decay.</p>
<p class="verse">Along the beach the great Achilles went, <br> Calling with mighty shouts the Grecian chiefs. <br> Then even they who till that day remained <br> Beside the fleet⁠—the pilots and the men <br> Who held the helm, the stewards of the ships, <br> And the purveyors⁠—all made haste to swell <br> The assembly, for they knew that he who long <br> Had borne no part in the disastrous war <br> Had now come forth. Two ministers of Mars, <br> The brave Tydides and the nobly born <br> Ulysses, both supported by their spears, <br> Came halting, for their wounds were painful yet; <br> They came and sat among the foremost chiefs. <br> And last came Agamemnon, king of men, <br> Wounded, for he had felt in thick of fight <br> The edge of the sharp spear which Coön bore, <br> Antenor’s son. Now when the Greeks were all <br> Assembled, swift Achilles rose and said:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Atrides, of a truth it would have been <br> Better for both of us had we done this <br> At first, though sorely angered, when we strove <br> For a girl’s sake so fiercely. Would that she <br> Had perished in my ships, by Dian’s shaft, <br> The day on which I laid Lyrnessus waste! <br> So many Greeks would then have not been forced, <br> Slain by the enemy’s hand, to bite the dust <br> Of the great earth, while I was brooding o’er <br> My wrath. All that was for the good of Troy <br> And Hector; but the Greeks, I think, will long <br> Remember our contention. Let us leave <br> These things among the things that were, and, though <br> They make us grieve, let us subdue our minds <br> To what the time requires. Here then my wrath <br> Shall end; it is not meet that it should burn <br> Forever. Hasten thou and rouse to war <br> The long-haired Greeks, that I may yet again <br> Go forth among the men of Troy, and learn <br> If they design to encamp another night <br> Before the fleet. There is among them all <br> No man, I ween, who will not joyfully <br> Sit down when he escapes my deadly spear.”</p>
<p class="verse">He ended, and the Achaians all rejoiced <br> To hear the brave Pelides thus renounce <br> His anger. Agamemnon, king of men, <br> Then rose. He came not forth into the midst, <br> But stood beside his seat, and thus he spake:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“O friends, Achaian heroes, ministers <br> Of Mars! Whoever rises up to speak <br> ’Tis well to hear him through, and not break in <br> Upon his speech, else is the most expert <br> Confounded. Who amid a clamorous throng <br> Can listen or can speak? The orator <br> Of clearest voice must utter it in vain. <br> Now I address Pelides; for the rest, <br> Hearken ye all, and ponder what I say. <br> The Greeks speak often of this feud, and cast <br> The blame on me. Yet was I not the cause, <br> But Jupiter and Fate, and she who walks <br> In darkness, dread Erynnis. It was they <br> Who filled my mind with fury in the hour <br> When from Achilles I bore off his prize. <br> What could I do? A deity prevails <br> In all things, Atè, mighty to destroy, <br> Daughter of Jove, and held in awe by all. <br> Delicate are her feet; she never comes <br> Near to the ground, but glides above the heads <br> Of men, to do them harm, and in her net <br> Entangles one at least of two who strive. <br> Jove, deemed the mightiest among men and gods, <br> Once felt her power of mischief. Him his spouse, <br> Juno, entrapped by cunning, when within <br> The massive walls of Thebes Alcmena lay <br> In childbed, and the mighty Hercules <br> Was near his birth. For Jupiter had said <br> Boastfully to the immortals: ‘Hear, ye gods <br> And goddesses, what I am moved to speak: <br> This day shall Ilithyia, who presides <br> At births, bring into light a prince whose rule <br> The neighboring tribes shall own; he shall be one <br> Who bears the blood of my illustrious race.’</p>
<p class="verse">“Imperial Juno thus, with words of guile, <br> Made answer: ‘What thou sayest will prove false, <br> Nor wilt thou keep thy word. Now swear to me, <br> Olympius, with the irrevocable oath, <br> That whosoever of thy race shall fall <br> This day between a woman’s feet shall bear <br> The rule o’er all the neighboring tribes.’ She spake, <br> And Jove, perceiving not her craft, complied, <br> And took the mighty oath, but afterward <br> Found himself wronged. For Juno, darting forth, <br> Shot from the Olympian summit, and at once <br> Alighted at Achaian Argos. There <br> She found the noble wife of Sthenelus, <br> The son of Perseus, pregnant with a son, <br> In the seventh month. She caused him to be born, <br> The number of his months yet incomplete, <br> And kept Alcmena’s hour of childbirth back, <br> And stayed her pangs. The goddess then made haste <br> To bear the tidings to Saturnian Jove.</p>
<p class="verse">“ ‘O Father Jupiter, by whom are hurled <br> The ruddy lightnings, I have news for thee. <br> A man-child of a generous stock is born⁠— <br> Eurystheus, whom the Argives shall obey⁠— <br> Born at this hour to Sthenelus, the son <br> Of Perseus, who is thine. And well it is <br> That such a prince should rule the Argive race.’</p>
<p class="verse">“She ended: Jupiter was deeply grieved, <br> And, seizing Atè by her shining locks, <br> In his great wrath, he swore a mighty oath⁠— <br> That Atè, whose delight it is to bring <br> Mischief to all, should never tread again <br> Olympus and the starry floor of heaven. <br> Thus having sworn, he swung her, with raised arm, <br> On high, and hurled her from the starry heaven <br> Downward, where soon she reached the haunts of men; <br> Yet oft in after time because of her <br> He sighed, beholding his beloved son <br> Doomed by Eurystheus to unworthy tasks. <br> So I, while crested Hector in his might <br> Made havoc at our fleet among the Greeks <br> Even by their prows, remembered well my fault. <br> And now since I have borne the penalty, <br> And Jupiter it was who took away <br> My reason, I would gladly make amends <br> With liberal gifts. But rise and join the war; <br> Inflame the courage of the rest; the gifts <br> Will I supply⁠—all that were promised thee <br> When nobly born Ulysses yesterday <br> Went to thy tents. Or, if it please thee, wait, <br> Though armed for battle, and my train shall bring <br> The treasures from my ship, that thou mayst see <br> My presents are peace-offerings indeed.”</p>
<p class="verse">The swift of foot, Achilles, answered thus: <br> “Most glorious son of Atreus, king of men! <br> Whether, O Agamemnon, thou wilt give <br> Gifts, as is meet, or keep them, rests with thee. <br> Now let us think of war; it is not well <br> To waste the hour in talking, and put off <br> The mighty work that we have yet to do. <br> Let every Greek among you, as he sees <br> Achilles fighting in the foremost ranks, <br> And slaughtering the Trojan phalanxes, <br> Take heart and boldly combat with his man.”</p>
<p class="verse">And then Ulysses, wise in council, spake, <br> Answering Achilles: “Nay, thou shouldst not thus, <br> Brave as thou art, lead on the sons of Greece, <br> Yet fasting, to the conflict with the men <br> Of Troy beside their city. No brief space <br> The struggle will endure when once the foes <br> Rush on each other, and a god inspires <br> Both hosts with fury. Bid the Achaians take <br> In their swift galleys food and wine; in these <br> Are force and vigor. No man can endure <br> To combat all the day till set of sun, <br> Save with the aid of food, however great <br> The promptings of his valor; for his limbs <br> Grow heavy, thirst and hunger weaken him, <br> And his knees fail him as he walks. Not so <br> The warrior well supplied with food and wine: <br> He fights the foe all day; a resolute heart <br> Is in his bosom; nor does weariness <br> O’ertake him till all others leave the field. <br> Now let the people be dismissed awhile, <br> And a repast be ordered. Let the king, <br> Atrides, bring to the assembly here <br> His gifts, that all the Greeks may look on them, <br> And thou rejoice to see them. Let him rise <br> Among the Greeks, and take a solemn oath <br> That he has ne’er approached the maiden’s bed <br> To claim a husband’s right. Thus let thy heart <br> Be satisfied. Yet let the monarch spread <br> A sumptuous banquet in his tent for thee, <br> That thy redress may be complete. And thou, <br> Atrides, wilt hereafter be more just <br> To others. It dishonors not a king <br> To make amends to one whom he has wronged.”</p>
<p class="verse">And then King Agamemnon spake in turn: <br> “Son of Laertes, gladly have I heard <br> What thou hast said, and well hast thou discoursed <br> Of all things in their order. I will take <br> The oath of which thou speakest⁠—so my heart <br> Commands me. In the presence of a god <br> I take it, and commit no perjury. <br> Now let Achilles, though he longs for war, <br> Delay awhile; and all assembled here, <br> Remain ye on the ground till from my ship <br> The gifts are brought. This charge and this command <br> I give to thee, Ulysses. Take with thee <br> A band of youths, the noblest of the host, <br> And bring the presents promised yesterday <br> To Peleus’ son, and hither let them lead <br> The women. Meantime let Talthybius haste <br> To bring from our broad camp a boar, which I <br> Will offer up to Jove and to the Sun.”</p>
<p class="verse">The swift of foot, Achilles, thus replied: <br> “Most glorious son of Atreus, king of men, <br> These things are for the time when there shall come <br> A pause from battle, and this warlike heat <br> Within my breast shall cool. They whom the spear <br> Of Hector, son of Priam, has o’ercome <br> Lie mangled on the earth, since Jupiter <br> Awarded him the glory of the day:⁠— <br> And ye propose a banquet. I would call <br> The sons of Greece to rush into the war <br> Unfed and fasting, and when this disgrace <br> Shall be avenged, I would, at sunset, spread <br> A liberal feast. Be sure that I, till then, <br> Taste neither food nor drink, while my slain friend <br> Lies gashed with weapons in my tent, amidst <br> His sorrowing comrades. Little I regard <br> The things of which thou speakest, for my thoughts <br> Are all of bloodshed and of dying groans.”</p>
<p class="verse">Ulysses, the sagacious, thus rejoined: <br> “Achilles, son of Peleus, bravest far <br> Of all the Achaians, mightier with the spear <br> By no small odds than I, yet do I stand <br> In prudence much above thee; I have lived <br> More years, and more have learned. Let then thy mind <br> Accept what I shall say. Men soon become <br> Weary of warfare, even when the sword <br> Lays its most ample harvest on the earth. <br> But fewer sheaves are reaped when Jupiter, <br> The arbiter of battles, turns the scale. <br> It is not well that we of Greece should mourn <br> The dead with fasting, since from day to day <br> Our warriors fall in numbers. Where were then <br> Respite from daily fasts? Lay we our slain <br> In earth and mourn a day. We who outlive <br> The cruel combat should refresh ourselves <br> With food and wine, that we may steadily <br> Maintain in arms the conflict with the foe. <br> And then let no man idly wait to hear <br> A further call to war⁠—for it will come <br> Freighted with evil to the man who skulks <br> Among the ships⁠—but let us all go forth <br> To wage fierce battle with the knights of Troy.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake, and summoned to his side the sons <br> Of glorious Nestor, and Meriones, <br> And Meges, son of Phyleus, and with them <br> Thoas, and Lycomedes, Creon’s son, <br> And Melanippus. Straight they took their way <br> To Agamemnon’s tent, and there their task <br> Was done as quickly as the word was given. <br> They brought seven tripods forth, the promised gifts, <br> And twenty burnished cauldrons, and twelve steeds, <br> And led away seven graceful women trained <br> In household arts⁠—the maid with rosy cheeks, <br> Briseis, was the eighth. Ulysses came, <br> Leading the way, and bearing, duly weighed, <br> Ten talents, all of gold. The Achaian youths <br> Followed, and placed the presents in the midst <br> Of that assembly. Agamemnon rose; <br> And then Talthybius, who was like a god <br> In power of voice, came near and took his place <br> Beside the monarch, holding in his hands <br> A boar. The son of Atreus drew a knife, <br> Which hung by the great scabbard of his sword, <br> And, cutting off the forelock of the boar, <br> Prayed with uplifted hands to Jupiter: <br> Meantime the Greeks in silence kept their seats, <br> And, as became them, listened to the king, <br> Who looked into the sky above, and said:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Now first bear witness, Jove, of all the gods <br> Greatest and best, and also Earth and Sun, <br> And Furies dwelling under Earth, who take <br> Vengeance on men forsworn, that never I <br> Have laid, for purpose of unchaste desire, <br> Or other cause, my hand upon the maid <br> Briseis. She hath dwelt inviolate <br> Within my tents. If yet in aught I say <br> Lurk perjury, then may the blessed gods <br> Heap on my head the many miseries <br> With which they punish those who falsely swear!”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake, and drew the unrelenting blade <br> Across the animal’s throat. Talthybius took <br> And swung the carcass round, and cast it forth <br> Into the gray sea’s depths, to be the food <br> Of fishes. Then again Achilles rose <br> Among the warlike sons of Greece, and said:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Great sorrows thou dost send, O Father Jove! <br> Upon mankind; for never would the son <br> Of Atreus have provoked the wrath that burned <br> Within my bosom, never would have thought <br> To bear away the maiden from my tent <br> In spite of me, had it not been the will <br> Of Jupiter that many a Greek should die. <br> But banquet now, and then prepare for war.”</p>
<p class="verse">So spake Achilles, and at once dissolved <br> The assembly, each repairing to his ship <br> Save the large-hearted Myrmidons, who still <br> Were busy with the gifts, and carried them <br> Toward their great general’s galley. These they laid <br> Carefully in the tents, and seated there <br> The women, while the attentive followers drave <br> The coursers to the stables. When the maid <br> Briseis, beautiful as Venus, saw <br> Patroclus lying gashed with wounds, she sprang <br> And threw herself upon the dead, and tore <br> Her bosom, her fair cheeks and delicate neck; <br> And thus the graceful maiden, weeping, said:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Patroclus, dear to my unhappy heart! <br> I left thee in full life, when from this tent <br> They led me; I return and find thee dead, <br> O chieftain of the people! Thus it is <br> That sorrow upon sorrow is my lot. <br> Him to whose arms my father, in my youth, <br> And gracious mother gave me as a bride, <br> I saw before our city pierced and slain, <br> And the three brothers whom my mother bore <br> Slain also⁠—brothers whom I dearly loved. <br> Yet thou, when swift Achilles struck to earth <br> My hapless husband, and laid waste the town <br> Of godlike Mynes, wouldst not suffer me <br> To weep despairingly; for thou didst give <br> Thy word to make me yet the wedded wife <br> Of great Achilles, bear me in the fleet <br> To Phthia, and prepare the wedding feast <br> Among the Myrmidons. O ever kind! <br> I mourn thy death, and cannot be consoled.”</p>
<p class="verse">Weeping she spake; the women wept with her <br> Seemingly for the dead, but each, in truth, <br> For her own griefs. Meanwhile the elders came <br> Around Achilles, praying him to join <br> The banquet, but the chief, with sighs, refused.</p>
<p class="verse">“Dear comrades, if ye love me, do not thus <br> Press me to sit and feast. A mighty woe <br> Weighs down my spirit; it is my resolve <br> To wait and bear until the setting sun.”</p>
<p class="verse">So saying, he dismissed the other kings. <br> The sons of Atreus, and the high-born chief <br> Ulysses, Nestor, and Idomeneus, <br> And Phoenix, aged knight, alone remained, <br> And anxiously they sought to comfort him <br> In his great grief; but comfort would he none <br> Ere entering the red jaws of war. He drew <br> Deep sighs, and, thinking on Patroclus, spake:</p>
<p class="verse">“The time has been when thou too, hapless one, <br> Dearest of all my comrades, wouldst have spread <br> With diligent speed before me in my tent <br> A genial banquet, while the Greeks prepared <br> For desperate battle with the knights of Troy. <br> Thou liest now a mangled corse, and I, <br> Through grief for thee, refrain from food and drink, <br> Though they are near. No worse calamity <br> Could light on me, not even should I hear <br> News of my father’s death, who haply now <br> Tenderly mourns with tears his absent son <br> In Phthia, while upon a foreign coast <br> I wage for hated Helen’s sake the war <br> Against the Trojans; or were I to hear <br> Tidings that my beloved son had died, <br> The noble Neoptolemus, who now, <br> If living, is in Scyros, growing up <br> To manhood. Once the hope was in my heart <br> That I alone should perish here at Troy, <br> Far from the Argive pastures full of steeds, <br> And thou return to Phthia and bring home <br> My son from Scyros in thy ship, and show <br> The youth my wealth, my servants, and my halls, <br> High-roofed and spacious. For my mind misgives <br> That Peleus either lives not, or endures <br> A painful age, and hardly lives, yet waits <br> To hear the sorrowful news that I am slain.”</p>
<p class="verse">So spake he weeping, and the elders sighed <br> To see his tears, as each recalled to mind <br> Those whom he left at home, while Saturn’s son <br> Beheld their grief with pity, and bespake <br> His daughter Pallas thus with wingèd words:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“My child, wilt thou desert that valiant man? <br> And shall Achilles be no more thy care? <br> Lo, by his ships, before their lofty prows, <br> He sits, lamenting his beloved friend. <br> The rest are at the banquet; he remains <br> Apart from them, and fasting. Hasten thou; <br> With nectar and ambrosial sweets refresh <br> His frame, that hunger overtake him not.”</p>
<p class="verse">As thus he spake he sent the goddess forth <br> Eager to do her errand. Plunging down, <br> In form a shrill-voiced harpy with broad wings, <br> She cleft the air. The Greeks throughout the camp <br> Were putting on their armor. She infused <br> Into the hero’s frame ambrosial sweets <br> And nectar, that his limbs might not grow faint <br> With hunger. Then the goddess sought again <br> The stable mansion of Almighty Jove, <br> While all the Greeks came pouring from the fleet.</p>
<p class="verse">As when the flakes of snow fall thick from heaven, <br> Driven by the north wind sweeping on the clouds <br> Before it, so from out the galleys came <br> Helms crowding upon helms that glittered fair, <br> Strong hauberks, bossy shields, and ashen spears. <br> The gleam of armor brightened heaven and earth, <br> And mighty was the sound of trampling feet. <br> Amidst them all the great Achilles stood, <br> Putting his armor on; he gnashed his teeth; <br> His eyes shot fire; a grief too sharp to bear <br> Was in his heart, as, filled with rage against <br> The men of Troy, he cased his limbs in mail, <br> The gift of Vulcan, from whose diligent hand <br> It came. And first about his legs he clasped <br> The beautiful greaves, with silver fastenings, <br> Fitted the corselet to his bosom next, <br> And from his shoulders hung the brazen sword <br> With silver studs, and then he took the shield, <br> Massive and broad, whose brightness streamed as far <br> As the moon’s rays. And as at sea the light <br> Of beacon, blazing in some lonely spot <br> By night, upon a mountain summit, shines <br> To mariners whom the tempest’s force has driven <br> Far from their friends across the fishy deep, <br> So from that glorious buckler of the son <br> Of Peleus, nobly wrought, a radiance streamed <br> Into the sky. And then he raised and placed <br> Upon his head the impenetrable helm <br> With horse-hair plume. It glittered like a star, <br> And all the shining tufts of golden thread, <br> With which the maker’s hand had thickly set <br> Its cone, were shaken. Next the high-born chief <br> Tried his new arms, to know if they were well <br> Adjusted to his shape, and left his limbs <br> Free play. They seemed like wings, and lifted up <br> The shepherd of the people. Then he drew <br> From its ancestral sheath his father’s spear, <br> Heavy and huge and tough. No man of all <br> The Grecian host could wield that weapon save <br> Achilles only. ’Twas a Pelian ash, <br> Which Chiron for his father had cut down <br> On Pelion’s highest peak, to be the death <br> Of heroes. Meantime, busy with the steeds, <br> Automedon and Alcimus put on <br> Their trappings and their yoke, and round their necks <br> Bound the fair collars, thrust into their mouths <br> The bit, and backward drew the reins to meet <br> The well-wrought chariot. Then Automedon <br> Took in his hand the showy lash, and leaped <br> Into the seat. Behind him, all equipped <br> For war, Achilles mounted, in a blaze <br> Of arms that dazzled like the sun, and thus <br> Called to his father’s steeds with terrible voice:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Xanthus and Balius, whom Podargè bore⁠— <br> A noble stock⁠—I charge you to bring back <br> Into the Grecian camp, the battle done, <br> Him whom ye now are bearing to the field, <br> Nor leave him, as ye left Patroclus, dead.”</p>
<p class="verse">Swift-footed Xanthus from beneath the yoke <br> Answered him with bowed head and drooping mane <br> That, flowing through the yoke-ring swept the ground⁠— <br> For Juno gave him then the power of speech:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“For this one day, at least, we bear thee safe, <br> O fiery chief, Achilles! But the hour <br> Of death draws nigh to thee, nor will the blame <br> Be ours; a mighty god and cruel fate <br> Ordain it. Not through our neglect or sloth <br> Did they of Troy strip off thy glorious arms <br> From slain Patroclus. That invincible god, <br> The son of golden-haired Latona, smote <br> The hero in the foremost ranks, and gave <br> Glory to Hector. Even though our speed <br> Were that of Zephyr, fleetest of the winds, <br> Yet certain is thy doom to be o’ercome <br> In battle by a god and by a man.”</p>
<p class="verse">Thus far he spake, and then the Furies checked <br> His further speech. Achilles, swift of foot, <br> Replied in anger: “Xanthus, why foretell <br> My death? It is not needed; well I know <br> My fate⁠—that here I perish, far away <br> From Peleus and my mother. I shall fight <br> Till I have made the Trojans sick of war.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake, and, shouting to his firm-paced steeds, <br> Drave them, among the foremost, toward the war.</p>
