<p class="lede">A council of the gods⁠—Jupiter forbids them to take part with either side⁠—Minerva permitted to advise the Greeks⁠—Beginning of the second battle⁠—The fate of the two armies weighed in the scales by Jupiter⁠—Nestor rescued by Diomed⁠—Exploits of Diomed and of Hector⁠—Neptune denies the request of Juno to aid the Greeks⁠—Teucer wounded by Hector⁠—Juno and Minerva restrained by Jupiter from going to the aid of the Greeks, who are driven within their entrenchments⁠—The Trojans pass the night before the Greek camp, and kindle fires around it.</p>
<p class="verse">Now morn in saffron robes had shed her light <br> O’er all the earth, when Jove the Thunderer <br> Summoned the gods to council on the heights <br> Of many-peaked Olympus. He addressed <br> The assembly, and all listened as he spake:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Hear, all ye gods and all ye goddesses! <br> While I declare the thought within my breast. <br> Let none of either sex presume to break <br> The law I give, but cheerfully obey, <br> That my design may sooner be fulfilled. <br> Whoever, stealing from the rest, shall seek <br> To aid the Grecian cause, or that of Troy, <br> Back to Olympus, scourged and in disgrace, <br> Shall he be brought, or I will seize and hurl <br> The offender down to rayless Tartarus, <br> Deep, deep in the great gulf below the earth, <br> With iron gates and threshold forged of brass, <br> As far beneath the shades as earth from heaven. <br> Then shall he learn how greatly I surpass <br> All other gods in power. Try if ye will, <br> Ye gods, that all may know: suspend from heaven <br> A golden chain; let all the immortal host <br> Cling to it from below: ye could not draw, <br> Strive as ye might, the all-disposing Jove <br> From heaven to earth. And yet, if I should choose <br> To draw it upward to me, I should lift, <br> With it and you, the earth itself and sea <br> Together, and I then would bind the chain <br> Around the summit of the Olympian mount, <br> And they should hang aloft. So far my power <br> Surpasses all the power of gods and men.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake; and all the great assembly, hushed <br> In silence, wondered at his threatening words, <br> Until at length the blue-eyed Pallas said:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Our Father, son of Saturn, mightiest as <br> Among the potentates, we know thy power <br> Is not to be withstood, yet are we moved <br> With pity for the warlike Greeks, who bear <br> An evil fate and waste away in war. <br> If such be thy command, we shall refrain <br> From mingling in the combat, yet will aid <br> The Greeks with counsel which may be their guide, <br> Lest by thy wrath they perish utterly.”</p>
<p class="verse">The Cloud-compeller Jove replied, and smiled:⁠— <br> “Tritonia, daughter dear, be comforted. <br> I spake not in the anger of my heart, <br> And I have naught but kind intents for thee.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake, and to his chariot yoked the steeds, <br> Fleet, brazen-footed, and with flowing manes <br> Of gold, and put his golden armor on, <br> And took the golden scourge, divinely wrought, <br> And, mounting, touched the coursers with the lash <br> To urge them onward. Not unwillingly <br> Flew they between the earth and starry heaven, <br> Until he came to Ida, moist with springs <br> And nurse of savage beasts, and to the height <br> Of Gargarus, where lay his sacred field, <br> And where his fragrant altar fumed. He checked <br> Their course, and there the Father of the gods <br> And men released them from the yoke and caused <br> A cloud to gather round them. Then he sat, <br> Exulting in the fullness of his might, <br> Upon the summit, whence his eye beheld <br> The towers of Ilium and the ships of Greece.</p>
<p class="verse">Now in their tents the long-haired Greeks had shared <br> A hasty meal, and girded on their arms. <br> The Trojans, also, in their city armed <br> Themselves for war, as eager for the fight, <br> Though fewer; for a hard necessity <br> Forced them to combat for their little ones <br> And wives. They set the city-portals wide, <br> And forth the people issued, foot and horse <br> Together, and a mighty din arose. <br> And now, when host met host, their shields and spears <br> Were mingled in disorder; men of might <br> Encountered, cased in mail, and bucklers clashed <br> Their bosses; loud the clamor: cries of pain <br> And boastful shouts arose from those who fell <br> And those who slew, and earth was drenched with blood.</p>
<p class="verse">While yet ’twas morning, and the holy light <br> Of day grew bright, the men of both the hosts <br> Were smitten and were slain; but when the sun <br> Stood high in middle heaven, the All-Father took <br> His golden scales, and in them laid the fates <br> Which bring the sleep of death⁠—the fate of those <br> Who tamed the Trojan steeds, and those who warred <br> For Greece in brazen armor. By the midst <br> He held the balance, and, behold, the fate <br> Of Greece in that day’s fight sank down until <br> It touched the nourishing earth, while that of Troy <br> Rose and flew upward toward the spacious heaven. <br> With that the Godhead thundered terribly <br> From Ida’s height, and sent his lightnings down <br> Among the Achaian army. They beheld <br> In mute amazement and grew pale with fear.</p>
<p class="verse">Then neither dared Idomeneus remain, <br> Nor Agamemnon, on the ground, nor stayed <br> The chieftains Ajax, ministers of Mars. <br> Gerenian Nestor, guardian of the Greeks, <br> Alone was left behind, and he remained <br> Unwillingly. A steed of those that drew <br> His car was sorely wounded by a shaft <br> Which Alexander, fair-haired Helen’s spouse, <br> Sent from his bow. It pierced the forehead where <br> The mane begins, and where a wound is death. <br> The arrow pierced him to the brain; he reared <br> And whirled in torture with the wound, and scared <br> His fellow-coursers. While the aged man <br> Hastened to sever with his sword the thongs <br> That bound him to the car, the rapid steeds <br> Of Hector bore their valiant master on <br> With the pursuing crowd. The aged chief <br> Had perished then, if gallant Diomed <br> Had not perceived his plight. He lifted up <br> His voice, and, shouting to Ulysses, said:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“High-born Ulysses, man of subtle shifts, <br> Son of Laertes, whither dost thou flee? <br> Why like a coward turn thy back? Beware, <br> Lest there some weapon smite thee. Stay and guard <br> This aged warrior from his furious foe.”</p>
<p class="verse">So spake he; but the much-enduring man, <br> Ulysses, heard not the reproof, and passed <br> Rapidly toward the hollow ships of Greece. <br> Tydides, single-handed, made his way <br> Among the foremost warriors, till he stood <br> Before the horses of the aged son <br> Of Neleus, and in wingèd accents said:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“The younger warriors press thee sore, old chief! <br> Thy strength gives way; the weariness of age <br> Is on thee; thy attendant is not strong; <br> Thy steeds are slow. Mount, then, my car, and see <br> What Trojan horses are; how rapidly <br> They turn to right and left, and chase and flee. <br> I took them from the terror of the field, <br> Aeneas. To our servants leave thine own, <br> While we with these assault the Trojan knights, <br> And teach even Hector that the spear I wield <br> Can make as furious havoc as his own.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake; and Nestor, the Gerenian knight, <br> Complied. The two attendants, valiant men⁠— <br> Sthenelus and the good Eurymedon⁠— <br> Took charge of Nestor’s steeds. The chieftains climbed <br> The car of Diomed, and Nestor took <br> Into his hand the embroidered reins and lashed <br> The horses with the scourge. They quickly came <br> To Hector. As the Trojan hastened on, <br> The son of Tydeus hurled a spear; it missed, <br> But spared not Eniopeus, him who held <br> The reins, the hero’s charioteer, and son <br> Of brave Thebaeus. In the breast between <br> The paps it smote him; from the car he fell, <br> And the swift horses started back; his soul <br> And strength passed from him. Hector bitterly <br> Grieved for his death, yet left him where he fell, <br> And sought another fitting charioteer. <br> Nor had the fiery coursers long to wait <br> A guide, for valiant Archeptolemus, <br> The son of Iphitus, was near at hand. <br> And him he caused to mount the chariot drawn <br> By his fleet steeds, and gave his hand the reins.</p>
<p class="verse">Then great had been the slaughter; fearful deeds <br> Had then been done; the Trojans had been scared <br> Into their town like lambs into the fold⁠— <br> Had not the Father of the immortal gods <br> And mortal men beheld, and from on high <br> Terribly thundered, sending to the earth <br> A bolt of fire. He flung it down before <br> The car of Diomed; and fiercely glared <br> The blazing sulphur; both the frightened steeds <br> Cowered trembling by the chariot. Nestor’s hand <br> Let fall the embroidered reins; his spirit sank <br> With fear, and thus he said to Diomed:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Tydides, turn thy firm-paced steeds, and flee. <br> Dost thou not see that victory from Jove <br> Attends thee not? Today doth Saturn’s son <br> Award the glory to the Trojan chief. <br> Hereafter he will make it ours, if such <br> Be his good pleasure. No man, though he be <br> The mightiest among men, can thwart the will <br> Of Jupiter, with whom abides all power.”</p>
<p class="verse">The great in battle, Diomed, replied:⁠— <br> “Truly, O ancient man, thou speakest well; <br> But this it is that grieves me to the heart⁠— <br> That Hector to the Trojan host will say, <br> ‘I put to flight Tydides, and he sought <br> Shelter among his ships.’ Thus will he boast <br> Hereafter; may earth open then for me!”</p>
<p class="verse">And Nestor, the Gerenian knight, rejoined:⁠— <br> “What, son of warlike Tydeus, hast thou said? <br> Though Hector call thee faint of heart and weak, <br> The Trojans and Dardanians, and the wives <br> Of the stout-hearted Trojans armed with shields, <br> Whose husbands in their youthful prime thy hand <br> Hath laid in dust, will not believe his words.”</p>
<p class="verse">Thus having said, he turned the firm-paced steeds <br> Rearward, and mingled with the flying crowd. <br> And now the Trojans and their leader gave <br> A mighty cry, and poured on them a storm <br> Of deadly darts, and crested Hector raised <br> His thundering voice and shouted after them:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“O son of Tydeus! The swift-riding Greeks <br> Have honored thee beyond all other men, <br> At banquets, with high place and delicate meats <br> And flowing cups. They will despise thee now, <br> For thou art like a woman. Timorous girl! <br> Take thyself hence, and never think that I <br> Shall yield to thee, that thou mayst climb our towers <br> And bear away our women in thy ships; <br> For I shall give thee first the doom of death.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake; and Diomed, in doubtful mood, <br> Questioned his spirit whether he should turn <br> His steeds and fight with Hector. Thrice the thought <br> Arose within his mind, and thrice on high <br> Uttered the all-forecasting Jupiter <br> His thunder from the Idaean mount, a sign <br> Of victory changing to the Trojan side. <br> Then Hector to the Trojans called aloud:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Trojans and Lycians all, and ye who close <br> In deadly fight, the sons of Dardanus! <br> Acquit yourselves like men, my friends; recall <br> Your fiery valor now, for I perceive <br> The son of Saturn doth award to me <br> Victory and vast renown, and to the Greeks <br> Destruction. Fools! who built this slender wall <br> Which we contemn, which cannot stand before <br> The strength I bring; our steeds can overleap <br> The trench they digged. When I shall reach their fleet, <br> Remember the consuming power of fire, <br> That I may give their vessels to the flames, <br> And hew the Achaians down beside their prows, <br> While they are wrapped in the bewildering smoke.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake; and then he cheered his coursers thus:⁠— <br> “Xanthus, Podargus, Lampus nobly bred, <br> And Aethon, now repay the generous care, <br> The pleasant grain which my Andromache, <br> Daughter of great Eëtion, largely gives. <br> She mingles wine that ye may drink at will <br> Ere yet she ministers to me, who boast <br> To be her youthful husband. Let us now <br> Pursue with fiery haste, that we may seize <br> The shield of Nestor, the great fame of which <br> Has reached to heaven⁠—an orb of massive gold <br> Even to the handles. Let us from the limbs <br> Of Diomed, the tamer of fleet steeds, <br> Strip off the glorious mail that Vulcan forged: <br> This done, our hope may be that all the Greeks <br> Will climb their galleys and depart tonight.”</p>
<p class="verse">So boasted he; but queenly Juno’s ire <br> Was kindled, and she shuddered on her throne <br> Till great Olympus trembled. Thus she spake <br> To Neptune, mighty ruler of the deep:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Earth-shaker! Thou who rulest far and wide! <br> Is there no pity for the perishing Greeks <br> Within that breast of thine? They bring to thee <br> At Helicè and Aegae costly gifts <br> And many, wherefore thy desire should be <br> That they may win the victory. If the gods <br> Who favor the Achaians should combine <br> To drive the Trojans back, and hold in check <br> High-thundering Jupiter, the God would sit <br> In sullen grief on Ida’s top alone.”</p>
<p class="verse">Earth-shaking Neptune answered in disdain:⁠— <br> “O Juno, rash in speech! What words are these? <br> Think not that I can wish to join the gods <br> In conflict with the monarch Jupiter, <br> The son of Saturn, mightier than we all.”</p>
<p class="verse">So held they colloquy. Meanwhile the space <br> Betwixt the galleys and the trench and wall <br> Was crowded close with steeds and shielded men; <br> For Hector, son of Priam, terrible <br> As Mars the lightning-footed, drave them on <br> Before him. Jove decreed him such renown. <br> And now would he have given that noble fleet <br> To the consuming flame, if Juno, queen <br> Of heaven, had not beheld, and moved the heart <br> Of Agamemnon to exhort the Greeks <br> That they should turn and combat. With quick steps <br> He passed beside the fleet, among the tents, <br> Bearing in his strong hand his purple robe, <br> And climbed the huge black galley which had brought <br> Ulysses to the war⁠—for in the midst <br> It lay, and thence the king might send his voice <br> To either side, as far as to the tents <br> Of Ajax and Achilles, who had moored <br> Their galleys at the different extremes <br> Of the long camp, confiding in their might <br> Of arm and their own valor. Thence he called, <br> With loud, clear utterance, to the Achaian host:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“O Greeks! Shame on ye! Cravens who excel <br> In form alone! Where now are all the boasts <br> Of your invincible valor⁠—the vain words <br> Ye uttered pompously when at the feast <br> In Lemnos sitting ye devoured the flesh <br> Of hornèd beeves, and drank from bowls of wine, <br> Flower-crowned, and bragged that each of you would be <br> A match for fivescore Trojans, or for twice <br> Fivescore? And now we all are not a match <br> For Hector singly, who will give our fleet <br> Soon to consuming flames. O Father Jove, <br> Was ever mighty monarch visited <br> By thee with such affliction, or so robbed <br> Of high renown! And yet in my good ship, <br> Bound to this luckless coast, I never passed <br> By thy fair altars that I did not burn <br> The fat and thighs of oxen, with a prayer <br> That I might sack the well-defended Troy. <br> Now be at least one wish of mine fulfilled⁠— <br> That we may yet escape and get us hence; <br> Nor let the Trojans thus destroy the Greeks.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake, and wept. The All-Father, pitying him, <br> Consented that his people should escape <br> The threatened ruin. Instantly he sent <br> His eagle, bird of surest augury, <br> Which, bearing in his talons a young fawn, <br> The offspring of a nimble-footed roe, <br> Dropped it at the fair altar where the Greeks <br> Paid sacrifice to Panomphaean Jove.</p>
<p class="verse">And they, when they beheld, and knew that Jove <br> Had sent the bird, took courage, rallying, <br> And rushed against the Trojans. Then no chief <br> Of all the Greeks⁠—though many they⁠—could boast <br> That he before Tydides urged his steeds <br> To sudden speed and drave them o’er the trench, <br> And mingled in the combat. First of all <br> He struck down Agelaus, Phradmon’s son, <br> Armed as he was, who turned his car to fly, <br> And as he turned, Tydides with his spear <br> Transfixed his back between the shoulder-blades, <br> And drave the weapon through his breast. He fell <br> To earth, his armor clashing with his fall. <br> Then Agamemnon followed, and with him <br> His brother Menelaus; after these <br> The chieftains Ajax, fearful in their strength; <br> Idomeneus, and he who bore his arms⁠— <br> Meriones, like Mars in battle-field; as <br> Eurypylus, Evaemon’s glorious son; <br> And ninthly Teucer came, who bent his bow <br> Beneath the shield of Ajax Telamon⁠— <br> For Ajax moved his shield from side to side, <br> And thence the archer looked abroad, and aimed <br> His arrows thence. Whoever in the throng <br> Was struck fell lifeless. Teucer all the while, <br> As hides a child behind his mother’s robe, <br> Sheltered himself by Ajax, whose great shield <br> Concealed the chief from sight. What Trojan first <br> Did faithful Teucer slay? Orsilochus, <br> Daetor, and Ophelestes, Ormenus, <br> Chromius, and Lycophontes nobly born, <br> And Hamopaon, Polyremon’s son, <br> And Melanippus⁠—one by one the shafts <br> Of Teucer stretched them on their mother earth. <br> Then Agamemnon, king of men, rejoiced <br> As he beheld him, with his sturdy bow, <br> Breaking the serried phalanxes of Troy; <br> And came, and, standing near, bespake him thus:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Beloved Teucer! Son of Telamon, <br> Prince of the people! Ever be thy shafts <br> Aimed thus, and thou shalt be the light and pride <br> Of Greece, and of thy father Telamon, <br> Who reared thee from a little child with care <br> In his own halls, though spurious was thy birth. <br> Go on to do him honor, though he now <br> Be far away. And here I say to thee⁠— <br> And I will keep my word⁠—if Jupiter <br> The Aegis-bearer and Minerva deign <br> To let me level the strong walls of Troy, <br> To thee will I assign the noblest prize <br> After my own⁠—a tripod, or two steeds <br> And chariot, or a wife to share thy bed.”</p>
<p class="verse">And thus the blameless Teucer made reply:⁠— <br> “Why, glorious son of Atreus, wouldst thou thus <br> Admonish me, while yet I do my best, <br> And pause not in the combat? From the time <br> When we began to drive the enemy back <br> To Ilium, I have smitten and have slain <br> Their warriors with my bow. Eight barbed shafts <br> I sent, and each has pierced some warlike youth; <br> But this fierce wolf-dog have I failed to strike.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake, and sent another arrow forth <br> At Hector with an eager aim. It missed <br> Its mark, but struck Gorgythion down, the brave <br> And blameless son of Priam; through his breast <br> The arrow went. Fair Castianira brought <br> The warrior forth⁠—a dame from Aesyma, <br> Beautiful as a goddess. As within <br> A garden droops a poppy to the ground, <br> Bowed by its weight and by the rains of spring, <br> So drooped his head within the heavy casque.</p>
<p class="verse">And then did Teucer send another shaft <br> At Hector, eager still to smite. It missed <br> Its aim again, for Phoebus turned aside <br> The arrow, but it struck the charioteer <br> Of Hector, Archeptolemus the brave, <br> When rushing to the fight, and pierced his breast <br> Close to the nipple; from the car he fell, <br> The swift steeds started back, and from his limbs <br> The life and strength departed. A deep grief <br> For his slain charioteer came darkly o’er <br> The mind of Hector, yet, though sorrowing, <br> He left him where he fell, and straightway called <br> Cebriones, his brother, who was near, <br> To mount and take the reins. Cebriones <br> Heard and obeyed. Then from the shining car <br> Leaped Hector with a mighty cry, and seized <br> A ponderous stone, and, bent to crush him, ran <br> At Teucer, who had from his quiver drawn <br> One of his sharpest arrows, placing it <br> Upon the bowstring. As he drew the bow, <br> The strong-armed Hector hurled the jagged stone, <br> And smote him near the shoulder, where the neck <br> And breast are sundered by the collar bone⁠— <br> A fatal spot. The bowstring brake; the arm <br> Fell nerveless; on his knees the archer sank, <br> And dropped the bow. Then did not Ajax leave <br> His fallen brother to the foe, but walked <br> Around him, sheltering him beneath his shield, <br> Till two dear friends of his⁠—Menestheus, son <br> Of Echius, and Alastor nobly born⁠— <br> Approached, and took him up and carried him, <br> Heavily groaning, to the hollow ships.</p>
<p class="verse">Then did Olympian Jove again inspire <br> The Trojan host with valor, and they drave <br> The Achaians backward to the yawning trench. <br> Then Hector came, with fury in his eyes, <br> Among the foremost warriors. As a hound, <br> Sure of his own swift feet, attacks behind <br> The lion or wild boar, and tears his flank, <br> Yet warily observes him as he turns, <br> So Hector followed close the long-haired Greeks, <br> And ever slew the hindmost as they fled. <br> Yet now, when they in flight had crossed again <br> The trench and palisades, and many a one <br> Had died by Trojan hands, they made a halt <br> Before their ships, and bade each other stand, <br> And lifted up their hands and prayed aloud as <br> To all the gods; while Hector, urging on <br> His long-maned steeds, and with stern eyes that seemed <br> The eyes of Gorgon or of murderous Mars, <br> Hither and thither swept across the field.</p>
<p class="verse">The white-armed Juno saw, and, sorrowing, <br> Addressed Minerva with these wingèd words:⁠— <br> “Ah me! Thou daughter of the God who bears <br> The aegis, shall we not descend to aid <br> The perishing Greeks in their extremity? <br> A cruel doom is theirs, to fall, destroyed <br> By one man’s rage⁠—the terrible assault <br> Of Hector, son of Priam, who has made <br> Insufferable havoc in the field.”</p>
<p class="verse">And thus in turn the blue-eyed Pallas spake:⁠— <br> “That warrior long ere this had lost his life, <br> Slain by the Greeks on his paternal soil, <br> But that my father’s mind is warped by wrath. <br> Unjust to me and harsh, he thwarts my aims, <br> Forgetting all I did for Hercules, <br> His son⁠—how often, when Eurystheus set <br> A task too hard for him, I saved his life. <br> To heaven he raised his eyes and wept, and Jove <br> Despatched me instantly to succor him. <br> And yet if I, in my forecasting mind, <br> Had known all this when he was bid to bring <br> From strong-walled Erebus the dog of hell, <br> He had not safely crossed the gulf of Styx. <br> But now Jove hates me; now he grants the wish <br> Of Thetis, who hath kissed his knees and touched <br> His beard caressingly, and prayed that he <br> Would crown the overthrower of walled towns, <br> Achilles, with great honor. Well, the time <br> Will come when he shall call me yet again <br> His dear Minerva. Hasten now to yoke <br> For us thy firm-paced steeds, while in the halls <br> Of aegis-bearing Jupiter I brace <br> My armor on for war⁠—and I shall see <br> If Hector of the beamy helm, the son <br> Of Priam, will rejoice when we appear <br> Upon the field again. Assuredly <br> The men of Troy shall die, to feast the birds <br> Of prey and dogs beside the Grecian fleet.”</p>
<p class="verse">She ended, and the white-armed deity <br> Juno obeyed her. Juno the august, <br> The mighty Saturn’s daughter, hastily <br> Caparisoned the golden-bitted steeds. <br> Meanwhile, Minerva on the palace-floor <br> Of Jupiter let drop the gorgeous robe <br> Of many hues, which her own hands had wrought, <br> And, putting on the Cloud-compeller’s mail, <br> Stood armed for cruel war. And then she climbed <br> The glorious car, and took in hand the spear⁠— <br> Huge, heavy, strong⁠—with which she overthrows <br> The serried phalanxes of valiant men <br> Whene’er this daughter of the Almighty One <br> Is angered. Juno bore the lash, and urged <br> The coursers to their speed. The gates of heaven <br> Opened before them of their own accord⁠— <br> Gates guarded by the Hours, on whom the care <br> Of the great heaven and of Olympus rests, <br> To open or to close the wall of cloud. <br> Through these they guided their impatient steeds.</p>
<p class="verse">From Ida Jupiter beheld, in wrath, <br> And summoned Iris of the golden wings, <br> And bade her do this errand: “Speed thee hence, <br> Fleet Iris! Turn them back; allow them not <br> Thus to defy me: it is not for them <br> To engage with me in war. I give my word⁠— <br> Nor shall it lack fulfilment⁠—I will make <br> The swift steeds lame that draw their car, and hurl <br> The riders down, and dash the car itself <br> To fragments. Ten long years shall wear away <br> Before they cease to suffer from the wounds <br> Made by the thunderbolt. Minerva thus <br> May learn the fate of those who strive with Jove. <br> With Juno I am less displeased, for she <br> Is ever bent to thwart my purposes.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake; and Iris, with the tempest’s speed <br> Departing, bore the message from the heights <br> Of Ida to the great Olympus, where, <br> Among the foremost passes of the mount, <br> All seamed with hollow vales, she met and stayed <br> The pair, delivering thus the word of Jove:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Now whither haste ye? What strange madness fires <br> Your breasts? The son of Saturn suffers not <br> That ye befriend the Greeks. He threatens thus⁠— <br> And will fulfil his threat⁠—that he will make <br> The coursers lame that draw your car, and hurl <br> The riders down, and dash the car itself <br> To fragments, and that ten long years must pass <br> Ere ye shall cease to suffer from the wounds <br> Made by the thunderbolt. So shalt thou learn, <br> O Pallas! what it is to strive with Jove. <br> With Juno is he less displeased, for she <br> Is ever bent to thwart his purposes; <br> But thou, he says, art guilty above all, <br> And shameless as a hound, if thou dare lift <br> Thy massive spear against thy father Jove.”</p>
<p class="verse">So spake fleet-footed Iris, and withdrew; <br> And thus again to Pallas Juno said:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Child of the Aegis-bearer! Let us strive <br> With Jove no longer for the sake of men, <br> But let one perish and another live, <br> As chance may rule the hour, and let the God, <br> Communing with his secret mind, mete out <br> To Greeks and Trojans their just destiny.”</p>
<p class="verse">She spake, and turned the firm-paced coursers back, <br> The coursers with fair-flowing manes. The Hours <br> Unyoked them, bound them to the ambrosial stalls, <br> And leaned against the shining walls the car; <br> While Juno and Minerva went among <br> The other deities and took their place <br> Upon their golden seats, though sad at heart. <br> Then with his steeds, and in his bright-wheeled car, <br> Came Jove from Ida to the dwelling-place <br> Of gods upon Olympus. There did he <br> Who shakes the islands loose the steeds and bring <br> The chariot to its place, and o’er it spread <br> Its covering of lawn. The Thunderer <br> Seated himself upon his golden throne, <br> The great Olympus trembling as he stepped; <br> While Juno and Minerva sat apart <br> Together, nor saluted him, nor asked <br> Of aught; but he perceived their thoughts and said:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Juno and Pallas! Why so sad? Not long <br> Ye toiled in glorious battle to destroy <br> The Trojans, whom ye hold in bitter hate: <br> This strength of mine, and this invincible arm <br> Not all the gods upon the Olympian mount <br> Can turn to flight, while your fair limbs were seized <br> With trembling ere ye entered on the shock <br> And havoc of the war. Now let me say⁠— <br> And well the event would have fulfilled my words⁠— <br> That, smitten with the thunder from my hand, <br> Your chariots never would have brought you back <br> To this Olympus and the abode of gods.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake; while Pallas and the queen of heaven <br> Repined with close-pressed lips, and in their hearts <br> Devised new mischiefs for the Trojan race. <br> Silent Minerva sat, nor dared express as <br> The anger that she bore her father Jove; <br> But Juno could not curb her wrath, and spake:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“What words, austere Saturnius, hast thou said? <br> Thou art, we know, invincible in might; <br> Yet must we sorrow for the heroic Greeks, <br> Who, by a cruel fate, are perishing. <br> We stand aloof from war, if thou require; <br> Yet would we counsel the Achaian host, <br> Lest by thy wrath they perish utterly.”</p>
<p class="verse">And then the Cloud-compeller, answering, said:⁠— <br> “O Juno, large-eyed and august, if thou <br> Look forth tomorrow, thou shalt then behold <br> The all-powerful son of Saturn laying waste <br> With greater havoc still the mighty host <br> Of warlike Greeks. For Hector, great in war, <br> Shall pause not from the conflict, till he rouse <br> The swift-paced son of Peleus at the ships, <br> When, pent in narrow space, the armies fight <br> For slain Patroclus: such the will of fate. <br> As for thyself, I little heed thy rage: <br> Not even shouldst thou wander to the realm <br> Where earth and ocean end, where Saturn sits <br> Beside Iapetus, and neither light <br> Of overgoing suns nor breath of wind <br> Refreshes them, but gulfs of Tartarus <br> Surround them⁠—shouldst thou even thither bend <br> Thy way, I shall not heed thy rage, who art <br> Beyond all others shamelessly perverse.”</p>
<p class="verse">He ceased; but white-armed Juno answered not. <br> And now into the sea the sun’s bright light <br> Went down, and o’er the foodful earth was drawn <br> Night’s shadow. Most unwillingly the sons <br> Of Troy beheld the sunset. To the Greeks <br> Eagerly wished the welcome darkness came.</p>
<p class="verse">Then from the fleet illustrious Hector led <br> The Trojans, and beside the eddying stream, <br> In a clear space uncumbered by the slain, <br> Held council. There, alighting from their cars, <br> They listened to the words that Hector spake⁠— <br> Hector, beloved of Jove. He held a spear, <br> In length eleven cubits, with a blade <br> Of glittering brass, bound with a ring of gold. <br> On this he leaned, and spake these wingèd words:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Hear me, ye Trojans, Dardans, and allies. <br> But now I thought that, having first destroyed <br> The Achaian host and fleet, we should return <br> This night to wind-swept Ilium. To their aid <br> The darkness comes, and saves the Greeks, and saves <br> Their galleys ranged along the ocean-side. <br> Obey we, then, the dark-browed night; prepare <br> Our meal; unyoke the steeds with flowing manes, <br> And set their food before them. Bring at once <br> Oxen and fatlings of the flock from town, <br> And from your dwellings bread and pleasant wine. <br> And let us gather store of wood, to feed <br> A multitude of blazing fires all night, <br> Till Morning, daughter of the Dawn, appear⁠— <br> Fires that shall light the sky, lest in the hours <br> Of darkness with their ships the long-haired Greeks <br> Attempt escape across the mighty deep. <br> And, that they may not climb their decks unharmed. <br> Let every foeman bear a wound to cure <br> At home⁠—an arrow-wound or gash of spear, <br> Given as he leaps on board. So other foes <br> Shall dread a conflict with the knights of Troy. <br> And let the heralds, dear to Jove, command <br> That all grown youths and hoary-headed men <br> Keep watch about the city in the towers <br> Built by the gods; and let the feebler sex <br> Kindle large fires upon their hearths at home; <br> And let the guard be strengthened, lest the foe <br> Should steal into the city while its sons <br> Are all abroad. Thus let it be till morn, <br> Brave Trojans! I but speak of what the time <br> Requires, and on the morrow I shall speak <br> Of what the Trojan knights have then to do. <br> My prayer to Jove and to the other gods, <br> And my hope is, that I may drive away <br> These curs, brought hither by an evil fate <br> In their black ships. All night will we keep watch, <br> And, arming, with the early morn renew <br> The desperate conflict at the hollow ships. <br> Then shall I see if valiant Diomed <br> Tydides has the power to make me leave <br> The Grecian galleys for the city-walls, <br> Or whether I shall slay him with my spear <br> And take his bloody spoils. Tomorrow’s sun <br> Will make his valor known, if he withstand <br> The assault of this my weapon. Yet I think <br> The sunrise will behold him slain among <br> The first, with many comrades lying round. <br> Would that I knew myself as certainly <br> Secure from death and the decays of age, <br> And to be held in honor like the gods <br> Apollo and Minerva, as I know <br> This day will bring misfortune to the Greeks!”</p>
<p class="verse">So Hector spake, and all the Trojan host <br> Applauded; from the yoke forthwith they looped <br> The sweaty steeds, and bound them to the cars <br> With halters; to the town they sent in haste <br> For oxen and the fatlings of the flock, <br> And to their homes for bread and pleasant wine, <br> And gathered fuel in large store. The winds <br> Bore up the fragrant fumes from earth to heaven.</p>
<p class="verse">So, high in hope, they sat the whole night through <br> In warlike lines, and many watch-fires blazed. <br> As when in heaven the stars look brightly forth <br> Round the clear-shining moon, while not a breeze <br> Stirs in the depths of air, and all the stars <br> Are seen, and gladness fills the shepherd’s heart, <br> So many fires in sight of Ilium blazed, <br> Lit by the sons of Troy, between the ships <br> And eddying Xanthus: on the plain there shone <br> A thousand; fifty warriors by each fire <br> Sat in its light. Their steeds beside the cars⁠— <br> Champing their oats and their white barley⁠—stood, <br> And waited for the golden morn to rise.</p>
