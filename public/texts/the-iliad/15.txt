<p class="lede">The anger of Jupiter on awaking appeased by Juno’s denial that she had instigated Neptune to aid the Greeks⁠—Iris despatched to recall Neptune from the field⁠—Mars, enraged at the death of his son Ascalaphus and arming to aid the Trojans, is restrained by Minerva⁠—Hector healed by Apollo⁠—His return to the field⁠—The Greeks driven back to the ships by the Trojans, who attempt to set the fleet on fire⁠—Defence of the ships by Ajax.</p>
<p class="verse">Now when the Trojans in their flight had crossed <br> Rampart and trench, and many had been slain <br> By the pursuing Greeks, they made a halt <br> Beside their chariots, in despair and pale <br> With terror. Meanwhile Jupiter awoke, <br> On Ida’s height, from slumber by the side <br> Of Juno, goddess of the golden throne. <br> At once he rose and saw the Trojan host <br> Routed, and, following close upon their flight, <br> The Argive warriors putting them to rout, <br> Aided by Neptune, sovereign of the sea, <br> And Hector lying on the field among <br> His fellow-warriors, breathing painfully, <br> Vomiting blood, and senseless, for the arm <br> That smote was not the feeblest of the Greeks. <br> The Father of immortals and of men <br> Beheld and pitied him, and terribly <br> Frowned upon Juno, and bespake her thus:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“O evil minded Juno, full of guile! <br> Thy arts have made the noble Hector leave <br> The combat, and have forced his troops to flee. <br> I know not whether’t were not well that thou <br> Shouldst taste the fruit of thy pernicious wiles, <br> Chastised by me with stripes. Dost thou forget <br> When thou didst swing suspended, and I tied <br> Two anvils to thy feet, and bound a chain <br> Of gold that none could break around thy wrists? <br> Then didst thou hang in air amid the clouds, <br> And all the gods of high Olympus saw <br> With pity. They stood near, but none of them <br> Were able to release thee. Whoso came <br> Within my reach I seized, and hurled him o’er <br> Heaven’s threshold, and he fell upon the earth <br> Scarce breathing. Yet the passion of my wrath, <br> Caused by the wrongs of godlike Hercules, <br> Was not to be so calmed; for craftily <br> Hadst thou called up the violent northern blast, <br> To chase him far across the barren deep, <br> And drive him from his course to populous Cos. <br> I rescued him at length, and brought him back <br> To Argos famed for steeds, though after long <br> And many hardships. I remind thee now <br> Of this, that thou mayst see of what avail <br> Hereafter thy dissembled love and all <br> Thy cunning strategies will be to thee.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake, and Juno, large-eyed and august, <br> Shuddered, and answered Jove with wingèd words:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Be witness, Earth, and the great Heavens above, <br> And waters of the Styx that glide beneath⁠— <br> That dreadful oath which most the blessed gods <br> Revere⁠—be witness, too, that sacred head <br> Of thine, and our own nuptial couch, by which <br> I would not rashly swear at any time, <br> That not by my persuasion Neptune went⁠— <br> The shaker of the shores⁠—to harass Troy <br> And Hector, and to aid the cause of Greece. <br> He went self-counselled; he had seen the Greeks <br> Pressed grievously beside their fleet, and took <br> Compassion on them. Yet would I advise <br> That he obey thy word, and take his place <br> Where thou, the Cloud compeller, bid’st him go.”</p>
<p class="verse">She ended, and the Father of the gods <br> And mortals smiled, and said, in wingèd words:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Large-eyed, imperial Juno, wouldst thou sit <br> In council with the immortals, and assist <br> My purposes, then Neptune, though at heart <br> He were averse, would yet conform his will <br> To mine and thine. If thou dost truly speak, <br> And from thy heart, go now to where the gods <br> Assemble, summon Iris, and with her <br> The archer-god Apollo. Give in charge <br> To Iris that she hasten to the host <br> Of the mailed Greeks, and bid king Neptune leave, <br> The battle for his palace. Let the god <br> Phoebus, preparing Hector for the fight, <br> Breathe strength into his frame, that so he lose <br> The sense of pain which bows his spirit now, <br> And he shall force the Greeks again to flee <br> In craven fear. Then shall their flying host <br> Fall back upon the galleys of the son <br> Of Peleus, who shall send into the fight <br> His friend Patroclus. Him the mighty spear <br> Of Hector shall o’erthrow before the walls <br> Of Ilium, after many a Trojan youth <br> Shall by his hand have fallen, and with them <br> My noble son, Sarpedon. Roused to rage, <br> Then shall the great Achilles take the life <br> Of Hector. Be it from this time my care <br> That all the assaults of Trojans in the fleet <br> Be beaten back, till by Minerva’s aid <br> The Greeks possess the lofty town of Troy. <br> Still am I angry, nor will I allow <br> One of the ever-living gods to aid <br> The Greeks, until the prayer of Peleus’ son <br> Shall fully be accomplished, as my word <br> And nod were given, when Thetis clasped my knees, <br> Entreating me to honor, signally, <br> Her son, Achilles, spoiler of walled towns.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake; the white-armed goddess willingly <br> Obeyed him, and from Ida’s summit flew <br> To high Olympus. As the thought of man <br> Flies rapidly, when, having travelled far, <br> He thinks, “Here would I be, I would be there,” <br> And flits from place to place, so swiftly flew <br> Imperial Juno to the Olympian mount, <br> And there she found the ever-living gods <br> Assembled in the halls of Jupiter. <br> These, as they saw her, starting from their seats, <br> Reached forth their cups to greet her. All the rest <br> She overlooked, and took the beaker held <br> By blooming Themis, who in haste had run <br> To meet her, and in wingèd accents said:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Why comest thou, O Juno! with the look <br> Of one o’ercome with fear. Hath Saturn’s son, <br> Thy lord, disquieted thy soul with threats?”</p>
<p class="verse">The white-armed goddess Juno answered her:⁠— <br> “Ask me not, heavenly Themis⁠—thou dost know <br> The cruel, arrogant temper that is his⁠— <br> But sit presiding at the common feast, <br> In this fair palace of the gods, and thou <br> And all in heaven shall hear what evils Jove <br> Has threatened. All, I think, will not rejoice <br> To hear the tidings, be they gods or men, <br> Though some contentedly are feasting now.”</p>
<p class="verse">Thus having said, imperial Juno took <br> Her place, and all the gods within the halls <br> Of Jupiter were grieved. The goddess smiled, <br> But only with the lips; her forehead wore <br> Above the jetty brows no sign of joy, <br> While thus she spake in anger to the rest:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Vainly, and in our madness, do we strive <br> With Father Jove. We come and seek by craft <br> Or force to move his stubborn will; he sits <br> Apart, unyielding, unregarding, proud <br> Of the vast strength and power in which he stands <br> Above all other of the deathless gods. <br> Bear therefore patiently whatever ill <br> He sends to each. Already, as I learn, <br> Hath Mars his share of sorrow. In the war <br> Ascalaphus hath perished, whom he loved <br> Dearly, beyond all other men, and whom <br> The fiery god acknowledged as his son.”</p>
<p class="verse">As thus she spake, Mars smote his sinewy thighs <br> With his dropped hands, and sorrowfully said:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Be not offended with me, ye who make <br> Your dwelling on Olympus, if I go <br> Down to the Achaian fleet, and there avenge <br> The slaughter of my son, though I be doomed <br> To fall before the thunderbolt of Jove, <br> And lie in blood and dust among the dead.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake, and summoned Fear and Flight to yoke <br> His steeds, and put his glorious armor on. <br> Then greater and more terrible had been <br> The avenging wrath of Jupiter inflamed <br> Against the gods, if Pallas in her fear <br> For all the heavenly dwellers had not left <br> Her throne, and, rushing through the portals, snatched <br> The helmet from his head, and from his arm <br> The shield, and from his brawny hand the spear, <br> And laid the brazen weapon by, and thus <br> Rebuked the fiery temper of the god:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Thou madman, thou art frantic, thou art lost! <br> Hast thou not ears to hear, nor any shame <br> Nor reason left? Hast thou not heard the words <br> Of white-armed Juno, who so lately left <br> Olympian Jupiter? Wouldst thou return <br> In pain and sorrow to the Olympian heights, <br> Driven back ingloriously, and made the cause <br> Of many miseries to all the gods?⁠— <br> For Jove would leave the Trojans and their foes, <br> The gallant Greeks, and turn on us, and bring <br> Ruin upon Olympus. He would seize <br> Guilty and guiltless in his rage alike. <br> Wherefore I counsel thee to lay aside <br> Resentment for the slaughter of thy son, <br> Since braver men and stronger have been slain, <br> And will be slain hereafter. Vain it were <br> To seek from death to save the race of man.”</p>
<p class="verse">She said, and, leading back the fiery Mars, <br> Seated him on his throne, while Juno called <br> Apollo forth, with Iris, messenger <br> Of heaven, and thus in wingèd accents spake:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Jove calls you both to Ida. When ye reach <br> Its heights, and look upon his countenance, <br> Receive his sovereign mandate and obey.”</p>
<p class="verse">So spake imperial Juno, and withdrew <br> And took her seat again, while they in haste <br> Flew toward the mount of Ida, seamed with rills <br> And nurse of savage beasts. Upon the top <br> Of Gargarus they found the Thunderer, <br> The son of Saturn, sitting. In a cloud <br> Of fragrant haze he sat concealed; the twain <br> Entered and stood before the God of Storms, <br> Who saw them not displeased, so speedily <br> Had they obeyed his consort. First he turned <br> To Iris, and in wingèd accents said:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Haste thee, swift Iris, and report my words <br> To royal Neptune, and report them right. <br> Bid him, withdrawing from the battle-field, <br> Repair to the assembly of the gods, <br> Or the great ocean. If he disobey, <br> Contemning my command, then bid him think <br> Maturely, whether, mighty though he be, <br> He can withstand when I put forth my power <br> Against him. Greater is my strength than his, <br> And elder-born am I. Yet in his pride <br> Of heart he dares to call himself my peer, <br> Though all the others look on me with awe.”</p>
<p class="verse">Thus spake the god, and Iris, whose swift feet <br> Are like the wind, obeyed, and downward plunged <br> From Ida’s height to sacred Troy. As when <br> Snow-flakes or icy hail are dropped to earth <br> From clouds before the north wind when it sweeps <br> The sky, so darted Iris to the ground, <br> And stood by mighty Neptune’s side, and said:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“O dark-haired shaker of the shores, I bring <br> A message from the Aegis-bearer, Jove, <br> That thou, withdrawing from the battle-field, <br> Repair to the assembly of the gods, <br> Or the great ocean. If thou disobey, <br> Contemning his command, then hear his threat: <br> He will come hither and put forth his power <br> Against thee, and he warns thee not to tempt <br> The strife; for greater is his power than thine, <br> And he is elder-born, though in thy pride <br> Of heart thou dost declare thyself the peer <br> Of him whom all the rest regard with awe.”</p>
<p class="verse">Illustrious Neptune answered with disdain:⁠— <br> “In truth an arrogant speech; he seeks by force <br> To bar me from my purpose, who can claim <br> Rights equal to his own, though great his power. <br> We are three brothers⁠—Rhea brought us forth⁠— <br> The sons of Saturn⁠—Jupiter, and I, <br> And Pluto, regent of the realm below. <br> Three parts were made of all existing things, <br> And each of us received his heritage. <br> The lots were shaken; and to me it fell <br> To dwell forever in the hoary deep, <br> And Pluto took the gloomy realm of night, <br> And, lastly, Jupiter the ample heaven <br> And air and clouds. Yet doth the earth remain, <br> With high Olympus, common to us all. <br> Therefore I yield me not to do his will, <br> Great as he is; and let him be content <br> With his third part. He cannot frighten me <br> With gestures of his arm. Let him insult <br> With menaces the daughters and the sons <br> Of his own loves, and give them law, since they <br> Perforce must hear, and patiently submit.”</p>
<p class="verse">Then the fleet-footed Iris spake again:⁠— <br> “O dark-haired Neptune, shall I bear from thee <br> This harsh, defiant answer back to Jove, <br> Or shall it yet be changed? The prudent mind <br> Yields to the occasion, and thou knowest well <br> The Furies wait upon the elder-born.”</p>
<p class="verse">Then spake in turn the god who shakes the shores:⁠— <br> “O goddess Iris, thou hast wisely said. <br> An excellent thing it is when messengers <br> Know how to counsel well. But in my heart <br> And soul a wrathful sense of injury <br> Arises when he chides with insolent words <br> Me, who was equal with him in my lot, <br> And born to equal destinies. Yet now, <br> Although offended, I give way; but this <br> I tell thee, and ’tis from my heart⁠—if he, <br> In spite of me and Pallas, spoiler-queen, <br> And Juno, Mercury, and Vulcan, spare <br> The towers of Troy⁠—if he refuse to bring <br> Ruin on her, and glory on the Greeks, <br> Then let him know that hatred without end <br> Or intermission is between us two.”</p>
<p class="verse">As thus he spake, the shaker of the shores <br> Quitted the Grecian army, took his way <br> Seaward, and plunged into the deep. The host <br> Perceived their loss. Then Cloud-compelling Jove <br> Turned to Apollo and addressed him thus:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Now go at once to Hector, mailed in brass, <br> Beloved Phoebus, for the god who shakes <br> The earth, departing to the ocean-deeps, <br> Avoids our wrath; else had the other gods, <br> Even they who far beneath the earth surround <br> Old Saturn, heard our quarrel. Well it is <br> For both of us that he, although enraged, <br> Braved not my arm, for otherwise the strife <br> Had not been ended without sweat. Now take <br> The fringèd aegis in thy hands, and shake <br> Its orb before the warrior Greeks, to fill <br> Their hearts with fear. I give, O archer-god, <br> Illustrious Hector to thy charge. Revive <br> The might that dwelt within him, till the Greeks <br> Reach, in their flight, the fleet and Hellespont; <br> Then shall it be my care, by word and deed, <br> To give them rest and respite from their toils.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake: Apollo hearkened and obeyed <br> His father, darting down from Ida’s height <br> Like the fleet falcon, chaser of the dove, <br> And swiftest of the race of birds. He found <br> Hector, the warlike Priam’s noble son, <br> No longer on his bed. He sat upright; <br> The life was coming back; he knew again <br> His friends; the heavy breathing ceased; the sweat <br> Was stanched; the will of aegis-bearing Jove <br> Revived the warrior’s strength. The archer-god, <br> Phoebus, approached, and, standing by him, said:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Why, Hector, son of Priam, dost thou sit <br> Languishing thus, apart from all the host? <br> Has aught of evil overtaken thee?”</p>
<p class="verse">And then the crested Hector feebly said: <br> “Who mayst thou be, O kindest of the gods, <br> That thus dost question me? Hast thou not heard <br> That the great warrior Ajax, with a stone, <br> Smote me upon the breast, and made me leave <br> The battle-field, where I o’ertook and slew <br> His comrades by the galleys of the Greeks? <br> I thought to be this day among the dead <br> In Pluto’s mansion; even now it seemed <br> That I was breathing my dear life away.”</p>
<p class="verse">Then spake again Apollo, archer-god:⁠— <br> “Take courage, for the son of Saturn sends <br> From Ida’s summit one who will attend <br> And aid thee⁠—Phoebus of the golden sword, <br> Long practised to defend thy Troy and thee. <br> Rise now, encouraging thy numerous host <br> Of charioteers to press with their swift steeds <br> Straight toward the roomy galleys of the Greeks, <br> I go before to smooth for them the way, <br> And turn the Achaian bands, and make them flee.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake, and into the great ruler’s breast <br> Breathed strength and courage. As a stabled horse, <br> Fed at his crib with barley, breaks the thong <br> That fastened him, and, issuing, scours the plain <br> Where he was wont in some smooth-flowing stream <br> To bathe his sides⁠—he holds his head aloft <br> Proudly, and o’er his shoulders streams the mane⁠— <br> Consciously beautiful, he darts away <br> On nimble knees, that bear him to the fields <br> He knows so well, and pastures of the mares;⁠— <br> So after he had hearkened to the god <br> Moved the swift feet of Hector, and he flew <br> To cheer his horsemen on. As peasant men <br> Rush with their dogs in chase of horned stag <br> Or mountain goat, whose refuge is among <br> Thickets and lofty rocks, nor can they take <br> Their prey, for at their clamor there appears <br> A maned lion in the way, and turns <br> The chasers back, although in hot pursuit⁠— <br> Thus did the Greeks embattled close pursue <br> The men of Ilium, striking with their swords <br> And two-edged spears; but when at length they saw <br> Hector among the ranks of armèd men, <br> Their hearts were troubled, and their courage sank.</p>
<p class="verse">Thoas, Andrannon’s son, the bravest far <br> Among the Aetolians, skilled to cast the spear <br> And combat hand to hand, addressed the Greeks. <br> In council few excelled him, when the youths <br> Assembled for debate. With prudent speech <br> Thoas bespake his fellow-warriors thus:⁠— <br> “Gods! What a marvel do mine eyes behold; <br> Hector has risen from death! We fully thought, <br> Each one of us, that, smitten by the hand <br> Of Telamonian Ajax, he had died. <br> Some god hath rescued and restored to strength <br> This Hector who hath slain, and yet will slay, <br> I fear, so many Greeks. He comes not thus <br> Leading the charge without the aid of Jove, <br> The God of Thunders. Now let all of us <br> Follow this counsel: bid the multitude <br> Retreat upon the ships, and let the rest, <br> Who boast ourselves the bravest of the host, <br> Stand firm and breast his onset, and so break <br> Its fury with our lifted spears. I think, <br> With all his rage, he will be slow to fling <br> Himself into a band of armèd Greeks.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake; they hearkened and at once complied; <br> The Ajaxes, the Prince Idomeneus, <br> Teucer, Meriones, and Meges, peer <br> Of Mars, assembled all the chiefs, and ranked <br> Their files to encounter Hector and his band <br> Of Trojans, while the multitude fell back <br> To the Greek galleys. Then, in close array, <br> The Trojan host moved forward. Hector led <br> The van in rapid march. Before him walked <br> Phoebus, the terrible aegis in his hands <br> Dazzlingly bright within its shaggy fringe, <br> By Vulcan forged, the great artificer, <br> And given to Jupiter, with which to rout <br> Armies of men. With this in hand he led <br> The assailants on. The Achaians kept their ground <br> In serried ranks, and a sharp yell arose <br> From Greeks and Trojans. Arrows from the string <br> Flew through the air, and spears from valiant hands. <br> Some pierced the breasts of warrior-youths, but more <br> Fell half-way ere they reached their aim, and plunged <br> Into the ground, still hungering for their prey. <br> As long as Phoebus held the aegis still, <br> The weapons reached and wounded equally <br> Both armies, and in both the people fell; <br> But ever when the god looked face to face <br> On the Greek knights, and shook the orb, and gave <br> A mighty shout, he made their hearts to sink <br> Within their bosoms, and their courage fled. <br> As when two beasts of prey at dead of night <br> Suddenly, while their keeper is away, <br> Scatter a herd of beeves or flock of sheep, <br> So the disheartened Greeks were put to rout <br> For Phoebus sent among them fear, and gave <br> Victory to Hector and the men of Troy.</p>
<p class="verse">Then, as the lines were broken, man slew man. <br> First Stichius fell by Hector’s hand, and next <br> Arcesilaus; one was chief among <br> The mailed Boeotians, one the trusty friend <br> Of brave Menestheus. Medon fell before <br> Aeneas, and with him Iasus died. <br> Medon was great Otleus’ base-born son, <br> And Ajax was his brother, and he dwelt <br> In Phylacè, an exile, for his hand <br> Had slain the brother of his father’s wife, <br> The step-dame Eriopis, late espoused. <br> Iasus was appointed to command <br> The warriors sent from Athens, and he claimed <br> His birth from Sphelus, son of Bucolus. <br> Mecistes fell before Polydamas. <br> Polites struck down Echius in the van, <br> And Clonius died by great Agenor’s hand; <br> And Paris, when Deiochus had turned <br> To flee, among the foremost combatants, <br> Smote him upon the shoulder from behind, <br> And drave the brazen weapon through his heart.</p>
<p class="verse">Then, while the Trojans stripped the dead, the Greeks <br> Fled every way, and, falling as they ran <br> Into the trench and on the stakes, were driven <br> Back o’er the rampart. Hector lifted up <br> His mighty voice, and bade the Trojans leave <br> The bloody spoil and hasten to the ships. <br> “And whomsoever I shall find apart <br> In any place, at distance from the ships, <br> There will I slay him. None of all his kin, <br> Women or men, shall build his funeral pile, <br> But dogs shall tear his limbs in sight of Troy.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake; and on the shoulders of his steeds <br> He laid the lash, and urged them toward the foe, <br> And cheered the Trojans on. They joined their shouts <br> To his, and charged with all their steeds and cars; <br> And fearful was the din. Apollo marched <br> Before them, treading down with mighty feet <br> The banks of the deep ditch, and casting them <br> Back to the middle, till a causey rose, <br> Broad, and of length like that to which a spear <br> Reaches when thrown by one who tries his strength. <br> O’er this the Trojans poured into the camp <br> By squadrons, with Apollo still in front, <br> Holding the marvellous aegis. He with ease <br> O’erthrew the rampart. As a boy at play <br> Among the sea-shore sands in childish sport <br> Scatters with feet and hands the little mounds <br> He reared, thus didst thou cause the mighty work, <br> O archer Phoebus, which the Greeks had reared <br> With so much toil, to crumble. Thou didst fill <br> Their hearts with eager thoughts of flight, till, hemmed <br> Between the assailants and their ships, they stopped <br> And bade each other stand, and raised their hands <br> To all the gods, and offered vows aloud. <br> Gerenian Nestor, guardian of the Greeks, <br> With arms extended toward the starry skies, <br> Prayed earnestly: “O Father Jove, if e’er <br> In fruitful Argos there were burned to thee <br> The thighs of fattened oxen or of sheep, <br> By one who asked a safe return to Greece, <br> And thou didst promise it, remember him, <br> God of Olympus, and avert from us <br> The day of evil. Suffer not the Greeks <br> To perish, slaughtered by the sons of Troy.”</p>
<p class="verse">So spake he supplicating. Jupiter <br> The All-disposer thundered as he heard <br> The old man’s prayer. The Trojans by that voice <br> Of aegis-bearing Jove were moved to press <br> The Greeks more resolutely, and were filled <br> With fiercer valor. As a mighty wave <br> On the great ocean, driven before a gale <br> Such as rolls up the hugest billow, sweeps <br> O’er the ship’s side, so swept the Trojan host <br> With dreadful tumult o’er the wall. They drave <br> Their steeds into the camp, and there they fought <br> Beside the galley-sterns, and hand to hand, <br> With two-edged spears⁠—they from their cars, the Greeks <br> From their black ships on high with long-stemmed poles <br> Which lay upon the decks, prepared for fight <br> At sea, and strongly joined to blades of brass.</p>
<p class="verse">Patroclus, while the Greeks and Trojans fought <br> Around the wall, at distance from the fleet <br> Sat with the brave Eurypylus in his tent, <br> Amusing him with pleasant talk, and dressed <br> His wound with balms that calmed the bitter pain. <br> But when he saw the Trojans bursting in <br> Over the wall, and heard the din, and saw <br> The Achaians put to rout, he gave a cry <br> Of sudden grief, and with his open hands <br> Smote both his thighs, and sorrowfully said:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Eurypylus, I cannot stay with thee, <br> Much as thou needest me, for desperate grows <br> The struggle. Now let thine attendant take <br> The charge of thee. I hasten to persuade <br> Achilles to the field. Who knows but I, <br> With Jove’s good help, may change his purpose yet? <br> For potent are the counsels of a friend.”</p>
<p class="verse">The hero spake, and instantly his feet <br> Bore him away. Meanwhile the Achaian host <br> Firmly withstood the onset of their foes. <br> And yet, though greater was their multitude, <br> They could not drive the Trojans from the fleet, <br> Nor could the Trojans break, with all their power, <br> The serried lines, and reach the tents and ships. <br> As when a plumb-line, in the skilful hands <br> Of shipwright well instructed in his art <br> By Pallas, squares the beam that builds a barque, <br> So even was the fortune of the fray.</p>
<p class="verse">While some beside one galley waged the war, <br> And others round another, Hector came <br> To encounter Ajax the renowned, and both <br> Fought for one ship. The Trojan could not drive <br> The Greek away, and burn his ship with fire, <br> Nor the Greek drive the Trojan, for a god <br> Had brought him thither. Then did Ajax smite <br> Caletor, son of Clytius, with his spear <br> Upon the breast, as he was bringing fire <br> To burn the ship; he dropped the torch, and fell, <br> With clashing armor. Hector, as he saw <br> His kinsman lying slain amid the dust <br> By the black galley, raised his voice, and thus <br> Called to the Lycians and the men of Troy:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Hear, men of Troy and Lycia, and ye sons <br> Of Dardanus, who combat hand to hand, <br> Stand firm, and never yield this narrow ground. <br> Rescue the son of Clytius, who has fallen <br> Before the ships, nor let the Achaians make <br> His arms their spoil.” The hero spake, and aimed <br> His shining spear at Ajax, whom it missed, <br> But smote Lycophron, Master’s son, who served <br> Ajax, and dwelt with him, for he had left <br> His native land, Cythera, having slain <br> One of the gallant Cytherean race. <br> Him Hector smote upon the head beneath <br> The ear with his keen weapon, as he stood <br> Near Ajax; from the galley’s stern he fell <br> Headlong upon the ground, with lifeless limbs. <br> Then to his brother Teucer Ajax spake:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Dear Teucer, see, our faithful friend is gone, <br> The son of Mastor, from Cythera’s isle, <br> Whom we had learned to honor equally <br> With our own parents in our palaces. <br> He falls before the great-souled Hector’s hand. <br> Where, then, are now thy shafts that carry death, <br> And where the bow that Phoebus gave to thee?”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake, and Teucer, hearkening, came in haste, <br> With his bent bow, and quiver full of shafts, <br> And, standing near him, sent his arrows forth <br> Among the Trojan warriors. There he smote <br> Clitus, Pisenor’s eminent son, the friend <br> Of the renowned Polydamas, who claimed <br> His birth from Panthoüs. Clitus held the reins, <br> Guiding the coursers of Polydamas <br> Where most the crowded Grecian phalanxes <br> Wavered and broke, that so he might support <br> Hector and his companions. Soon he met, <br> Brave as he was, disaster which no hand <br> Had power to avert: the bitter arrow struck <br> His neck behind, and from the chariot-seat <br> He fell to earth; the startled steeds sprang back; <br> The empty chariot rattled. This the king <br> Polydamas perceived, and came to meet <br> His steeds, and gave them to Astinous, <br> The son of Protiaon, charging him <br> To keep them ever near, and in his sight, <br> While he, returning, mingled with the throng <br> That struggled in the van. Then Teucer aimed <br> Another shaft at Hector mailed in brass, <br> Which, had it reached him fighting gallantly, <br> Had made him leave the battle, for his life <br> Had ended there. The act was not unseen <br> By All-disposing Jupiter, whose power <br> Protected Hector, and denied the Greek <br> The glory hoped for; for he snapped in twain <br> The firmly twisted cord as Teucer drew <br> That perfect bow; the brazen arrow flew <br> Aside; the warrior’s hands let fall the bow, <br> And, shuddering, he bespake his brother thus:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Now woe is me! Some deity, no doubt, <br> Brings all our plans to nought. ’Tis he whose touch <br> Strikes from my hand the bow, and snaps in twain <br> The cord just twisted, which I bound myself <br> This morning to the bow, that it might bear <br> The frequent arrow bounding toward the foe.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake, and thus replied the man of might, <br> The Telamonian Ajax: “Lay aside <br> Thy bow, my brother, and thy store of shafts, <br> Since, in displeasure with the Greeks, a god <br> Has made them useless. Haste to arm thy hand <br> With a long spear, and on thy shoulders lay <br> A buckler, and with these attack the foe, <br> And bid thy fellows stand. Let Trojans see <br> That, even though the day thus far be theirs, <br> They cannot lay their hands on our good ships <br> Without a mighty struggle. Let us all <br> Be mindful of our fame for gallant deeds.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake, and Teucer went to place the bow <br> Within the tents, and on his shoulders hung <br> A fourfold shield, and placed on his grand brows <br> A stately helmet with a horse-hair crest <br> That nodded fearfully. He took in hand <br> A ponderous spear with brazen blade, and sprang <br> Forward with hasty steps, and stood beside <br> His brother Ajax. Hector, when he saw <br> That Teucer’s shafts had failed him, called aloud <br> Upon the men of Lycia and of Troy:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Ye men of Troy and Lycia, and ye sons <br> Of Dardanus who combat hand to hand, <br> Acquit yourselves like men, my friends, and prove <br> Your fiery valor by these roomy ships; <br> For I have seen with mine own eyes the shafts <br> Of their chief warrior rendered impotent <br> By Jupiter. His hand is plainly seen <br> Among the sons of men; to some he gives <br> Glory above the rest; from some he takes <br> The glory, and withdraws from their defence. <br> He withers now the courage of the Greeks, <br> And succors us. Press closely round the fleet, <br> And combat. Whosoe’er among you all, <br> Wounded or beaten down, shall meet his death, <br> So let him die; ’tis no inglorious fate <br> To perish fighting in his country’s cause; <br> And he shall leave his wife and children safe, <br> His home and household store inviolate, <br> If now the Greeks depart to their own land.”</p>
<p class="verse">With words like these he filled their hearts anew <br> With strength and courage. On the other side <br> Ajax exhorted thus his warrior friends:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Shame on you, Greeks! We perish here, unless <br> We rescue with strong arms our host and fleet. <br> Think ye that, should the crested Hector seize <br> Our galleys, ye may reach your homes on foot? <br> Hear ye not Hector’s voice, who, fiercely bent <br> To burn our ships with fire, is cheering on <br> His warriors? To no dance he summons them, <br> But to the battle. Nought is left for us, <br> And other counsel there is none, save this: <br> Close with the foe; let every hand put forth <br> Its strength; far better’t were to die at once, <br> Or make at once our safety sure, than thus <br> To waste away, in lingering fight, beside <br> Our ships, destroyed by weaker arms than ours.”</p>
<p class="verse">So spake the chief, and all who heard received <br> Courage and strength. Then Hector put to death <br> Schedius, the son of Perimedes, prince <br> Of the Phocaeans. Ajax also slew <br> Laodamas, Antenor’s honored son, <br> A chief of infantry. Polydamas <br> Struck down Cyllenian Otus, who had come, <br> The comrade of Phylides, at the head <br> Of the high-souled Epeians. Meges saw, <br> And rushed upon Polydamas, who sprang <br> Aside unharmed, for Phoebus suffered not <br> The son of Panthoüs thus to be o’erthrown, <br> Fighting among the foremost. But the spear <br> Of Meges wounded Croesmus in the breast; <br> He fell with clanging arms. The slayer stripped <br> The corpse; but Dolops, son of Lampus, skilled <br> To wield the spear, leaped on him in the act. <br> Lampus, the father, best of men, was son <br> Of king Laomedon, and eminent <br> For warlike prowess. Dolops struck the shield <br> Of Meges in the midst; the corselet stayed <br> The blade with its close jointed plates, and saved <br> The warrior’s life. That corselet Phyleus brought <br> From Ephyrè, beside the Selleis, <br> Given by his host, Euphetes, king of men, <br> For his defence in battle, and it now <br> Preserved his son from death. Then Meges smote <br> With his sharp spear the helm that Dolops wore, <br> And from its summit struck the horse-hair crest, <br> New-tinged with purple, and the cone entire <br> Fell midst the dust. While Meges, standing firm, <br> Fought thus, and hoped the victory, to his aid <br> Came warlike Menelaus, unobserved, <br> And, standing near, smote Dolops from behind, <br> Beneath the shoulder, and drave through the spear <br> Till it appeared beyond. The Trojan fell <br> Upon his face, and both the Greeks rushed on <br> To wrench the brazen armor from his limbs, <br> When Hector saw his fall and called aloud <br> Upon the kindred of the slain. He first <br> Rebuked the valiant Melanippus, son <br> Of Hicetaon, who but lately fed <br> His slow-paced beeves at Percote, while yet <br> The enemy was far from Troy; but when <br> The Achaians landed from their well-oared barques, <br> He came to Troy, and took an eminent place <br> Among the Trojans. Near to Priam’s halls <br> He had his dwelling, honored equally <br> With Priam’s sons. Him Hector thus rebuked:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Why, Melanippus, are we loitering thus? <br> Grievest thou not to see thy kinsman slain? <br> And see’st thou not how eagerly the Greeks <br> Are spoiling Dolops of his arms? Come on <br> With me. No time is this for distant fight, <br> But either we must rout the Greeks, or they <br> Will level to the ground the lofty towers <br> Of Ilium, and will slay its citizens.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake, and led the way; his godlike friend <br> Followed him, while the son of Telamon, <br> Ajax, exhorted thus the sons of Greece:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Be men, my friends, and let a noble dread <br> Of shame possess your hearts, and jealously <br> Look to each other’s honor in the heat <br> Of battle; for to men who flee there comes <br> No glory, and that way no safety lies.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake, and all were eager to drive back <br> The assaulting foe; they heeded well his words, <br> And drew around their barques a fence of mail, <br> While Jove urged on the Trojans. Then it was <br> That Menelaus, brave in battle, spake <br> To rouse the courage of Antilochus:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Antilochus, there is no other Greek <br> Younger than thou, or fleeter; none so strong <br> For combat. Would that, springing on the foe, <br> Thou mightest strike some Trojan warrior down.”</p>
<p class="verse">So speaking, he drew back; but he had roused <br> The courage of his friend, who, springing forth <br> From midst the foremost combatants, took aim, <br> First looking keenly round, with his bright spear, <br> From which the Trojans shrank as they beheld <br> The hero cast it. Not in vain he threw <br> The weapon, for it struck upon the breast <br> Brave Melanippus, Hicetaon’s son; <br> Beneath the pap it smote him as he came. <br> He fell with ringing arms; Antilochus <br> Sprang toward him like a hound that springs to seize <br> A wounded fawn, which, leaping from its lair, <br> Is stretched disabled by the hunter’s dart. <br> So sprang the stout Antilochus on thee, <br> O Melanippus!⁠—sprang to spoil thy limbs <br> Of armor; but the noble Hector saw, <br> And, hastening through the thick of battle, came <br> Against him. Mighty as he was in war, <br> Yet ventured not Antilochus to wait <br> His coming; but as flees a savage beast, <br> Conscious of guilty deed, when, having slain <br> Herdsman or hound, that kept the pastured kine, <br> He steals away before a crowd of men, <br> So fled the son of Nestor. On his rear <br> The Trojans under Hector poured a storm <br> Of weapons, and the din was terrible. <br> Yet when he reached the serried ranks of Greece <br> He turned and stood. Meanwhile the Trojan host, <br> Like ravening lions, fiercely rushed against <br> The galleys, that the will of Jupiter <br> Might be fulfilled; for now he nerved their limbs <br> With vigor ever new, while he denied <br> Stout hearts and victory to the Greeks, and cheered <br> Their foes with hope. His purpose was to give <br> The victory to Hector, Priam’s son, <br> Till he should cast upon the beaked ships <br> The fierce, devouring fire, and bring to pass <br> The end for which the cruel Thetis prayed.</p>
<p class="verse">Therefore did Jove the All-disposer wait <br> Till from a burning galley he should see <br> The flames arise. Then must the Trojan host⁠— <br> Such was his will⁠—retreating from the fleet, <br> Yield to the Greeks the glory of the day. <br> For this he moved the already eager heart <br> Of Hector, son of Priam, to attack <br> The roomy ships. The hero was aroused <br> To fury fierce as Mars when brandishing <br> His spear, or as a desolating flame <br> That rages on a mountain-side among <br> The thickets of a close-grown wood. His lips <br> Were white with foam; his eyes from underneath <br> His frowning brows streamed fire; and as he fought, <br> Upon the hero’s temples fearfully <br> The helmet nodded. Jupiter himself <br> Sent aid from his high seat, and heaped on him <br> Honor and fame beyond the other chiefs⁠— <br> And they were many⁠—for his term of life <br> Was to be short. Minerva even now <br> Was planning to bring on its closing day, <br> Made fatal by the might of Peleus’ son. <br> And now he strove to break the Grecian ranks, <br> Assaulting where he saw the thickest crowd <br> And the best weapons; yet in vain he strove <br> With all his valor. Through the serried lines <br> He could not break; the Greeks in solid squares <br> Resisted, like a rock that huge and high <br> By the gray deep abides the buffetings <br> Of the shrill winds and swollen waves that beat <br> Against it. Firmly thus the Greeks withstood <br> The Trojan host, and fled not. In a blaze <br> Of armor, Hector, rushing toward their ranks, <br> Fell on them like a mighty billow raised <br> By the strong cloud-born winds, that flings itself <br> On a swift ship, and whelms it in its spray, <br> While fearfully among the cordage howls <br> The blast; the sailors tremble and are faint <br> With fear, as men who deem their death-hour nigh. <br> So the Greek warriors were dismayed at heart.</p>
<p class="verse">As when a hungry lion suddenly <br> Springs on a herd of kine that crop the grass <br> By hundreds in the broad moist meadow-grounds, <br> Beneath the eye of one who never learned <br> To guard his hornèd charge from beasts of prey, <br> But ever walks before them or behind, <br> While the grim spoiler bounds into the midst <br> And makes a prey of one, and all the rest <br> Are scattered in affright, so all the Greeks <br> Were scattered by the will of heaven before <br> Hector and Father Jove. Yet only one, <br> Young Periphoetes of Mycenae, fell, <br> The son of Copreus. Once his father went <br> An envoy from Eurystheus to the court <br> Of mighty Hercules. The son excelled <br> The father in all gifts of form and mind, <br> In speed, in war, in council eminent <br> Among the noblest of his land. His death <br> Brought Hector new renown; for as he turned, <br> Stepping by chance upon his buckler’s rim, <br> That reached the ground⁠—the buckler which had been <br> His fence against the enemy’s darts⁠—he fell <br> Backward, his helmet clashing fearfully <br> Around his temples. Hector saw, and came <br> In haste, and pierced his bosom with his spear, <br> Among his fellow-warriors, who with grief <br> Beheld, yet dared not aid him, such their awe <br> Of noble Hector. Now the Greeks retired <br> Among that row of galleys which were first <br> Drawn up the beach; the foe poured after them, <br> In hot pursuit; again the Greeks fell back, <br> Constrained, and left that foremost row behind, <br> And stood beside their tents in close array, <br> And not dispersed throughout the camp, for shame <br> And fear restrained them, and unceasingly <br> With shouts they bade each other bravely stand. <br> Chiefly Gerenian Nestor, wise to guide <br> The counsels of the Greeks, adjured them all, <br> And in their parents’ name, to keep their ground.</p>
<p class="verse">“O friends, be men; so act that none may feel <br> Ashamed to meet the eyes of other men. <br> Think each one of his children and his wife, <br> His home, his parents, living yet or dead. <br> For them, the absent ones, I supplicate, <br> And bid you rally here, and scorn to fly.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake, and his brave words to every heart <br> Carried new strength and courage. Pallas then <br> Lifted the heaven-sent cloud that veiled the fight, <br> And all things in the clear full light were seen <br> On either side, both where the galleys lay <br> And where the warriors struggled. They beheld <br> Hector the great in war, and all his host, <br> Both those who formed the rear and wielded not <br> Their arms, and those who combated in front <br> Beside the ships. And now it pleased no more <br> The soul of valiant Ajax to remain <br> In the thick squadrons with the other Greeks, <br> But, striding on the galley-decks, he bore <br> A sea-pike two and twenty cubits long, <br> Huge, and beset with iron nails. As when <br> One who is skilled to vault on running steeds <br> Chooses four horses from a numerous herd, <br> And on the highway to a populous town <br> Drives them, while men and women in a crowd <br> Behold his feats with wonder, as he leaps <br> Boldly, without a fall, from steed to steed, <br> And back again, and all the while they run, <br> So on the lofty decks of those good ships <br> From ship to ship flew Ajax, lifting up <br> His mighty voice⁠—a shout that reached to heaven⁠— <br> And bade the Greeks defend their fleet and tents. <br> Nor loitered Hector in those armèd throngs <br> Of Troy, but as a tawny eagle swoops <br> Upon a flock of birds that seek their food <br> Along a river’s border⁠—geese or cranes, <br> Or long-necked swans⁠—so Hector in hot haste <br> Sprang toward a galley with an azure prow, <br> While mightily the power of Jove impelled <br> The hero onward, and inflamed his train <br> With courage. Fiercely then around the ships <br> The struggle was renewed. Thou wouldst have said <br> No toils of war could tire those resolute arms, <br> So stubbornly they fought. In every mind <br> The thought was this: the Greeks were in despair <br> Of rescue, and believed their hour had come <br> To perish; every Trojan hoped to give <br> The fleet to flames, and slay the sons of Greece. <br> With thoughts like these the hostile warriors closed.</p>
<p class="verse">Then Hector laid his hand upon the stern <br> Of a stanch galley, beautiful and swift, <br> In which Protesilaüs came to Troy⁠— <br> It never bore him back. Around its keel <br> The Trojans and the Greeks fought hand to hand, <br> And slew each other. For no more they sent <br> The arrow or the javelin from afar, <br> Waiting to see the wound it gave, but each <br> With equal fury pressed upon his foe <br> With halberd and with trenchant battle-axe, <br> Huge sword and two-edged spear. Upon the ground <br> Had fallen many a fair black-hilted sword <br> With solid handles, some from slain men’s hands, <br> Some from lopped arms of warriors; the dark earth <br> Ran red with blood. But Hector, having laid <br> His hand upon the galley’s stern, held fast <br> To the carved point, and called upon his men:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Bring fire, and press in throngs upon the foe; <br> For now doth Jove vouchsafe to us a day <br> Worth all the past⁠—a day on which we make <br> The ships our prey. Against the will of Heaven <br> They landed on our coast, and brought on us <br> Disasters many, through the coward fears <br> Of our own elders, who denied my wish <br> To combat at the galleys, and held back <br> The people. But if then the Thunderer <br> Darkened our minds, his spirit moves us now <br> In what we do, and we obey his will.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake; and they with fiercer valor fell <br> Upon the Greeks. Even Ajax could no more <br> Withstand the charge, but, fearing to be slain, <br> Amid a storm of darts withdrew a space, <br> To where the seven-foot bench of rowers lay, <br> And left the galley’s stern. There, as he stood, <br> He watched the assailants keenly, and beat back <br> With thrusts of his long spear whoever brought <br> The firebrand. With terrific shouts he called <br> Upon the Greeks to combat manfully:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“O friends, Achaian heroes, ministers <br> Of Mars, be men, be mindful of your fame <br> For valor. Do ye dream that in your rear <br> Are succors waiting us, or firmer walls <br> That may protect us yet? Nay, no fenced town <br> Have we for refuge, flanked with towers from which <br> Fresh troops may take our place. Between the sea <br> And country of the well-armed Trojans lie <br> Our tents; our native land is far away; <br> And now our only hope of safety left <br> Is in our weapons: there is no retreat.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake, and mightily with his sharp spear <br> Thrust at whoever of the men of Troy <br> At Hector’s bidding came with fire to burn <br> The galleys. On the blade of that long spear <br> The hero took them as they came, and slew <br> In close encounter twelve before the fleet.</p>
