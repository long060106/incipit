<p class="lede">Division of the Trojan army, by advice of Polydamas, into five bodies, to storm the Greek entrenchments⁠—A breach in the wall made by Sarpedon⁠—One of the gates beaten open by Hector with a stone⁠—His entrance at the head of his troops.</p>
<p class="verse">Thus in the camp Menoetius’ valiant son <br> Tended Eurypylus, and dressed his wounds; <br> While yet in mingled throngs the warriors fought⁠— <br> Trojans and Greeks. Nor longer was the trench <br> A barrier for the Greeks, nor the broad wall <br> Which they had built above it to defend <br> Their fleet; for all around it they had drawn <br> The trench, yet not with chosen hecatombs <br> Paid to the gods, that so it might protect <br> The galleys and the heaps of spoil they held. <br> Without the favor of the gods it rose, <br> And therefore was not long to stand entire. <br> As long as Hector lived, and Peleus’ son <br> Was angered, and King Priam’s city yet <br> Was not o’erthrown, so long the massive wall <br> Built by the Greeks stood firm. But when at length <br> The bravest of the Trojans had been slain, <br> And many of the Greeks were dead⁠—though still <br> Others survived⁠—and when in the tenth year <br> The city of Priam fell, and in their ships <br> The Greeks went back to their beloved land, <br> Then did Apollo and the god of sea <br> Consult together to destroy the wall <br> By turning on it the resistless might <br> Of rivers, all that from the Idaean heights <br> Flow to the ocean⁠—Rhesus, Granicus, <br> Heptaporus, Caresus, Rhodius, <br> Aesepus, and Scamander’s hallowed stream, <br> And Simoïs, in whose bed lay many shields <br> And helms and bodies of slain demigods. <br> Phoebus Apollo turned the mouths of these <br> All toward one spot; nine days against the wall <br> He bade their currents rush, while Jupiter <br> Poured constant rain, that floods might overwhelm <br> The rampart; and the god who shakes the earth, <br> Wielding his trident, led the rivers on. <br> He flung among the billows the huge beams <br> And stones which, with hard toil, the Greeks had laid <br> For the foundations. Thus he levelled all <br> Beside the hurrying Hellespont, destroyed <br> The bulwarks utterly, and overspread <br> The long broad shore with sand; and then he brought <br> Again the rivers to the ancient beds <br> In which their gently flowing waters ran.</p>
<p class="verse">This yet was to be done in time to come <br> By Neptune and Apollo. Meanwhile raged <br> Battle and tumult round that strong-built wall. <br> The towers in all their timbers rang with blows; <br> And, driven as by the scourge of Jove, the Greeks, <br> Hemmed closely in beside their roomy ships, <br> Trembled at Hector, the great scatterer <br> Of squadrons, fighting, as he did before, <br> With all a whirlwind’s might. As when a boar <br> Or lion mid the hounds and huntsmen stands, <br> Fearfully strong, and fierce of eye, and they <br> In square array assault him, and their hands <br> Fling many a javelin;⁠—yet his noble heart <br> Fears not, nor does he fly, although at last <br> His courage cause his death; and oft he turns, <br> And tries their ranks; and where he makes a rush <br> The ranks give way;⁠—so Hector moved and turned <br> Among the crowd, and bade his followers cross <br> The trench. The swift-paced horses ventured not <br> The leap, but stood upon the edge and neighed <br> Aloud, for the wide space affrighted them; <br> And hard it was to spring across, or pass <br> From side to side, for on each side the brink <br> Was steep, and bristled with sharp stakes, close set <br> And strong, which there the warrior sons of Greece <br> Had planted, a defence against the foe. <br> No steed that whirled the rapid car along <br> Could enter, but the soldiery on foot <br> Eagerly sought to pass, and in these words <br> Polydamas to daring Hector spake:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Hector, and ye who lead the troops of Troy <br> And our auxiliars! Rashly do we seek <br> To urge our rapid steeds across the trench <br> So hard to pass, beset with pointed stakes⁠— <br> And the Greek wall so near. The troops of horse <br> Cannot descend nor combat there: the space <br> Is narrow: they would all be slain. If Jove, <br> The Thunderer of the skies, design to crush <br> The Greeks and succor Troy, I should rejoice <br> Were the design at once fulfilled, and all <br> The sons of Greece ingloriously cut off, <br> Far from their Argos. But if they should turn <br> Upon us, and repulse us from their fleet, <br> And we become entangled in the trench, <br> I deem no messenger would e’er go back <br> To Troy from fighting with the rallied Greeks. <br> Heed, then, my words, and let the charioteers <br> Stay with the coursers at the trench, while we, <br> Armed, and on foot, and all in close array, <br> Follow our Hector. For the Greeks in vain <br> Will strive to stem our onset if, in truth, <br> The hour of their destruction be at hand.”</p>
<p class="verse">So spake Polydamas; and Hector, pleased <br> To hear the prudent counsel, leaped to earth <br> With all his arms, and left his car. The rest <br> Rode with their steeds no more, but, hastily <br> Dismounting, as they saw their noble chief, <br> Each bade his charioteer hold back his steeds, <br> Reined at the trench, in ranks. And then, apart, <br> They mustered in five columns, following close <br> Their leaders. First, the largest, bravest band, <br> Those who, with resolute daring, longed to break <br> The rampart and to storm the fleet, were led <br> By Hector and the good Polydamas, <br> Joined with Cebriones⁠—for Hector left <br> His chariot to the care of one who held <br> An humbler station than Cebriones. <br> Paris, Alcathoüs, and Agenor led <br> A second squadron. Helenus, a son <br> Of Priam, and Deïphobus, a youth <br> Of godlike form, his brother, took command <br> Of yet a third⁠—with whom in rank was joined <br> The hero Asius, son of Hyrtacus, <br> Whose bright-haired coursers, of majestic size, <br> Had borne him from Arisba and the banks <br> Of Selleis. Aeneas led the fourth⁠— <br> The brave son of Anchises; and with him <br> Were joined Archilochus and Acamas, <br> Sons of Antenor, skilled in arts of war. <br> The band of Troy’s illustrious allies <br> Followed Sarpedon, who from all the rest <br> Had chosen, to partake in the command, <br> Glaucus and brave Asteropoeus. These <br> He deemed the bravest under him; yet he <br> Stood foremost of them all in warlike might.</p>
<p class="verse">Then all, with their stout bucklers of bull’s-hide <br> Adjusted to each other, bravely marched <br> Against the Greeks, who, as they deemed, must fly <br> Before them, and must fall by their black ships. <br> Then all the other Trojans, and the allies <br> From foreign shores, obeyed the counsel given <br> By good Polydamas; but Asius, son <br> Of Hyrtacus, and prince of men, chose not <br> To leave his chariot and his charioteer, <br> But drave with them against the roomy ships. <br> Vain youth!⁠—he was not destined to return, <br> Borne by his steeds and chariot, from the fleet, <br> And from the fate he braved, to wind-swept Troy. <br> His evil fate o’ertook him from the spear <br> Of great Idomeneus, Deucalion’s son; <br> For toward the galleys moored upon the left <br> He hastened by the way in which the Greeks, <br> With steeds and cars, retreated from the plain. <br> Thither he drave his coursers; there he found <br> The gates not closed, nor the long bar across, <br> But warriors held them open to receive <br> In safety their companions as they fled <br> From battle to the fleet. Exultingly <br> He turned his coursers thither, and his men <br> Followed him, shouting; for they thought the Greeks <br> Could not abide their onset, but must yield, <br> And perish by their ships. Deluded men!⁠— <br> They met two mighty warriors at the gate⁠— <br> The brave descendants of the Lapithae, <br> That warlike tribe: Pirithoüs’ gallant son <br> Was one, named Polypoetes; with him stood <br> Leonteus, strong as Mars the slayer of men. <br> By the tall gates they stood, as giant oaks <br> Stand on the mountains and abide the wind <br> And the tempestuous rains of all the year, <br> Firm-planted on their strong and spreading roots. <br> So they, confiding in their strength of arm, <br> Waited for mighty Asius hasting on, <br> And fled not. Onward came the hostile troop, <br> With their tough shields uplifted, and with shouts: <br> All rushing toward the massive wall they came, <br> Following King Asius, and Iamenus, <br> Orestes, Thoön, Acamas the son <br> Of Asius, and Oenomaüs. Meanwhile <br> Leonteus and his comrade had retired <br> Within, encouraging the well-armed Greeks <br> To combat for the fleet; but when they saw <br> The rout and panic of their flying host, <br> They darted forth and fought before the gates⁠— <br> Fought like wild boars that in the mountains meet <br> A clamorous troop of men and dogs, and dart <br> Sideway at their assailants, break the trees <br> Close to the root, and fiercely gnash their tusks, <br> Until some javelin strikes them, and they die. <br> So on the breasts of the two warriors rang <br> The shining brass, oft smitten; for they fought <br> Fearlessly, trusting in the aid of those <br> Who held the wall, and their own valiant arms. <br> And they who stood on the strong towers hurled down <br> Stones, to defend the Achaians and their tents <br> And their swift ships. As snow-flakes fall to earth <br> When strong winds, driving on the shadowy cloud, <br> Shower them upon the nourishing glebe, so thick <br> Were showered the weapons from the hands of Greeks <br> And Trojans; and the helms and bossy shields, <br> Beaten by stones, resounded. Asius then⁠— <br> The son of Hyrtacus⁠—in anger groaned, <br> And smote his thighs impatiently, and said:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“O Father Jove! Thou then art wholly false. <br> I did not look to see the men of Greece <br> Stand thus before our might and our strong arms; <br> Yet they, like pliant-bodied wasps or bees, <br> That build their cells beside the rocky way, <br> And quit not their abode, but, waiting there <br> The hunter, combat for their young⁠—so these, <br> Although but two, withdraw not from the gates, <br> Nor will, till they be slain or seized alive.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake; but moved not thus the will of Jove, <br> Who planned to give the glory of the day <br> To Hector. Meanwhile, at the other gates <br> Fought other warriors⁠—but ’twere hard for me, <br> Were I a god, to tell of all their deeds; <br> For round the wall on every side there raged, <br> Fierce as consuming fire, a storm of stones. <br> The Greeks, in bitter anguish, yet constrained, <br> Fought for their fleet; and sorrowful were all <br> The gods who in the battle favored Greece.</p>
<p class="verse">Now the two Lapithae began the fight. <br> Pirithoüs’ son, brave Polypoetes, cast <br> His spear at Damasus; it broke its way <br> Through the helm’s brazen cheek⁠—nor that alone: <br> Right through the temple went the brazen blade, <br> And crushed the brain within. He left him slain, <br> And next struck Pylon down, and Ormenus. <br> Leonteus, of the stock of Mars, assailed <br> Hippomachus, who from Antimachus <br> Derived his birth; he pierced him at the belt, <br> And, drawing forth his trenchant sword, hewed down, <br> In combat hand-to-hand, Antiphates; <br> He dashed him backward to the ground, and next <br> Smote Menon and Iamenus; and last <br> He slew Orestes: at his feet they lay, <br> A pile of dead, upon their mother Earth.</p>
<p class="verse">Then, as the twain were stripping from the dead <br> Their glittering arms, the largest, bravest band <br> Of those who eagerly desired to break <br> The rampart and to burn the ships with fire, <br> Following Polydamas and Hector, stood <br> Consulting at the trench. An augury, <br> Just as they were in act to cross, appeared <br> Upon the left: an eagle high in air, <br> Between the armies, in his talons bore <br> A monstrous serpent, bleeding, yet alive <br> And palpitating⁠—nor disabled yet <br> For combat; for it turned, and on the breast <br> Wounded the eagle, near the neck. The bird <br> In pain let fall his prize amid the host, <br> And flew away, with screams, upon the wind. <br> The Trojans shuddered at the spotted snake <br> Lying among them, and Polydamas <br> Said thus to fearless Hector, standing near:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Hector, thou almost ever chidest me <br> In council, even when I judge aright. <br> I know it ill becomes the citizen <br> To speak against the way that pleases thee, <br> In war or council⁠—he should rather seek <br> To strengthen thy authority; yet now <br> I will declare what seems to me the best: <br> Let us not combat with the Greeks, to take <br> Their fleet; for this, I think, will be the end, <br> If now the omen we have seen be meant <br> For us of Troy who seek to cross the trench; <br> This eagle, flying high upon the left, <br> Between the hosts, that in his talons bore <br> A monstrous serpent, bleeding, yet alive, <br> Hath dropped it mid our host before he came <br> To his dear nest, nor brought it to his brood; <br> So we, although by force we break the gates <br> And rampart, and although the Greeks fall back, <br> Shall not as happily retrace our way; <br> For many a Trojan shall we leave behind, <br> Slain by the weapons of the Greeks, who stand <br> And fight to save their fleet. Thus will the seer, <br> Skilled in the lore of prodigies, explain <br> The portent, and the people will obey.”</p>
<p class="verse">Sternly the crested Hector looked, and spake:⁠— <br> “Polydamas, the thing that thou hast said <br> Pleases me not, and easily couldst thou <br> Frame better counsels. If thy words convey <br> Thy earnest thought, the gods assuredly <br> Have made thee lose thy senses. Thou dost ask <br> That I no longer reverence the decree <br> Of Jove, the Thunderer of the sky, who gave <br> His promise, and confirmed it. Thou dost ask <br> That I be governed by the flight of birds, <br> Which I regard not, whether to the right <br> And toward the morning and the sun they fly, <br> Or toward the left and evening. We should heed <br> The will of mighty Jupiter, who bears <br> Rule over gods and men. One augury <br> There is, the surest and the best⁠—to fight <br> For our own land. Why dreadest thou the war <br> And conflict? Though we all should fall beside <br> The galleys of the Greeks, there is no fear <br> That thou wilt perish, for thou hast no heart <br> To stand against the foe;⁠—no warrior thou! <br> Yet, if thou dare to stand aloof, or seek <br> By words to turn another from the fight, <br> The spear I wield shall take thy life at once.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake, and went before; and all his band <br> Followed with fearful clamor. Jupiter, <br> The God of thunders, sending a strong wind <br> From the Idaean summits, drave the dust <br> Full on the galleys, and made faint the hearts <br> Of the Greek warriors, and gave new renown <br> To Hector and the men of Troy. For these, <br> Trusting in portents sent from Jupiter, <br> And their own valor, labored to break through <br> The massive rampart of the Greeks: they tore <br> The galleries from the towers, and levelled down <br> The breastworks, heaved with levers from their place <br> The jutting buttresses which Argive hands <br> Had firmly planted to support the towers, <br> And brought them to the ground; and thus they hoped <br> To force a passage to the Grecian camp. <br> Not yet did they of Greece give way: they fenced <br> The rampart with their ox-hide shields, and smote <br> The enemy from behind them as he came <br> Under the wall. The chieftains Ajax flew <br> From tower to tower, and cheered the Achaians on, <br> And roused their valor⁠—some with gentle words, <br> And some with harsh rebuke⁠—whome’er they saw <br> Skulk from the toils and dangers of the fight. <br> “O friends!” they said, “ye great in war, and ye <br> Of less renown, and ye of little note!⁠— <br> For all are not alike in war⁠—the time <br> Demands the aid of all, as well ye know: <br> And now let no man turn him toward the fleet <br> Before the threats of Hector, but press on, <br> And each exhort his fellow: so may Jove, <br> Who flings the lightning from Olympus, grant <br> That, driving back their onset, we may chase <br> The enemy to the very walls of Troy.”</p>
<p class="verse">Thus in the van they shouted, and awoke <br> New courage in the Greeks. As when the flakes <br> Of snow fall thick upon a winter-day, <br> When Jove the Sovereign pours them down on men, <br> Like arrows, from above;⁠—he bids the wind <br> Breathe not; continually he pours them down, <br> And covers every mountaintop and peak, <br> And flowery mead, and field of fertile tilth, <br> And sheds them on the havens and the shores <br> Of the gray deep; but there the waters bound <br> The covering of snows⁠—all else is white <br> Beneath that fast-descending shower of Jove;⁠— <br> So thick the shower of stones from either side <br> Flew toward the other⁠—from the Greeks against <br> The Trojans, and from them against the Greeks; <br> And fearful was the din along the wall.</p>
<p class="verse">Yet would illustrious Hector and the men <br> Of Troy have failed to force the gates and burst <br> The bar within, had not all-seeing Jove <br> Impelled his son Sarpedon to attack <br> The Greeks as falls a lion on a herd <br> Of horned beeves. The warrior held his shield, <br> A brazen orb, before him⁠—beautiful, <br> And fenced with metal; for the armorer laid <br> Broad plates without, while under these he sewed <br> Bull’s-hides the toughest, edged with golden wires <br> Upon the rim. With this the warrior came, <br> Wielding two spears. As when a lion, bred <br> Among the mountains, fasting long from flesh, <br> Comes into the fenced pastures, without fear, <br> To prey upon the flock; and though he meet <br> The shepherds keeping watch with dogs and spears, <br> Yet will he not be driven thence until <br> He makes a spring into the fold and bears <br> A sheep away, or in the act is slain, <br> Struck by a javelin from some ready hand;⁠— <br> Sarpedon, godlike warrior, thus was moved <br> By his great heart to storm the wall and break <br> Through the strong barrier; and to Glaucus, son <br> Of Lycia’s king Hippolochus, he said:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Why, Glaucus, are we honored, on the shores <br> Of Lycia, with the highest seat at feasts, <br> And with full cups? Why look men up to us <br> As to the gods? And why do we possess <br> Broad, beautiful enclosures, full of vines <br> And wheat, beside the Xanthus? Then it well <br> Becomes us, foremost in the Lycian ranks <br> To stand against the foe, where’er the fight <br> Is hottest; so our well-armed Lycian men <br> Shall say, and truly: ‘Not ingloriously <br> Our kings bear rule in Lycia, where they feast <br> On fatlings of the flock, and drink choice wine; <br> For they excel in valor, and they fight <br> Among our foremost.’ O my friend, if we, <br> Leaving this war, could flee from age and death, <br> I should not here be fighting in the van, <br> Nor would I send thee to the glorious war <br> But now, since many are the modes of death <br> Impending o’er us, which no man can hope <br> To shun, let us press on and give renown <br> To other men, or win it for ourselves!”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake; and Glaucus not unwillingly <br> Heard and obeyed. Right on the warriors pressed, <br> Leading the Lycian host. Menestheus, son <br> Of Peteus, saw, and trembled; for they came <br> With evil menace toward his tower. He looked <br> Along the Grecian lines in hope to see <br> Some chieftain there whose ready help might save <br> His comrades from their danger. He beheld <br> The rulers Ajax, never tired of war, <br> Standing with Teucer, who just then had left <br> His tent; and yet they could not hear his shout, <br> So fearful was the din that rose to heaven <br> From all the shields, and crested helms, and gates, <br> Smitten with missiles⁠—for at all the gates <br> The Lycians thundered, struggling hard to break <br> A passage through them. Then Menestheus called <br> A herald near, and bade Thoötes bear <br> A message to the leaders Ajax, thus:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Go, nobly born Thoötes, and in haste <br> Call Ajax⁠—call them both, for that were best⁠— <br> Since terrible will be the slaughter here, <br> So fiercely are the Lycians pressing on, <br> Impetuous ever in assault. If there <br> The fight be also urgent, then at least <br> Let the brave Telamonian Ajax come, <br> And Teucer, the great archer, follow him.</p>
<p class="verse">He spake. The herald listened and obeyed, <br> And flew along the summit of the wall <br> Built by the Greeks. He reached, and stood beside, <br> The chieftains Ajax, and addressed them thus:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Ajaces, leaders of the warlike Greeks, <br> The honored son of noble Peteus asks <br> That ye will come, though for a little space, <br> To aid him and to share his warlike toils; <br> For terrible will be the slaughter there, <br> So fiercely are the Lycians pressing on, <br> Impetuous ever in assault. If here <br> The fight be also urgent, then at least <br> Let the brave Telamonian Ajax come. <br> And Teucer, the great archer, follow him.”</p>
<p class="verse">He ended. Ajax, son of Telamon, <br> Hearkened, and to his fellow-warrior said:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Here, where the gallant Lycomedes stands, <br> Ajax! remain, and, cheering on the Greeks, <br> Lead them to combat valiantly. I go <br> To stem the battle there, and when our friends <br> Are succored I will instantly return.”</p>
<p class="verse">So speaking, Ajax, son of Telamon, <br> Departed thence, and with him Teucer, sprung <br> From the same father. With them also went <br> Pandion, carrying Teucer’s crooked bow. <br> They came to brave Menestheus at his tower, <br> And went within the wall and met their friends, <br> Hard-pressed⁠—for gallantly the Lycian chiefs <br> And captains, like a gloomy tempest, rushed <br> Up the tall breastworks; while the Greeks withstood <br> Their onset, and a mighty clamor rose.</p>
<p class="verse">Then Telamonian Ajax smote to death <br> Epicles, great of soul, Sarpedon’s friend: <br> Against that chief he cast a huge, rough stone, <br> That lay high up beside a pinnacle <br> Within the wall. No man with both his hands⁠— <br> Such men as now are⁠—though in prime of youth, <br> Could lift its weight; and yet he wielded it <br> Aloft, and flung it. Through the four-coned helm <br> It crashed, and brake the skull within. Down plunged <br> The Lycian, like a diver, from his place <br> On the high tower, and life forsook his limbs. <br> Then Teucer also wounded with a shaft <br> Glaucus, the brave son of Hippolochus, <br> As he leaped forth to scale the lofty wall⁠— <br> Wounded him where the naked arm was seen, <br> And made him leave the combat. Back he sprang, <br> Hiding amid the crowd, that so the Greeks <br> Might not behold the wounded limb, and scoff. <br> With grief Sarpedon saw his friend withdraw, <br> Yet paused not from the conflict, but took aim <br> At Thestor’s son, Alcmaon, with his spear; <br> Pierced him; and drew the weapon out. The Greek, <br> Following the spear, fell headlong; and his arms, <br> Studded with brass, clashed round him as he fell. <br> Then did Sarpedon seize, with powerful hands, <br> The battlement; he wrenched it, and it came <br> To earth, and laid the rampart’s summit bare, <br> To make a passage for the assailing host. <br> Ajax and Teucer saw, and both took aim <br> Together at Sarpedon: Teucer’s shaft <br> Struck in the midst the buckler’s glittering belt, <br> Just at the bosom; but Jove warded off <br> The death-stroke from his son, lest he should fall <br> Beside the galleys. Ajax, springing, struck <br> The buckler with his spear, and pierced its folds, <br> And checked the eager warrior, who gave way <br> A little, yet retreated not, but turned, <br> Encouraging the godlike Lycians thus:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Where, Lycians, is your fiery valor now? <br> Were I the bravest, it were hard, alone, <br> For me to force a passage to the fleet, <br> Though I have cleared the way. Come on with me! <br> Light is the task when many share the toil.” <br> He spake; and they who reverenced his words <br> Of exhortation drew more closely round <br> Their counsellor and sovereign, while the Greeks <br> Above them made their phalanxes more strong <br> Within the wall⁠—for urgent was the need; <br> Since neither could the gallant Syrians break <br> The barrier of the Greeks, and cut their way <br> Through to the fleet, nor could the warlike Greeks <br> Drive back the Lycians when they once had reached <br> The rampart. As two men upon a field, <br> With measuring-rods in hand, disputing stand <br> Over the common boundary, in small space, <br> Each one contending for the right he claims, <br> So, kept asunder by the breastwork, fought <br> The warriors over it, and fiercely struck <br> The orbèd bull’s-hide shields held up before <br> The breast, and the light targets. Many a one <br> Was smitten when he turned and showed the back <br> Unarmed, and many wounded through the shield. <br> The towers and battlements were steeped in blood <br> Of heroes⁠—Greeks and Trojans. Yet were not <br> The Greeks thus put to flight; but, as the scales <br> Are held by some just woman, who maintains, <br> By spinning wool, her household⁠—carefully <br> She poises both the wool and weights, to make <br> The balance even, that she may provide <br> A pittance for her babes⁠—thus equally <br> Were matched the warring hosts, till Jupiter <br> Conferred the eminent glory of the day <br> On Hector, son of Priam. He it was <br> Who first leaped down into the space within <br> The Grecian wall, and, with far-reaching voice, <br> Thus shouted, calling to the men of Troy:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Rush on, ye knights of Troy! Rush boldly on, <br> And break your passage through the Grecian wall, <br> And hurl consuming flames against their fleet!”</p>
<p class="verse">So spake he, cheering on his men. They heard, <br> And rushed in mighty throngs against the wall, <br> And climbed the battlements, to charge the foe <br> With spears. Then Hector stooped, and seized a stone <br> Which lay before the gate, broad at the base <br> And sharp above, which two, the strongest men⁠— <br> As men are now⁠—could hardly heave from earth <br> Into a wain. With ease he lifted it, <br> Alone, and brandished it: such strength the son <br> Of Saturn gave him, that it seemed but light. <br> As when a shepherd carries home with ease <br> A wether’s fleece⁠—he bears it in one hand, <br> And little is he cumbered with its weight⁠— <br> So Hector bore the lifted stone, to break <br> The beams that strengthened the tall folding-gates. <br> Two bars within, laid crosswise, hold them firm⁠— <br> Both fastened with one bolt. He came and stood <br> Before them; with wide-parted feet he stood, <br> And put forth all his strength, that so his arm <br> Might drive the missile home; and in the midst <br> He smote the folding-gates. The blow tore off <br> The hinges; heavily the great stone fell <br> Within: the portals crashed; nor did the bars <br> Withstand the blow: the shattered beams gave way <br> Before it; and illustrious Hector sprang <br> Into the camp. His look was stern as night; <br> And terribly the brazen armor gleamed <br> That swathed him. With two spears in hand he came, <br> And none except the gods⁠—when once his foot <br> Was on the ground⁠—could stand before his might. <br> His eyes shot fire, and, turning to his men, <br> He bade them mount the wall; and they obeyed: <br> Some o’er the wall, some through the sculptured gate, <br> Poured in. The Achaians to their roomy ships <br> Fled, and a fearful uproar filled the air.</p>
