<p class="lede">Renewal of the fight by Agamemnon⁠—His prowess⁠—Hector warned by Iris not to fight till Agamemnon is wounded⁠—Agamemnon disabled⁠—Hector makes great havoc till checked by Ulysses and Diomed⁠—Diomed wounded by Paris, and rescued by Ajax, who rallies the Greeks⁠—Machaon wounded⁠—Conversations of Nestor and Patroclus.</p>
<p class="verse">Now did the Morning from her couch beside <br> Renowned Tithonus rise, that she might bring <br> The light to gods and men, when Jupiter <br> To the swift galleys of the Grecian host <br> Sent baleful Strife, who bore in hand aloft <br> War’s ensigns. On the huge black ship that brought <br> Ulysses, in the centre of the fleet, <br> She stood, where she might shout to either side⁠— <br> To Telamonian Ajax in his tents <br> And to Achilles, who had ranged their ships <br> At each extreme of the Achaian camp, <br> Relying on their valor and strong arms. <br> Loud was the voice, and terrible, in which <br> She shouted from her station to the Greeks, <br> And into every heart it carried strength <br> And the resolve to combat manfully <br> And never yield. The battle now to them <br> Seemed more to be desired than the return <br> To their dear country in their roomy ships. <br> Atrides called aloud, exhorting them <br> To gird themselves for battle. Then he clad <br> Himself in glittering brass. First to his thighs <br> He bound the beautiful greaves with silver clasps, <br> Then fitted to his chest the breastplate given <br> By Cinyras, a pledge of kind intent;⁠— <br> For, when he heard in Cyprus that the Greeks <br> Were bound for Ilium in their ships, he sent <br> This gift, a homage to the king of men;⁠— <br> Ten were its bars of tawny bronze, and twelve <br> Were gold, and twenty tin; and on each side <br> Were three bronze serpents stretching toward the neck, <br> Curved like the colored bow which Saturn’s son <br> Sets in the clouds, a sign to men. He hung <br> His sword, all glittering with its golden studs, <br> About his shoulders. In a silver sheath <br> It nestled, which was slung on golden rings. <br> And then he took his shield, a mighty orb, <br> And nobly wrought and strong and beautiful, <br> Bound with ten brazen circles. On its disk <br> Were twenty bosses of white tin, and one <br> Of tawny bronze just in the midst, where glared <br> A Gorgon’s-head with angry eyes, round which <br> Were sculptured Fear and Flight. Along its band <br> Of silver twined a serpent wrought in bronze, <br> With three heads springing from one neck and formed <br> Into an orb. Upon his head he placed <br> A helmet rough with studs on every side, <br> And with four bosses, and a horse-hair plume <br> That nodded fearfully on high. He took <br> In hand two massive spears, brass-tipped and sharp, <br> That shone afar and sent their light to heaven, <br> Where Juno and Minerva made a sound <br> Like thunder in mid-sky, as honoring <br> The sovereign of Mycenae rich in gold.</p>
<p class="verse">Each chief gave orders to his charioteer <br> To stay his horses firmly by the trench, <br> While they rushed forth in arms. At once arose, <br> Ere yet the sun was up, a mighty din. <br> They marshalled by the trench the men on foot; <br> The horse came after, with short space between. <br> The son of Saturn sent among their ranks <br> Confusion, and dropped down upon the host <br> Dews tinged with blood, in sign that he that day <br> Would send to Hades many a valiant chief.</p>
<p class="verse">The Trojans, on their side, in the mid-plain <br> Drew up their squadrons on a hill, around <br> The mighty Hector, and Polydamas <br> The blameless, and Aeneas, who among <br> The sons of Troy was honored like a god, <br> And three sons of Antenor, who were named <br> Agenor and the noble Polybus <br> And the young Acamas of godlike bloom, <br> There Hector in the van uplifted bore <br> His broad round shield. As some portentous star <br> Breaks from the clouds and shines, and then again <br> Enters their shadow, Hector thus appeared <br> Among the foremost, issuing his commands, <br> Then sought the hindmost. All in brass, he shone <br> Like lightnings of the Aegis-bearer, Jove.</p>
<p class="verse">As when two lines of reapers, face to face, <br> In some rich landlord’s field of barley or wheat <br> Move on, and fast the severed handfuls fall, <br> So, springing on each other, they of Troy <br> And they of Argos smote each other down, <br> And neither thought of ignominious flight. <br> They met each other man to man; they rushed <br> Like wolves to combat. Cruel Strife looked on <br> Rejoicing; she alone of all the gods <br> Was present in the battle; all the rest, <br> Far off, sat quiet in their palaces, <br> The glorious mansions built for them along <br> The summits of Olympus. Yet they all <br> Blamed Saturn’s son that he should honor thus <br> The Trojans. The All-Father heeded not <br> Their murmurings, but, seated by himself <br> Apart, exulting in his sovereignty, <br> Looked on the city of Troy, the ships of Greece, <br> The gleam of arms, the slayers, and the slain.</p>
<p class="verse">While yet ’twas morn, and still the holy light <br> Of day was brightening, fast the weapons smote <br> On either side, and fast the people fell; <br> But at the hour when on the mountain-slope <br> The wood cutter makes ready his repast, <br> Weary with felling lofty trees, and glad <br> To rest, and eager for the grateful meal, <br> The Greeks, encouraging each other, charged <br> And broke the serried phalanxes of Troy. <br> First Agamemnon, springing forward, slew <br> The shepherd of his people and their chief, <br> Bienor, and his trusty comrade next⁠— <br> The charioteer Oileus, who had leaped <br> Down from his chariot to confront the king. <br> Him Agamemnon with his trenchant spear <br> Smote in the forehead as he came. The helm <br> Of massive brass was vain to stay the blow: <br> The weapon pierced it and the bone, and stained <br> The brain with blood; it felled him rushing on. <br> The monarch stripped the slain, and, leaving them <br> With their white bosoms bare, went on to slay <br> Isus and Antiphus, King Priam’s sons⁠— <br> One born in wedlock, one of baser birth⁠— <br> Both in one chariot. Isus held the reins <br> While Antiphus, the high-born brother, fought. <br> These had Achilles once on Ida’s height <br> Made prisoners, as they fed their flocks; he bound <br> Their limbs with osier bands, but gave them up <br> For ransom to the Trojans. Now the king <br> Of men, Atrides Agamemnon, pierced <br> Isus above the nipple with his spear, <br> And with his falchion smiting Antiphus <br> Beside the ear, he hurled him from his car. <br> Then hastening up, and stripping from the dead <br> Their shining mail, he knew them; he had seen <br> Both at the ships to which the fleet of foot, <br> Achilles, brought them bound from Ida’s side. <br> As when a lion comes upon the haunt <br> Of a swift hind, to make an easy prey <br> Of her young fawns, and, with his powerful teeth <br> Seizing them, takes their tender lives; while she, <br> Though nigh, can bring no aid but yields herself <br> To mortal fear, and, to escape his rage, <br> Flies swiftly through the wood of close-grown oaks, <br> With sweaty sides⁠—thus none of all the host <br> Of Trojans could avert from Priam’s sons <br> Their fate, but fled in terror from the Greeks. <br> Next on Pisander and Hippolochus <br> Atrides rushed⁠—brave warriors both, and sons <br> Of brave Antimachus, the chief who took <br> Gold and rich gifts from Paris, and refused <br> To let the Trojans render Helen back <br> To fair-haired Menelaus. His two sons, <br> Both in one car, and reining their fleet steeds, <br> Atrides intercepted; they let fall <br> The embroidered reins, dismayed, as, lion-like, <br> Forward he came; and, cowering, thus they prayed:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Take us alive, Atrides, and accept <br> A worthy ransom, for Antimachus <br> Keeps in his halls large treasures⁠—brass and gold. <br> And well-wrought steel; and he will send, from these, <br> Large ransom, hearing we are at the fleet <br> Alive.” So prayed they with bland words, and met <br> Harsh answer: “Since ye call Antimachus <br> Your father, who in Trojan council once <br> Proposed that Menelaus, whom we sent <br> A legate with Ulysses the divine, <br> Should not return to Greece, but suffer death, <br> Your blood must answer for your father’s guilt.”</p>
<p class="verse">So spake the king, and, striking with his spear <br> Pisander’s breast, he dashed him from the car. <br> Prone on the ground he lay. Hippolochus <br> Leaped down and met the sword. Atrides lopped <br> His hands and drave the weapon through his neck, <br> And sent the head to roll among the crowd. <br> And then he left the dead, and rushed to where <br> The ranks were in disorder; with him went <br> His-well-armed Greeks; there they who fought on foot <br> Slaughtered the flying foot; the horsemen there <br> Clove horsemen down: the coursers’ trampling feet <br> Raised the thick dust to shadow all the plain; <br> While Agamemnon cheered the Achaians on, <br> And chased and slew the foe. As when a fire <br> Seizes a thick-grown forest, and the wind <br> Drives it along in eddies, while the trunks <br> Fall with the boughs amid devouring flames, <br> So fell the flying Trojans by the hand <br> Of Agamemnon. Many high-maned steeds <br> Dragged noisily their empty cars among <br> The ranks of battle, never more to bear <br> Their charioteers, who lay upon the earth <br> The vulture’s feast, a sorrow to their wives.</p>
<p class="verse">But Jove beyond the encountering arms, the dust, <br> The carnage, and the bloodshed and the din <br> Bore Hector, while Atrides in pursuit <br> Was loudly cheering the Achaians on. <br> Meantime the Trojans fled across the plain <br> Toward the wild fig-tree growing near the tomb <br> Of ancient Ilus, son of Dardanus⁠— <br> Eager to reach the town; and still the son <br> Of Atreus followed, shouting, and with hands <br> Blood-stained and dust-begrimed. And when they reached <br> The Scaean portals and the beechen tree, <br> They halted, waiting for the rear, like beeves <br> Chased panting by a lion who has come <br> At midnight on them, and has put the herd <br> To flight, and one of them to certain death⁠— <br> Whose neck he breaks with his strong teeth and then <br> Devours the entrails, lapping up the blood. <br> Thus did Atrides Agamemnon chase <br> The Trojans; still he slew the hindmost; still <br> They fled before him. Many by his hand <br> Fell from their chariots prone, for terrible <br> Beyond all others with the spear was he. <br> But when he now was near the city-wall, <br> The Father of immortals and of men <br> Came down from the high heaven, and took his seat <br> On many-fountained Ida. In his grasp <br> He held a thunderbolt, and this command <br> He gave to Iris of the golden wings:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Haste, Iris fleet of wing, and bear my words <br> To Hector:⁠—While he sees the king of men, <br> Atrides, in the van and dealing death <br> Among the ranks of warriors, let him still <br> Give way, encouraging his men to hold <br> Unflinching battle with the enemy. <br> But when Atrides, wounded by a spear <br> Or arrow, shall ascend his chariot, then <br> Will I nerve Hector’s arm with strength to slay <br> Until he come to the good ships of Greece, <br> And the sun set, and hallowed night come down.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake; and she, whose feet are like the wind <br> In swiftness, heeded the command, and flew <br> From Ida’s summit to the sacred town <br> Of Troy, and found the noble Hector, son <br> Of warlike Priam, standing mid the steeds <br> And the strong chariots, and, approaching, said:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“O Hector, son of Priam, and like Jove <br> In council! Jove the All-Father bids me say, <br> As long as thou shalt see the king of men, <br> Atrides, in the van, and dealing death <br> Among the ranks of warriors, thou shalt still <br> Give way, encouraging thy men to hold <br> Unflinching battle with the enemy; <br> But when Atrides, wounded by a spear <br> Or arrow, shall ascend his chariot, then <br> Will Jove endue thy arm with strength to slay <br> Until thou come to the good ships of Greece, <br> And the sun set, and hallowed night come down.”</p>
<p class="verse">So the fleet Iris spake, and went her way; <br> While Hector, leaping from his car in arms, <br> And wielding his sharp spears, went everywhere <br> Among the Trojan ranks, exhorting them <br> To combat, and renewed the stubborn fight. <br> They rallied and stood firm against the Greeks. <br> The Greeks, in turn, made strong their phalanxes. <br> The battle raged again, as front to front <br> They stood, while Agamemnon eagerly <br> Pressed forward, proud to lead the van in fight.</p>
<p class="verse">Say, Muses, dwellers of Olympus! Who <br> First of the Trojans or their brave allies <br> Encountered Atreus’ son? Iphidamas, <br> Son of Antenor, strong and daring, bred <br> On the rich soil of Thrace, the nurse of flocks. <br> His grandsire Cisseus, from whose loins the fair <br> Theano sprang, had reared him from a child <br> Within his palace; and, when he attained <br> Youth’s glorious prime, still kept him, giving him <br> His child to wife. He wedded her, but left <br> At once the bridal chamber when he heard <br> Of the Greek war on Ilium, and set sail <br> With twelve beaked galleys. These he afterward <br> Left at Percope⁠—marching on to Troy. <br> And he it was who came to meet the son <br> Of Atreus. As the heroes now drew near <br> Each other, Agamemnon missed his aim; <br> His thrust was parried. Then Iphidamas <br> Dealt him beneath the breastplate on the belt <br> A vigorous blow, and urged the spear with all <br> His strength of arm; yet through the plated belt <br> It could not pierce, for there it met a plate <br> Of silver, and its point was turned like lead. <br> With lion strength, Atrides seized and drew <br> The weapon toward him, plucked it from the hand <br> That held it, and let fall his falchion’s edge <br> Upon the Trojan’s neck and laid him dead. <br> Unhappy youth! He slept an iron sleep⁠— <br> Slain fighting for his country, far away <br> From the young virgin bride yet scarcely his, <br> For whom large marriage-gifts he made⁠—of beeves <br> A hundred⁠—and had promised from the flocks <br> That thronged his fields a thousand sheep and goats. <br> Atrides Agamemnon spoiled the slain, <br> And bore his glorious armor off among <br> The Argive host. Antenor’s elder son, <br> Illustrious Coön, saw, and bitter grief <br> For his slain brother dimmed his eyes. He stood <br> Aside, with his spear couched, while unaware <br> The noble Agamemnon passed, and pierced <br> The middle of the monarch’s arm below <br> The elbow; through the flesh the shining point <br> Passed to the other side. The king of men, <br> Atrides, shuddered, yet refrained not then <br> From combat; but with his wind-seasoned spear <br> He rushed on Coön, who, to drag away <br> His father’s son Iphidamas, had seized <br> The body by the feet, and called his friends, <br> The bravest, to his aid. Atrides thrust <br> His brazen spear below the bossy shield, <br> And slew him as he drew the corpse, and o’er <br> The dead Iphidamas struck off his head. <br> Thus were Antenor’s sons⁠—their doom fulfilled⁠— <br> Sent by Atrides to the realm of death. <br> And then he ranged among the enemy’s ranks <br> With wielded lance and sword and ponderous stones, <br> While yet the warm blood issued from his wound. <br> But when the wound grew dry, and ceased to flow <br> With blood, keen anguish seized his vigorous frame <br> As when a woman feels the piercing pangs <br> Of travail brought her by the Ilythian maids, <br> Daughters of Juno, who preside at births, <br> And walk the ministers of bitter pains⁠— <br> Such anguish seized on Agamemnon’s frame; <br> And, leaping to his chariot-seat, he bade <br> The guider of the steeds make haste to reach <br> The roomy ships, for he was overcome <br> With pain; but first he shouted to the Greeks:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“O friends, the chiefs and princes of the Greeks! <br> Yours is the duty to drive back the war <br> From our good ships, since all-disposing Jove <br> Forbids me, for this day, to lead the fight.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake. The charioteer applied the lash, <br> And not unwillingly the long-maned steeds <br> Flew toward the hollow ships; upon their breasts <br> Gathered the foam; beneath their rapid feet <br> Arose the dust, as from the battle’s din <br> They bore the wounded warrior. Hector saw <br> The flight of Agamemnon, and aloud <br> Called to the Trojans and the Lycians thus:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Trojan and Lycian warriors, and ye sons <br> Of Dardanus, who combat hand to hand, <br> Be men; be mindful of your fame in war. <br> Our mightiest foe withdraws; Saturnian Jove <br> Crowns me with glory. Urge your firm-paced steeds <br> On the brave Greeks, and win yet nobler fame.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake. His words gave courage and new strength <br> To every heart. As when a hunter cheers <br> His white-toothed dogs against some lioness <br> Or wild boar from the forest, Hector thus, <br> The son of Priam, terrible as Mars <br> The slayer of men, cheered on the gallant sons <br> Of Troy against the Greeks. Himself, inspired <br> With fiery valor, rushed among the foes <br> In the mid-battle foremost, like a storm <br> That swoops from heaven, and on the dark-blue sea <br> Falls suddenly, and stirs it to its depths.</p>
<p class="verse">Who then was slain the first, and who the last, <br> By Hector, Priam’s son, whom Jove designed <br> To honor? First, Asaeus; Uolops, son <br> Of Clytis; and Autonoüs; and then <br> Opites and Opheltius; next to whom <br> Aesymnus, Agelaus, Orus fell, <br> And resolute Hipponous the last. <br> All these, the princes of the Greeks, he slew, <br> Then smote the common crowd. As when a gale <br> Blows from the west upon the mass of cloud <br> Piled up before the south-wind’s powerful breath, <br> And tears it with a mighty hurricane, <br> While the swol’n billows tumble, and their foam <br> Is flung on high before the furious blast, <br> So by the sword of Hector fell the heads <br> Of the Greek soldiery; and there had been <br> Ruin and ravage not to be repaired, <br> And the defeated Greeks had flung themselves <br> Into their ships, had not Ulysses then <br> Exhorted thus Tydides Diomed:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Tydides! What has quenched within our hearts <br> Their fiery valor? Come, my friend, and take <br> Thy stand beside me: foul disgrace were ours <br> Should crested Hector make our fleet his prize.”</p>
<p class="verse">And thus the valiant Diomed replied:⁠— <br> “Most willingly I stand, and bear my part <br> In battle; but with little hope, for Jove, <br> The God of storms, awards the day to Troy.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake, and pierced Thymbraeus with his spear <br> Through the left breast, and dashed him from his car. <br> Meanwhile Ulysses struck Molion down, <br> The prince’s stately comrade. These they left <br> Never to fight again, and made their way <br> Through the thick squadrons, carrying, as they went, <br> Confusion with them. As two fearless boars <br> Rush on the hounds, so, mingling in the war, <br> They bore the foe before them, and the Greeks <br> Welcomed a respite from the havoc made <br> By noble Hector. Next they seized a car <br> Which bore two chiefs, the bravest of their host⁠— <br> Sons of Percosian Merops, who was skilled <br> Beyond all men in portents. He enjoined <br> His sons to keep aloof from murderous war. <br> Yet did they not obey him, for the fate <br> That doomed the twain to death impelled them on; <br> And Diomed, the mighty with the spear, <br> Spoiled them of life, and bore their armor off, <br> A glittering prize. Meantime Ulysses slew <br> Hippodamus, and next Hypirochus. <br> The son of Saturn looked from Ida’s height, <br> And bade the battle rage on either side <br> With equal fury: both the encountering hosts <br> Slew and were slain. Tydides with his spear <br> Smote on the hip the chief Agastrophus, <br> The son of Paeon, thoughtless wretch, whose steeds <br> Were not at hand for flight; his charioteer <br> Held them at distance, while their master rushed <br> Among the foremost warriors till he fell. <br> Hector perceived his fall, as through the files <br> He looked, and straightway hastened to the spot <br> With shouts; and after him came rapidly <br> The phalanxes of Trojans. Diomed, <br> The great in battle, shuddered as he saw, <br> And thus addressed Ulysses, who was near:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Lo! The destroyer, furious Hector, comes! <br> Let us stand firm, and face and drive him back.”</p>
<p class="verse">He said, and cast his brandished lance, nor missed <br> The mark: it smote the helm on Hector’s head. <br> The brass glanced from the brass; it could not pierce <br> To the fair skin; the high and threefold helm⁠— <br> A gift from Phoebus⁠—turned the point aside. <br> The chief fell back, and, mingling with the throng, <br> Dropped on one knee, and yet upheld himself <br> With one broad palm upon the ground, while night <br> Darkened his eyes. The son of Tydeus sprang <br> To seize his spear, which now stood fixed in earth <br> Among the foremost warriors. In that time <br> Did Hector breathe again, and, having leaped <br> Into his chariot, he avoided death, <br> By mingling with the crowd; while, spear in hand, <br> Brave Diomed pursued him, shouting thus:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“This time, thou cur, hast thou escaped thy doom, <br> Though it was nigh thee. Phoebus rescues thee⁠— <br> The god to whom thou dost address thy prayers⁠— <br> Whene’er thou venturest mid the clash of spears. <br> Yet will I surely slay thee when we meet, <br> If any god be on my side; and now <br> I go to strike where’er I find a foe.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake, and struck the son of Paeon down, <br> Skilful to wield the spear. But now the spouse <br> Of fair-haired Helen⁠—Alexander⁠—stood <br> Leaning against a pillar by the tomb <br> Of the Dardanian Ilus, who had been <br> An elder of the people; and he bent <br> His bow against the monarch Diomed, <br> Who at that moment knelt to strip the slain <br> Of the rich breastplate, and the shield that hung <br> Upon his shoulders, and the massive casque. <br> The Trojan drew the bow’s elastic horn, <br> And sent an arrow that not vainly flew, <br> But, striking the right foot, pierced through, and reached <br> The ground beneath. Then Paris, with a laugh, <br> Sprang from his ambush, shouting boastfully:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Lo, thou art smitten! Not in vain my shaft <br> Has flown; and would that it had pierced thy groin <br> And slain thee! Then the Trojans had obtained <br> Reprieve from slaughter⁠—they who dread thee now <br> As bleating goats a lion.” Undismayed, <br> The valiant Diomed made answer thus:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Archer and railer! Proud of thy smart bow, <br> And ogler of the women! Wouldst thou make <br> Trial of valor hand to hand with me, <br> Thy bow should not avail thee, nor thy sheaf <br> Of many arrows. Thou dost idly boast <br> That thou hast hit my foot. I heed it not. <br> It is as if a woman or a child <br> Had struck me. Lightly falls the weapon-stroke <br> Of an unwarlike weakling. ’Tis not so <br> With me, for when one feels my weapon’s touch, <br> It passes through him, and he dies; his wife <br> Tears with her hands her cheeks; his little ones <br> Are orphans; earth is crimsoned with his blood; <br> And flocking round his carcass in decay, <br> More numerous than women, are the birds.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake. Ulysses, mighty with the spear, <br> Came near and stood before him while he sat <br> Concealed, and drew the arrow from his foot. <br> Keen was the agony that suddenly <br> Shot through his frame: he leaped into his car, <br> And bade his charioteer make haste to reach <br> The roomy ships: the pain had reached his heart. <br> Ulysses, the great spearman, now was left <br> Alone, no Greek remaining by his side; <br> For fear had seized them all. With inward grief <br> The hero thus addressed his mighty soul:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“What will become of me? A great disgrace <br> Will overtake me if I flee in fear <br> Before this multitude; and worse will be <br> My fate if I am taken here alone, <br> While Jove has driven away the other Greeks <br> In terror. Why these questions, since I know <br> That cowards skulk from combat, while the brave, <br> Wounded or wounding others, keeps his ground?”</p>
<p class="verse">While thus he reasoned with himself, the ranks <br> Of Trojans armed with bucklers came and closed <br> Around their dreaded enemy. As when <br> A troop of vigorous dogs and youths assail <br> From every side a wild boar issuing forth <br> From a deep thicket, whetting the white tusks <br> Within his crooked jaws; they press around, <br> And hear his gnashings, yet beware to come <br> Too nigh the terrible animal⁠—so rushed <br> The Trojans round Ulysses, the beloved <br> Of Jupiter. Then first the hero smote <br> Deïopites on the shoulder-blade, <br> And next struck Thoön down, and Ennomus, <br> And in the navel pierced Chersidamas <br> With his sharp spear, below the bossy shield, <br> When leaping from his chariot. In the dust <br> He fell, and grasped the earth with dying hands. <br> Ulysses left them there, and with his spear <br> He wounded Charops, son of Hippasus, <br> And brother of brave Socus. Socus saw, <br> And hastened to his aid, and, standing near, <br> The godlike chief bespake Ulysses thus:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Renowned Ulysses! of whose arts and toils <br> There is no end, thou either shalt today <br> Boast to have slain two sons of Hippasus, <br> Brave as they are, and stripped them of their arms, <br> Or, smitten by my javelin, lose thy life.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake, and smote the Grecian’s orbèd shield. <br> The swift spear, passing through the shining disk, <br> And fixed in the rich breastplate, tore the skin <br> From all his side; yet Pallas suffered not <br> The blade to reach the inner parts. At once <br> The chief perceived that Socus had not given <br> A mortal wound, and, falling back a step, <br> Thus spake: “Unhappy youth, thy doom will soon <br> O’ertake thee. Though thou forcest me to pause <br> From combat with the Trojans, I declare, <br> This day thou sufferest the black doom of death. <br> Thou, smitten by my spear, shalt bring to me <br> Increase of glory, and shalt yield thy soul <br> To the grim horseman Pluto.” Thus he spake, <br> While Socus turned to flee; and as he turned, <br> Ulysses with the spear transfixed his back, <br> And drave the weapon through his breast: he fell, <br> With armor clashing, to the earth, while thus <br> The great Ulysses gloried over him:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“O Socus! son of warlike Hippasus <br> The horseman! Death has overtaken thee, <br> And thou couldst not escape. Unhappy one! <br> Now thou art dead thy father will not come <br> To close thy eyes, nor she, the honored one <br> Who gave thee birth; but birds of prey shall flap <br> Their heavy wings above thee, and shall tear <br> Thy flesh, while I in dying shall receive <br> Due funeral honors from the noble Greeks.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake, and from his wounded side drew forth, <br> And from his bossy shield, the ponderous spear <br> Which warlike Socus threw. A gush of blood <br> Followed, and torturing pain. Now, when they saw <br> Ulysses bleed, the gallant sons of Troy <br> Called to each other, rushing in a crowd <br> To where he stood. Retreating as they came, <br> He shouted to his comrades. Thrice he raised <br> His voice as loud as human lungs could shout; <br> Thrice warlike Menelaus heard the cry, <br> And spake at once to Ajax at his side:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Most noble Ajax, son of Telamon, <br> Prince of thy people! To my ear is brought <br> The cry of that unconquerable man, <br> Ulysses, seemingly as if the foe <br> Had hemmed him round alone, and pressed him sore <br> In combat. Break we through the crowd, and bring <br> Succor, lest harm befall him, though so brave⁠— <br> Fighting among the Trojans thus alone⁠— <br> And lest the Greeks should lose their mighty chief.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake, and led the way; his godlike friend <br> Followed. They found Ulysses, dear to Jove⁠— <br> The Trojans thronging round him like a troop <br> Of ravening jackals round an antlered stag <br> Which one who hunts upon the mountain-side <br> Hath stricken with an arrow from his bow: <br> By flight the stag escapes, while yet the blood <br> Is warm and easily the limbs are moved; <br> But when at last the shaft hath quelled his strength, <br> The hungry jackals in the forest shade <br> Among the hills attack him, till by chance <br> The dreaded lion comes; alarmed, they flee, <br> And he devours the prey. So in that hour, <br> Many and brave, the sons of Troy pursued <br> Ulysses, skilled in war and wiles; while he <br> Wielded the spear and warded off the day <br> Of death. Then Ajax, coming near him, stood, <br> With his tall buckler, like a tower of strength <br> Beside him, and the Trojans fled in fear <br> On all sides. Warlike Menelaus took <br> Ulysses by the hand, and led him forth <br> From the thronged spot, while his attendant brought <br> The chariot near him. Ajax sprang upon <br> The Trojans, slaying Doryclus, a son <br> Of Priam basely born. Then Pandorus <br> He wounded; next he struck Lysander down, <br> Pyrasus and Pylartes. As a stream, <br> Swol’n to a torrent by the showers of Jove <br> Sweeps down, from hill to plain, dry oaks and pines, <br> And pours into the sea a muddy flood, <br> So mighty Ajax routed and pursued <br> The Trojans o’er the plain, and cut his way <br> Through steeds and warriors. Hector knew not this. <br> He fought where, on the battle’s left, beside <br> The Xanthus, fastest fell the slain, and round <br> Great Nestor and the brave Idomeneus <br> Arose a mighty tumult. In that throng <br> Did Hector mingle with his spear and steeds, <br> Performing feats of valor, and laid waste <br> The ranks of youthful warriors. Yet the Greeks <br> Would not have yielded ground, if Paris, spouse <br> Of fair-haired Helen, had not forced the chief <br> Machaon, fighting gallantly, to pause; <br> For with an arrow triple-barbed he pierced <br> The chiefs right shoulder, and the valiant Greeks <br> Feared lest the battle turn and he be slain. <br> And thus Idomeneus to Nestor said:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Neleian Nestor, glory of the Greeks, <br> Haste, mount thy chariot; let Machaon take <br> A place beside thee; urge thy firm-paced steeds <br> Rapidly toward the fleet; a leech like him, <br> Who cuts the arrow from the wound and soothes <br> The pain with balms, is worth a host to us.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake; and the Gerenian knight obeyed, <br> And climbed the car in haste. Machaon, son <br> Of Aesculapius the peerless leech, <br> Mounted beside him; Nestor lashed the steeds, <br> And toward the roomy ships, which well they knew, <br> And longed to reach, they flew with eager speed.</p>
<p class="verse">Meantime Cebriones, who had his seat <br> By Hector in the chariot, saw the ranks <br> Of Troy disordered, and addressed the chief:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“While we, O Hector, here are mid the Greeks <br> Just in the skirts of the tumultuous fray, <br> The other Trojans, men and steeds, are thrown <br> Into confusion where the warriors throng, <br> For Telamonian Ajax puts their ranks <br> To rout; I know him well by that broad shield <br> Borne on his shoulders. Thither let us drive <br> Our steeds and chariot, where in desperate strife <br> Meet horse and foot and hew each other down, <br> And a perpetual clamor fills the air.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake; and with the whistling lash he struck <br> The long-maned steeds, and, as they felt the stroke, <br> Forward they flew with the swift car among <br> The Greeks and Trojans, trampling in their way <br> Corpses and shields. The axle underneath <br> Was steeped in blood; the rim of the chariot-seat <br> Was foul with the red drops which from their hoofs <br> The coursers sprinkled and the wheels threw up. <br> Then Hector strove, by rushing on the crowd, <br> To pierce it and break through it. To the Greeks <br> His coming brought destruction and dismay; <br> And well his spear was wielded. Through the ranks <br> Of other warriors with the spear he ranged, <br> With sword and ponderous stones; yet warily <br> He shunned the fight with Ajax Telamon.</p>
<p class="verse">Then Father Jove Almighty touched with fear <br> The heart of Ajax. All amazed he stood, <br> And cast his sevenfold buckler of bull’s-hide <br> Upon his back, and, terrified, withdrew. <br> Now casting glances like a beast of prey <br> From side to side, he turned to right and left, <br> And, slowly yielding, moved knee after knee. <br> As when the rustics with their hounds drive off <br> A hungry lion from their stalls of kine, <br> Whom, watching all the night, they suffer not <br> To make their herd a prey; but he, intent <br> On ravin, rushes forward, yet in vain; <br> For many a javelin flies from daring hands <br> Against him, many a blazing torch is swung, <br> At which, though fierce, he trembles, and at morn <br> Stalks off in sullen mood;⁠—so Ajax, sad <br> At heart, and fearing for the Grecian fleet, <br> Unwillingly fell back before the foe. <br> And as, when entering in a field, an ass <br> Slow-paced, whose flanks have broken many a shaft <br> To splinters, crops the harvest as it grows, <br> And boys attack him with their rods⁠—though small <br> Their strength⁠—but scarce, till he has browsed his fill, <br> Can drive him forth⁠—so did the gallant sons <br> Of Troy, and their allies from distant lands, <br> Continually pursue the mighty son <br> Of Telamon, and hurl their spears against <br> The centre of his shield. And now he wheeled, <br> As conscious of great valor, and repulsed <br> The crowding phalanxes; and now again <br> He turned to flee. And thus he kept the foe <br> From reaching the swift galleys, while he stood <br> Between the Greeks and Trojans, terrible <br> In wrath. The javelins hurled by daring hands <br> Against him⁠—some hung fixed in his broad shield; <br> And many, ere they came to his fair skin, <br> Fell midway⁠—eager though they were to pierce <br> The warrior’s side⁠—and plunged into the earth.</p>
<p class="verse">Eurypylus, Evaemon’s noble son, <br> Saw Ajax sorely pressed with many darts, <br> And came and stood beside him, taking aim <br> With his bright spear, and in the liver smote, <br> Beneath the midriff, Apisaon, son <br> Of Phausias, and a prince among his tribe. <br> His knees gave way, and down he sank in death. <br> But godlike Alexander, who beheld <br> The slayer stripping Apisaon’s corpse <br> Of armor, at that moment bent his bow, <br> And pierced Eurypylus in the right thigh. <br> The reed brake in the wound. He writhed with pain, <br> And mingled with his fellows in the ranks, <br> Avoiding death, yet shouting to the Greeks:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“O friends, the chiefs and leaders of the Greeks, <br> Rally and keep your ground; ward off the fate <br> Of death from Ajax, who is sorely pressed <br> With darts, and, much I fear, may not escape <br> Safe from this stormy conflict. Stand ye firm <br> Around the mighty son of Telamon.”</p>
<p class="verse">So spake the wounded warrior; while his friends <br> Rallied around him, with their shields inclined <br> Against their shoulders, and with lifted spears. <br> And Ajax came and joined them; then he turned, <br> And firmly faced the foe. The Greeks renewed <br> The combat with a rage like that of fire.</p>
<p class="verse">Now meantime the Neleian coursers, steeped <br> In sweat, were bearing Nestor and the prince <br> Machaon from the battle. On the prow <br> Of his great ship, Achilles, swift of foot, <br> Looked forth, and, gazing on the hard-fought fray <br> And the sad rout, beheld them. Then he called <br> His friend Patroclus, shouting from the ship. <br> Patroclus heard, within the tent, and came, <br> Glorious as Mars;⁠—yet with that day began <br> His woes. The gallant Menoetiades <br> Made answer thus: “Why callest thou my name, <br> Achilles, and what needest thou of me?”</p>
<p class="verse">And thus rejoined Achilles, swift of foot:⁠— <br> “Son of Menoetius, nobly born, and well <br> Beloved by me, the Greeks, I deem, will soon <br> Be at my knees, imploring aid; for now <br> A hard necessity besets their host. <br> But go, Patroclus, dear to Jove, and ask <br> Of Nestor who it is that he hath brought <br> Thus wounded from the field. Seen from behind, <br> His form was like Machaon⁠—wholly like <br> That son of Aesculapius; but the face <br> I saw not, as the rapid steeds flew by.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake. Patroclus hearkened to his friend, <br> And hastened to the Grecian tents and ships.</p>
<p class="verse">Now when they reached the tent of Neleus’ son, <br> The warriors in the chariot set their feet <br> Upon the nourishing earth. Eurymedon, <br> The old man’s charioteer, took from the mares <br> Their harness; while the chieftains cooled themselves, <br> And dried their sweaty garments in the breeze, <br> Facing the border of the sea, and then, <br> Entering the tent of Nestor, sat them down <br> On couches. Hecamede, bright of hair, <br> Prepared for them a mingled draught; the maid, <br> A daughter of the great Arsinoüs, came <br> From Tenedos with Nestor, when the town <br> Was ravaged by Achilles, and the Greeks <br> Gave her to Nestor, chosen from the rest <br> For him, as wisest of their counsellors. <br> First she drew forth a table fairly wrought, <br> Of polished surface, and with steel-blue feet, <br> And on it placed a brazen tray which bore <br> A thirst-provoking onion, honeycomb, <br> And sacred meal of wheat. Near these she set <br> A noble beaker which the ancient chief <br> Had brought from home, embossed with studs of gold. <br> Four were its handles, and each handle showed <br> Two golden turtles feeding, while below <br> Two others formed the base. Another hand <br> Could scarce have raised that beaker from its place, <br> But Nestor lifted it with ease. The maid, <br> Fair as a goddess, mingled Pramnian wine, <br> And grated o’er it, with a rasp of brass, <br> A goat’s-milk cheese, and, sprinkling the white flour <br> Upon it, bade them drink. With this they quenched <br> Their parching thirst, and then amused the time <br> With pleasant talk. Patroclus to the door <br> Meantime, a godlike presence, came, and stood. <br> The old man, as he saw him, instantly <br> Rose from his princely seat and seized his hand, <br> And led him in and bade him sit; but he <br> Refused the proffered courtesy, and said:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Nay, ’tis no time to sit: persuade me not, <br> Nursling of Jove; for he is to be feared, <br> And prone to wrath, who sent me to inquire <br> What wounded man is with thee; but I know⁠— <br> Now that I see Machaon sitting here, <br> The shepherd of the people. I must haste <br> Back to Achilles, bearing my report. <br> Thou knowest, ancient chief, how quick he is <br> To take offence and blame the innocent.”</p>
<p class="verse">Then Nestor, the Gerenian knight, rejoined:⁠— <br> “Why does Achilles pity thus the sons <br> Of Greece when wounded? Little can he know <br> What sorrow reigns throughout the Grecian host <br> While, smitten in the close or distant fight, <br> Our bravest lie disabled in their ships. <br> The valiant son of Tydeus⁠—Diomed⁠— <br> Is wounded⁠—wounded Agamemnon lies, <br> And the great wielder of the javelin, <br> Ulysses. By an arrow in the thigh <br> Eurypylus is smitten, and I now <br> Bring home this warrior with an arrow-wound. <br> Yet doth Achilles, valiant as he is, <br> Care nothing for the Greeks. Will he then wait <br> Till our swift galleys, moored upon the shore, <br> After a vain defence shall feed the flames <br> Lit by the enemy’s hand, and we be slain, <br> And perish, heaps on heaps? My strength is now <br> Not that which dwelt in these once active limbs. <br> Would I were strong and vigorous as of yore, <br> When strife arose between our men and those <br> Of Elis for our oxen driven away, <br> And, driving off their beeves in turn, I slew <br> The Elean chief, the brave Itymoneus, <br> Son of Hypirochus! For, as he sought <br> To save his herd, a javelin from my arm <br> Smote him the first among his band. He fell; <br> His rustic followers fled on every side; <br> And mighty was the spoil we took: of beeves <br> We drave off fifty herds, as many flocks <br> Of sheep, of swine as many, and of goats <br> An equal number, and of yellow steeds <br> Thrice fifty;⁠—these were mares, and by their sides <br> Ran many a colt. We drave them all within <br> Neleian Pylos in the night. Well pleased <br> Was Neleus, that so large a booty fell <br> To me, who entered on the war so young. <br> When morning brake, the heralds’ cry was heard <br> Summoning all the citizens to meet <br> To whom from fruitful Elis debts were due; <br> And then the princes of the Pyleans came, <br> And made division of the spoil. For much <br> The Epeians owed us: we were yet but few <br> In Pylos, and had suffered grievously. <br> The mighty Hercules in former years <br> Had made us feel his wrath, and of our men <br> Had slain the bravest: of the twelve who drew <br> Their birth from Neleus, I alone am left; <br> The others fell. The Epeians brazen-mailed <br> Saw this, delighted, and insulted us <br> And did us wrong. When now the spoil was shared <br> The old man for himself reserved a herd <br> Of oxen, and a numerous flock of sheep⁠— <br> Three hundred, with their shepherds⁠—for to him <br> Large debts were due in Elis. He had sent <br> Four horses once, of peerless speed, with cars, <br> To win a tripod, the appointed prize. <br> Augeias, king of men, detained them there, <br> And sent the grieving charioteer away. <br> My father, angered at the monarch’s words <br> And acts, took large amends, and gave the rest <br> To share among the people, that no one <br> Might leave the ground, defrauded of his right. <br> All this was justly done, and we performed <br> Due sacrifices to the gods, throughout <br> The city;⁠—when the third day came, and brought <br> The Epeians all at once, in all their strength⁠— <br> Both men on foot and prancing steeds. With these <br> Came the Molions twain, well armed, though young <br> And yet untrained to war. There is a town <br> Named Thryoëssa, on a lofty hill <br> Far off beside Alpheius, on the edge <br> Of sandy Pylos. They beleaguered this, <br> And sought to overthrow it. As they crossed <br> The plain, Minerva came, a messenger, <br> By night from Mount Olympus, bidding us <br> Put on our armor. Not unwillingly <br> The Pyleans mustered, but in eager haste <br> For battle. Yet did Neleus not consent <br> That I should arm myself⁠—he hid my steeds; <br> For still he deemed me inexpert in war. <br> Yet even then, although I fought on foot, <br> I won great honor even among the knights; <br> For so had Pallas favored me. A stream <br> Named Minyeius pours into the sea <br> Near to Arena, where the Pylean knights <br> Waited the coming of the holy morn, <br> While those who fought on foot came thronging <br> Thence, with our host complete, and all in arms, <br> We marched, and reached at noon the sacred stream at <br> Alpheius, where to Jove Omnipotent <br> We offered chosen victims, and a bull <br> To the river-god, another to the god <br> Of ocean, and a heifer yet unbroke <br> To blue-eyed Pallas. Then we banqueted, <br> In bands, throughout the army, and lay down <br> In armor by the river-side to sleep. <br> Meantime the brave Epeians stood around <br> The city, resolute to lay it waste. <br> But first was to be done a mighty work <br> Of war; for as the glorious sun appeared <br> Above the earth we dashed against the foe, <br> Praying to Jove and Pallas. When the fight <br> Between the Eleans and the Pylean host <br> Was just begun, I slew a youthful chief⁠— <br> Mulius⁠—and bore away his firm-paced steeds. <br> The fair-haired Agamedé, eldest-born <br> Of King Augeias’ daughters, was his spouse; <br> And well to her each healing herb was known <br> That springs from the great earth. As he drew near, <br> I smote him with my brazen lance: he fell <br> To earth: I sprang into his car, and stood <br> Among the foremost warriors; while, around, <br> The brave Epeians, as they saw him fall⁠— <br> The leader of their knights, their mightiest <br> In battle⁠—turned and, panic-stricken, fled, <br> Each his own way. I followed on their flight <br> Like a black tempest; fifty cars I took, <br> And from each car I dashed two warriors down, <br> Pierced by my spear. And now I should have slain <br> The young Molions also, Actor’s sons, <br> Had not their father, he who shakes the earth, <br> Enshrouded them in mist, and hidden them <br> From all pursuit. Then with victorious might <br> Did Jove endue our arms, while we pursued <br> The foe across a region strewn with shields⁠— <br> Slaying, and gathering spoil⁠—until our steeds <br> Came to Buprasium, rich in fields of wheat, <br> And to the Olenian rock, and to the hill <br> Alesium in Coloné. Pallas there <br> Stayed our pursuit, and bade our host return. <br> There slew I the last man, and left him there. <br> And then the Achaians, guiding their swift steeds <br> Homeward to Pylos from Euprasium, gave <br> Great thanks to Jupiter among the gods, <br> And Nestor among men. Such was I then <br> Among the heroes; but Achilles keeps <br> His valor for himself alone⁠—and yet <br> Bitterly must he grieve when he beholds <br> Our people perish. O my friend! How well <br> Menoetius charged thee when he sent thee forth, <br> From Phthia, to Atrides! We were both⁠— <br> The nobly born Ulysses and myself⁠— <br> Within the palace, and we clearly heard <br> What he commanded thee. For we had come <br> To Peleus’ stately dwelling, on our way <br> Gathering a host in fertile Greece, and saw <br> The great Menoetius there, and there we found <br> Achilles with thee. There the aged knight <br> Peleus was burning, in the palace-court, <br> A steer’s fat thighs to Jove the Thunderer, <br> And lifted up a golden cup and poured <br> Dark wine upon the blazing sacrifice. <br> And both of you were busy with the flesh <br> When we were at the threshold. As he saw <br> Our coming, in surprise Achilles sprang <br> Toward us, and took our hands and led us in, <br> Bade us be seated, and before us placed <br> The generous banquet due to stranger-guests. <br> Then, having feasted, I began discourse, <br> Exhorting you to join us. Both of you <br> At once consented, and your fathers gave <br> Their admonitions. Aged Peleus charged <br> His son Achilles to excel the rest <br> In valor, while Menoetius, in his turn, <br> The son of Actor, gave thee this command:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“ ‘My son, Achilles is the nobler born, <br> But thou art elder. He surpasses thee <br> By far in warlike might, but thou must prompt <br> His mind with prudent counsels; thou must warn <br> And guide him; he will hearken to thy words <br> Meant for his good.’ The old man charged thee thus. <br> Thou hast forgotten it. Yet speak thou now <br> To Peleus’ warlike son; and haply he <br> May heed thy counsels. Thou perchance mayst bend <br> His will⁠—who knows?⁠—by thy persuasive words <br> For wholesome are the warnings of a friend. <br> Yet, if he shrink from some predicted doom, <br> Or if his goddess-mother have revealed <br> Aught of Jove’s counsels to him, then, at least <br> Let him send thee to war, and let his troop <br> Of Myrmidons go with thee, so that thou <br> Mayst carry succor to the Greeks. Yet more⁠— <br> Let him permit thee in the field to wear <br> His glorious armor, that the Trojan host, <br> Beholding thee so like to him, may shun <br> The combat, and the warlike sons of Greece, <br> Hard-pressed, may breathe again, and find at length <br> A respite from the conflict. Ye, who still <br> Are fresh and vigorous, shall assault and drive <br> Townward the weary foe from camp and fleet.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake. The spirit of the youth took fire, <br> And instantly he hastened toward the ships <br> Of Peleus’ son. But when he came where lay <br> The galleys of Ulysses the divine, <br> Where was the assembly-place and judgment-seat, <br> And where the altars of the immortals stood, <br> Evaemon’s noble son, Eurypylus, <br> Met him as from the battle-field he came <br> Halting, and with an arrow in his thigh. <br> The sweat ran down his shoulders and his brow, <br> And the black blood was oozing from his wound, <br> Yet was his spirit untamed. The gallant youth, <br> Son of Menoetius, saw with grief, and said:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Unhappy chiefs and princes of the Greeks! <br> Are ye then doomed to feast with your fair limbs <br> The famished dogs of Ilium, far away <br> From friends and country? Tell me, child of Jove, <br> Gallant Eurypylus, will yet the Greeks <br> Withstand the mighty Hector, or give way <br> And perish, overtaken by his spear?”</p>
<p class="verse">And thus the wise Eurypylus replied:⁠— <br> “Nursling of Jove, Patroclus! For the Greeks <br> There is no help, and all at their black ships <br> Must perish; for within them even now <br> All those who were our bravest warriors lie, <br> Wounded in close encounter, or from far, <br> By Trojan hands, whose strength with every hour <br> Becomes more terrible. Give now thine aid <br> And take me to my ship, and cut away <br> The arrow from my thigh, and from the part <br> Cleanse with warm water the dark blood, and shed <br> Soothing and healing balms upon the wound, <br> As taught thee by Achilles, who had learned <br> The art from Chiron, righteous in his day <br> Beyond all other Centaurs. Now the leech <br> Machaon lies, I think, among the tents, <br> Wounded, and needs the aid of others’ skill, <br> And Podalirius out upon the plain <br> Helps stem the onset of the Trojan host.”</p>
<p class="verse">Then spake the valiant Menoetiades:⁠— <br> “O brave Eurypylus! What yet will be <br> The end of this, and what are we to do? <br> Even now I bear a message on my way <br> From reverend Nestor, guardian of the Greeks, <br> To the great warrior, Peleus’ son; and yet <br> I must not leave thee in thine hour of need.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake; and, lifting in his arms the prince, <br> He bore him to his tent. A servant spread, <br> Upon his entering, hides to form a couch; <br> And there Patroclus laid him down and cut <br> The rankling arrow from his thigh, and shed <br> Warm water on the wound to cleanse away <br> The purple blood, and last applied a root <br> Of bitter flavor to assuage the smart, <br> Bruising it first within his palms: the pangs <br> Ceased; the wound dried; the blood no longer flowed.</p>
