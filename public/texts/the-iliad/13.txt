<p class="lede">Descent of Neptune in aid of the Greeks⁠—His exhortations addressed to the Chiefs⁠—The Trojans harangued by Hector, and the battle renewed with great fury⁠—Hector’s advance checked by the Ajaxes, who rally the Greeks⁠—Exploits of Meriones and Idomeneus⁠—Idomeneus forced to retire by Deïphobus and Aeneas⁠—The Trojans, hard pressed on their left, are rallied by Hector⁠—Reproof of Paris by Hector, and mutual defiance of Hector and Ajax.</p>
<p class="verse">When Jove had brought the Trojans and their chief, <br> Hector, beside the ships, he left them there <br> To toil and struggle and endure, while he <br> Turned his resplendent eyes upon the land <br> Of Thracian horsemen, and the Mysians, skilled <br> To combat hand to hand, and the famed tribe <br> Of long-lived Hippomulgi, reared on milk, <br> And the most just of men. On Troy no more <br> He turned those glorious eyes, for now he deemed <br> That none of all the gods would seek to aid <br> Either the Greeks or Trojans in the strife.</p>
<p class="verse">The monarch Neptune kept no idle watch; <br> For he in Thracian Samos, dark with woods, <br> Aloft upon the highest summit sat <br> O’erlooking thence the tumult of the war; <br> For thence could he behold the Idaean mount, <br> And Priam’s city, and the Grecian fleet. <br> There, coming from the ocean-deeps, he sat, <br> And pitied the Greek warriors put to rout <br> Before the Trojans, and was wroth with Jove. <br> Soon he descended from those rugged steeps, <br> And trod the earth with rapid strides; the hills <br> And forests quaked beneath the immortal feet <br> Of Neptune as he walked. Three strides he took, <br> And at the fourth reached Aegae, where he stopped, <br> And where his sumptuous palace-halls were built, <br> Deep down in ocean, golden, glittering, proof <br> Against decay of time. These when he reached, <br> He yoked his swift and brazen-footed steeds, <br> With manes of flowing gold, to draw his car, <br> And put on golden mail, and took his scourge, <br> Wrought of fine gold, and climbed the chariot-seat, <br> And rode upon the waves. The whales came forth <br> From their deep haunts, and frolicked round his way: <br> They knew their king. The waves rejoicing smoothed <br> A path, and rapidly the coursers flew; <br> Nor was the brazen axle wet below. <br> And thus they brought him to the Grecian fleet.</p>
<p class="verse">Deep in the sea there is a spacious cave, <br> Between the rugged Imbrus and the isle <br> Of Tenedos. There Neptune, he who shakes <br> The shores, held back his steeds, took off their yoke, <br> Gave them ambrosial food, and, binding next <br> Their feet with golden fetters which no power <br> Might break or loosen, so that they might wait <br> Their lord’s return, he sought the Grecian host.</p>
<p class="verse">Still did the Trojans, rushing on in crowds, <br> Like flames or like a tempest, follow close <br> Hector, the son of Priam; still their rage <br> Abated not; with stormy cries they came; <br> They hoped to seize the fleet and slay the Greeks <br> Beside it. But the power who swathes the earth <br> And shakes it, Neptune, coming from the deep, <br> Revived the valor of the Greeks. He took <br> The shape of Calchas and his powerful voice, <br> And thus to either Ajax, who yet stemmed <br> The battle with a resolute heart, he spake:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“O chieftains! Yours it is to save the host, <br> Recalling your old valor, with no thought <br> Of fatal flight. Elsewhere I feel no dread <br> Of what the daring sons of Troy may do <br> Who climb the wall in throngs; the well-greaved Greeks <br> Will meet them bravely. But where Hector leads, <br> Fierce as a flame, his squadrons, he who boasts <br> To be a son of sovereign Jove, I fear <br> Lest we should sorely suffer. May the gods <br> Strengthen your hearts to stand against the foe, <br> And flinch not, and exhort the rest to stand, <br> And drive him back, audacious as he is, <br> From the swift ships, though Jove should urge him on.”</p>
<p class="verse">Thus earth-surrounding Neptune said, and touched <br> Each hero with his sceptre, filled their hearts <br> With valor, gave new lightness to their limbs <br> And feet and hands, and then, as when a hawk <br> Shoots swiftly from some lofty precipice <br> And chases o’er the plain another bird, <br> So swiftly Neptune, shaker of the shores, <br> Darted from them away. Oileus’ son <br> Perceived the immortal presence first, and thus <br> At once to Telamonian Ajax spake:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Some god, O Ajax, from the Olympian hill, <br> Wearing the augur’s form, hath bid us fight <br> Beside the ships; nor can it be the seer <br> Calchas, for well I marked his feet and legs <br> As he departed; easily by these <br> The gods are known. I feel a spirit roused <br> In my own bosom eager to engage <br> In the fierce strife; my very feet below, <br> And hands above, take part in the desire.”</p>
<p class="verse">And thus the son of Telamon replied:⁠— <br> “So also these strong hands that grasp the spear <br> Burn eagerly to wield it, and my heart <br> Is full of courage. I am hurried on <br> By both my feet, and vehemently long <br> To try alone the combat with this chief <br> Of boundless valor, Hector, Priam’s son.”</p>
<p class="verse">Thus they conferred, rejoicing as they felt <br> That ardor for the battle which the god <br> Had breathed into their hearts. Meantime he roused <br> The Achaians at the rear, who in their ships too <br> Sought respite, and whose limbs were faint with toil, <br> And their hearts sad to see the Trojan host <br> With tumult pouring o’er the lofty wall. <br> As they beheld, the tears came gushing forth <br> From underneath their lids; they little hoped <br> For rescue from destruction; but when came <br> The power that shakes the shores, he woke anew <br> The spirit of their valiant phalanxes. <br> Teucer he first addressed, and Leitus, <br> The hero Peneleus and Thoas next, <br> Deipyrus, Meriones expert <br> In battle, and Antilochus his peer, <br> And thus exhorted them with wingèd words:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Shame on you, Argive youths! I put my trust <br> In your tried valor to defend our fleet; <br> But if ye fear to face the perilous fight, <br> The day has risen which shall behold us fall <br> Vanquished before the Trojans. O ye gods! <br> These eyes have seen a marvel, a strange sight <br> And terrible, which I had never thought <br> Could be⁠—the Trojans close upon our ships, <br> They who, erewhile, were like the timid deer <br> That wander in the wood an easy prey <br> To jackals, pards, and wolves⁠—weak things, unapt <br> For combat, fleeing, but without an aim. <br> Such were the Trojans, who till now ne’er dared <br> Withstand the might and prowess of the Greeks <br> Even for an hour. But now, afar from Troy <br> They give us battle at the hollow ships, <br> All through our general’s fault, and through the sloth <br> Of the Greek warriors, who, displeased with him, <br> Fight not for their swift galleys, but are slain <br> Beside them. Yet although our sovereign chief, <br> Atrides Agamemnon, may have done <br> Foul wrong, dishonoring the swift-footed son <br> Of Peleus, still ye cannot without blame <br> Decline the combat. Let us then repair <br> The mischief done; the hearts of valiant men <br> Are soon appeased. And not without the loss <br> Of honor can your fiery courage sleep, <br> Since ye are known the bravest of the host. <br> I would not chide the weak, unwarlike man <br> For shrinking from the combat; but for you⁠— <br> I look on you with anger in my heart. <br> Weaklings! Ye soon will bring upon yourselves <br> Some sorer evil if ye loiter thus. <br> Let each of you bethink him of the shame <br> And infamy impending. Terrible <br> The struggle is before us. Hector storms <br> The ships, loud-shouting Hector; he has burst <br> The gate and broken the protecting bar.”</p>
<p class="verse">So Neptune spake, encouraging the Greeks. <br> While firmly stood the serried phalanxes <br> Round either Ajax, nor could Mars himself, <br> Nor Pallas, musterer of armèd hosts, <br> Reprove their order. There the flower of Greece <br> Waited the Trojans and their noble chief, <br> Spear beside spear, and shield by shield, so close <br> That buckler pressed on buckler, helm on helm, <br> And man on man. The plumes of horse-hair touched <br> Each other as they nodded on the crests <br> Of the bright helms, so close the warriors stood. <br> The lances quivered in the fearless hands <br> Of warriors eager to advance and strike <br> The enemy. But the men of Troy began <br> The assault; the fiery Hector was the first <br> To rush against the Greeks. As when a stone <br> Rolls from a cliff before a wintry flood <br> That sweeps it down the steep, when mighty rains <br> Have worn away the props that held it fast; <br> It rolls and bounds on high; the woods around <br> Crash, as it tears its unresisted way <br> Along the slope until it reach the plain, <br> And there, however urged, moves on no more;⁠— <br> So Hector, menacing to cut his way <br> Through tents and galleys to the very sea, <br> Slaying as he went forward, when he now <br> Met the firm phalanxes and pressed them close, <br> Stopped suddenly; the sons of Greece withstood <br> His onset and repulsed it, striking him <br> With swords and two-edged spears, and made the chief <br> Give way before the shock. He lifted up <br> His voice and shouted to the Trojans thus:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Trojans and Lycians and Dardanians skilled <br> In fighting hand to hand, stand firm. Not long <br> Will the Greeks bide my onset, though drawn up <br> Square as a tower in close array. My spear, <br> I trust, will scatter them, if true it be <br> That Juno’s husband, Sovereign of the gods, <br> And Lord of thunders, prompts my arm today.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake, and kindled in the breasts of all <br> Fresh courage. In the band Deïphobus <br> Marched proudly, Priam’s son, with his round shield <br> Before him, walking with a quick, light step <br> Behind its shelter. Then Meriones <br> Aimed at the chief his glittering spear; the point <br> Missed not; it struck the orb of bullock’s hide, <br> Yet did not pierce it, for the weapon broke <br> Just at the neck. Deïphobus held forth <br> His shield far from him, dreading to receive <br> A spear-thrust from the brave Meriones. <br> Vexed thus to lose the victory, and the spear <br> Snapped by the blow, Meriones fell back <br> Into the column of his friends, and passed <br> Hastily toward the camp and ships, to bring <br> A powerful spear that stood within his tent, <br> While others fought, and fearful was the din.</p>
<p class="verse">Then Teucer first, the son of Telamon, <br> Smote gallant Imbrius, son of Mentor, lord <br> Of many steeds. He, ere the Greeks had come <br> To Troy, dwelt at Pedaeum and espoused <br> Medesicasta, Priam’s spurious child. <br> But when the well-oared galleys of the Greeks <br> Mustered at Troy, he also came, and there <br> Was eminent among her chiefs, and dwelt <br> With Priam, and was honored as his son. <br> The son of Telamon beneath the ear <br> Pierced him with his long javelin, and drew forth <br> The weapon. Headlong to the earth he fell. <br> As on a mountain height, descried from far, <br> Hewn by a brazen axe, an ash is felled <br> And lays its tender sprays upon the ground, <br> Thus Imbrius fell, and round him in his fall <br> Clashed his bright armor. Teucer sprang in haste <br> To spoil the dead, but Hector hurled at him <br> His shining spear; the wary Teucer stepped <br> Aside, and just escaped the brazen blade. <br> It struck Amphiniachus, Cteatus’ son, <br> And Actor’s grandson; as he came to join <br> The battle, he was smitten in the breast, <br> And fell, his armor clashing round his limbs. <br> Then Hector flew in haste to tear away <br> From the large-souled Amphimachus the helm <br> That cased his temples. Ajax saw, and hurled <br> His glittering spear at Hector as he came: <br> It made no wound; for Hector stood equipped <br> All o’er in formidable brass. The spear <br> Struck on the bossy shield with such a shock <br> As forced him to recoil, and leave unspoiled <br> The bodies, which the Achaians dragged away. <br> For Stichius and Menestheus, chief among <br> The Athenians, bore the dead Amphimachus <br> To the Greek camp, while the two men of might, <br> The chieftains Ajax, lifted Imbrius up; <br> And as two lions, bearing off among <br> The close-grown shrubs a goat, which they have snatched <br> From sharp-toothed dogs, uplift it in their jaws <br> Above the ground, so the two warriors raised <br> The corpse of Imbrius, and stripped off the mail, <br> While, angered that Amphilochus was slain, <br> Oileus’ son struck from the tender neck <br> The head, and sent it far among the crowd, <br> Whirled like a ball, to fall at Hector’s feet.</p>
<p class="verse">Meantime was Neptune moved with grief to see <br> His grandson perish in that desperate fray, <br> And passed among the Achaian tents and ships <br> Encouraging the men, and planning woes <br> For Ilium. There he met Idomeneus, <br> Expert to wield the spear, as he returned <br> From caring for a comrade who had left <br> The battle, wounded in the knee, and whom <br> His friends had carried in. Idomeneus <br> Had called the surgeons to his aid, and now <br> Was hastening to the field, intent to bear <br> His part in battle. Him the monarch god <br> Of ocean thus addressed, but first he took <br> The voice of Thoas, King Andraemon’s son, <br> Whose father ruled the Aetolians through the bounds <br> Of Pleuron, and in lofty Calydon, <br> And like a god was honored in the land.</p>
<p class="verse">“O counsellor of Crete, Idomeneus! <br> Where are the threats which late the sons of Greece <br> Uttered against the Trojans?” Promptly came <br> The Cretan leader’s answer: “No man here, <br> O Thoas, seems blameworthy, for we all <br> Are skilled in war, nor does unmanly fear <br> Hold any back; nor from the difficult strife <br> Does sloth detain one warrior. So it is <br> Doubtless that it seems good to Saturn’s son, <br> The All-disposer, that the Greeks, afar <br> From Argos, should ingloriously fall <br> And perish. Thoas, thou wert ever brave, <br> And didst exhort the laggards. Cease not now <br> To combat, cease not to exhort the rest.”</p>
<p class="verse">And Neptune, he who shakes the earth, rejoined:⁠— <br> “Idomeneus, whoever keeps aloof <br> From battle, willingly, today, may he <br> Never return from Troy, but be the prey <br> Of dogs. Take thou thy arms and come with me, <br> For we must quit ourselves like men, and strive <br> To aid our cause, although we be but two. <br> Great is the strength of feeble arms combined, <br> And we can combat even with the brave.”</p>
<p class="verse">So speaking, Neptune turned to share the toils <br> Of war. Idomeneus, who now had reached <br> His princely tent, put on his glorious mail, <br> And seized two spears, and flew upon his way, <br> Like lightning grasped by Saturn’s son and flung <br> Quivering above Olympus’ gleaming peak, <br> A sign to mortals, dazzled by the blaze, <br> So glittered, as he ran, his brazen mail. <br> His fellow-warrior, good Meriones, <br> Met him beside the tent, for he had come <br> To fetch a brazen javelin thence, and thus <br> The stout Idomeneus addressed his friend:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“O son of Molus, swift Meriones, <br> Dearest of all my comrades! Why hast thou <br> Thus left the battle-field? Hast thou a wound⁠— <br> A weapon’s point that galls thee? Dost thou bring <br> A message to me? Think not that I sit <br> Within my tent an idler: I must fight.”</p>
<p class="verse">Discreetly did Meriones reply:⁠— <br> “Idomeneus, whose sovereign counsels rule <br> The well-armed Cretans, I am come to seek <br> A spear if one be left within thy tents. <br> I broke the one I bore, in hurling it <br> Against the shield of fierce Deïphobus.”</p>
<p class="verse">The Cretan chief, Idomeneus, rejoined:⁠— <br> “If spears thou seek, there stand within my tent <br> Twenty and one against the shining walls. <br> I took them from slain Trojans. ’Tis my wont <br> Never to fight at distance from the foe, <br> And therefore have I spears, and bossy shields, <br> And helms, and body-mail of polished brass.”</p>
<p class="verse">Then spake in turn discreet Meriones:⁠— <br> “Within my tent are also many spoils <br> Won from the Trojans, and in my black ship; <br> But they are far away. I do not think <br> That I forget what valor is. I fight <br> Among the foremost in the glorious strife <br> Where’er the battle calls me. Other men <br> Among the well-armed Greeks may not have seen <br> What I perform, but thou must know me well.”</p>
<p class="verse">Idomeneus, the Cretan leader, spake:⁠— <br> “I know thy courage well. What need hast thou <br> To speak as thou hast done? If all of us, <br> The bravest of the Greeks, were set apart <br> To form an ambush;⁠—for an ambush tries <br> And shows men’s valor; there the craven, there <br> The brave, is known; the coward’s color comes <br> And goes; his spirit is not calm within <br> His bosom, so that he can rest awhile <br> And tremble not; he shifts his place; he sits <br> On both his feet; his heart beats audibly <br> Within his breast; his teeth at thought of death <br> Chatter; the brave man’s color changes not, <br> Nor when with other warriors he sits down <br> In ambush is he troubled, but he longs <br> To rise and mingle in the desperate fray;⁠— <br> For thee, in such an ambush, none could blame <br> Thy courage or thy skill. If there the foe <br> Should wound thee from afar, or smite thee near, <br> The weapon would not strike thy neck behind, <br> Or pierce thy back, but enter at thy breast <br> Or stomach, as thou wert advancing fast <br> Among the foremost. But enough of this. <br> Come! Stand we here no longer, idiot-like, <br> Lest someone chide us sharply. Hasten thou, <br> And bring a sturdy javelin from the tent.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake. Meriones, like Mars in port <br> And swiftness, hastened to the tent and brought <br> A brazen spear, and joined Idomeneus, <br> Eager for battle. As the god of war, <br> The man-destroyer, comes into the field, <br> With Terror, his strong-limbed and dauntless son, <br> Following and striking fear into the heart <br> Of the most resolute warrior, when from Thrace <br> They issue armed against the Ephyri, <br> Or else against the Phlegyans large of soul, <br> And hearken not to both the hosts, but give <br> To one the victory; so Meriones <br> Advanced to battle with Idomeneus, <br> Leaders of heroes both, and both equipped <br> In glittering helms. And first Meriones <br> Spake and addressed his fellow-warrior thus:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Son of Deucalion, at which point wilt thou <br> Enter the throng? Upon the army’s right, <br> Its centre, or its left? The long haired Greeks <br> Seem most to need our aid upon the left.”</p>
<p class="verse">Then spoke Idomeneus, in turn, the prince <br> Of Cretans: “At the centre of the fleet <br> Are others who will guard it. Posted there <br> Are either Ajax and the most expert <br> Of Grecian archers, Teucer, not less skilled <br> In standing fight, and amply will they task <br> The arm of Hector, Priam’s son, though bent <br> On desperate conflict, and though passing fierce. <br> With all his fierceness, he will find it hard <br> To quell their prowess, never yet o’ercome, <br> And fire the ships, unless Saturnian Jove <br> Himself should cast on them the flaming torch. <br> Nor yet will Telamonian Ajax yield <br> To any man of mortal birth, or reared <br> Upon the grains of Ceres, or whom brass <br> Or ponderous stones can wound. He would not own <br> The warlike son of Peleus mightier <br> Than he in standing fight, although in speed <br> He vies not with him. Lead us then to join <br> The army’s left, that we may learn at once <br> Whether our fate in battle shall confer <br> Glory on other men, or theirs on us.”</p>
<p class="verse">So spake the chief. Meriones, the peer <br> Of Mars in swiftness, hastened till he joined <br> The army where his comrade bade. The foe <br> Beheld Idomeneus, who like a flame <br> Swept on with his companion all in arms <br> Gloriously wrought; they raised from rank to rank <br> The battle-cry, and met him as he came, <br> And hand to hand, before the galleys’ sterns <br> Was waged the combat. As when storms arise, <br> Blown up by piping winds, when dust lies loose <br> Along the roads, a spreading cloud of dust <br> Fills the wide air, so came the battle on <br> Between the bands that struggled eagerly <br> To slay each other. All along the line <br> The murderous conflict bristled with long spears <br> That tore the flesh; the brazen splendor, shot <br> From gleaming helmets and from burnished mail <br> And shining bucklers, all in narrow space <br> Dazzled the eyes. Brave-hearted would he be, <br> The man who, gazing on it, could have seen <br> The furious strife rejoicing or unmoved.</p>
<p class="verse">Meantime the potent sons of Saturn each <br> Favored a different side, and planned new toils <br> For all the warriors, Jupiter had willed <br> That Hector and the Trojans should prevail, <br> Yet had he not decreed the Achaian host <br> To perish before Troy; he only sought <br> To honor Thetis and her large-souled son. <br> But Neptune, mingling with the Greeks, aroused <br> Their martial spirit. From the hoary deep <br> He came unmarked, for deeply was he grieved <br> To see the Greeks give way before the host <br> Of Troy, and he was wroth with Jupiter. <br> Both gods were of one race, and owed their birth <br> To the same parents; but the elder-born <br> Was Jupiter, and wiser. For that cause <br> Not openly did Neptune aid the Greeks, <br> But, as by stealth, disguised in human form, <br> Moved through their army and encouraged them <br> To combat. Thus it was the potent twain <br> Each drew, with equal hand, the net of strife <br> And fearful havoc, which no power could break <br> Or loosen, stretched o’er both the warring hosts, <br> And laying many a warrior low in death. <br> And now, although his brows were strewn with gray. <br> Idomeneus, encouraging the Greeks, <br> Rushed on the Trojans, and revived the fight. <br> He slew Orthryoneus, who just before, <br> Drawn by the rumor of the war, had left <br> Cabesus, and now made a lover’s suit <br> For Priam’s fairest daughter. Without dower <br> He sought to wed Cassandra, promising <br> A vast exploit⁠—to drive the Greeks from Troy, <br> In spite of all their valor. The old king <br> Consented that the maiden should be his; <br> And now he fought, and trusted to fulfil <br> His promise. But Idomeneus took aim, <br> And cast his glittering javelin at the youth. <br> It struck him marching proudly on, nor stopped <br> The weapon at the brazen mail, but pierced <br> The stomach. With a clash the warrior fell, <br> And thus the victor boasted over him:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Orthryoneus, I deem thee worthy of praise <br> Beyond all other men, if thou perform <br> What thou hast undertaken⁠—to defend <br> Dardanian Priam, who has promised thee <br> His daughter. We would make a compact too, <br> And will perform it⁠—to bestow on thee <br> A spouse, the fairest daughter of the house <br> Of Atreus’ son, and we will send for her <br> To Argos, if thou join us, and lay waste <br> The well built Ilium. Now, then, follow me, <br> And at the ships which brought us we will treat <br> Of marriage, and will make no niggard terms.”</p>
<p class="verse">So spake Idomeneus, and dragged the slain <br> Through the sharp conflict by the foot. He met <br> Asius, who walked before his car, and came <br> To avenge his friend. The attending charioteer <br> Behind him reined the steeds, that they should breathe <br> Over the shoulders of their lord, who sought <br> To smite Idomeneus. The Greek was first <br> To strike; he plunged the spear into his throat <br> Below the chin, and drave the weapon through. <br> The Trojan fell to earth as falls an oak, <br> Poplar, or stately pine, which woodmen fell <br> With their sharp axes on the mountain-side, <br> To form a galley’s beam. So there he lay <br> Stretched out before his coursers and his car, <br> And gnashed his teeth, and clenched the bloody dust. <br> The charioteer, amazed, and losing power <br> Of action, dared not turn the horses back <br> To bear him from the foe. Antilochus <br> The warlike cast his spear, and in the midst <br> Transfixed him. Little did the brazen mail <br> Avail to stay the blade, which cleft its way <br> Into the stomach. With a sudden gasp <br> He toppled from the sumptuous chariot-seat, <br> And large-souled Nestor’s son, Antilochus, <br> Drave with the chariot to the well-armed Greeks. <br> Deïphobus, who sorrowed for the fate <br> Of Asius, drawing near Idomeneus, <br> Hurled at him his bright spear. The Greek beheld, <br> As face to face they stood, and scaped the stroke, <br> Covered by his round shield, two-handled, strong, <br> With bullocks’ hides and glittering brass. With this <br> He hid himself, close couched within, and turned <br> The brazen point aside. The buckler rang <br> Shrilly; the weapon glanced away, yet flew <br> Not vainly from the Trojan’s powerful hand: <br> It struck Hypsenor, son of Hippasus, <br> The shepherd of the people, on the side <br> Where lies the liver, just below the breast. <br> His knees gave way; he fell; Deïphobus <br> Thus shouted o’er the dead his empty boast:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Not unavenged lies Asius, and no doubt, <br> In journeying to the massy gates and wall <br> Of Hades, will rejoice that I have sent <br> A soul to be companion of his way.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake; and at his boast the Greeks were moved <br> With anger⁠—most of all Antilochus <br> The warlike; yet he left not to the foe <br> His slain companion, but made haste to hold <br> His shield above him. His beloved friends, <br> Mecisteus, son of Echius, and the prince <br> Alastor, lifted up, with many a groan, <br> The corpse, and bore it to the roomy ships.</p>
<p class="verse">Meantime the valor of Idomeneus <br> Remitted not; he vehemently longed <br> To cover many a Trojan with the night <br> Of death, or fall himself with clashing arms, <br> In warring to defend the ships of Greece. <br> The brave Alcathoüs, the beloved son <br> Of Aesyetus, whom Anchises made <br> His son-in-law⁠—for he had given to him <br> Hippodameia, eldest-born of all <br> His daughters, whom her parents, while she dwelt <br> With them, loved dearly, fair and wise beyond <br> All other maidens of her age, and skilled <br> In household arts; so that the noblest prince <br> Of the broad Trojan kingdom made her his;⁠— <br> Him, by the weapon of Idomeneus, <br> Did Neptune bring to death. The sparkling eyes <br> Grew dim, and stiffened were the shapely limbs, <br> For neither could he flee nor turn aside; <br> But as he stood before him, column-like, <br> Or like a towering tree, Idomeneus <br> Transfixed him in the bosom with his spear <br> The brazen coat of mail gave way, which oft <br> Had saved him, breaking with a sharp, shrill sound <br> Before the severing blade. He fell to earth <br> With noise; the spear stood planted in his heart, <br> And as he panted quivered through its length, <br> Yet soon its murderous force was spent and still. <br> And then the victor boasted thus aloud:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Deïphobus, does this appear to thee <br> A fair return, when three are slain for one, <br> Or hast thou boasted idly? Yet do thou, <br> Vain as thou art, stand forth and face me here, <br> And I will teach thee of what race I am⁠— <br> An offshoot of the stock of Jove, whose son <br> Was Minos, guardian of our Crete, and he <br> Was father of the good Deucalion. <br> Deucalion’s son am I, and I am king <br> O’er many men in the broad isle of Crete. <br> My galleys brought me thence to be the dread <br> Of thee, thy father, and the men of Troy.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake. Deïphobus, irresolute, <br> Stood doubting whether to retreat and bring <br> Some other of the heroic sons of Troy <br> To aid him, or to try the fight alone. <br> As thus he mused, it seemed most wise to seek <br> Aeneas. Him he found withdrawn among <br> The rear of the army, for he was displeased <br> With noble Priam, who had paid his worth <br> With light esteem. Deïphobus approached, <br> And thus with wingèd words accosted him:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Aeneas, counsellor of Troy, if thou <br> Hadst ever a regard to him who was <br> Thy sister’s husband, it becomes thee now <br> To avenge him. Follow me, and help avenge <br> Alcathoüs, guardian of thy tender years, <br> Slain by the spear of famed Idomeneus.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake; and at his words Aeneas felt <br> His courage rise. Impatient for the fight, <br> He went to meet Idomeneus; yet fear <br> Fell not upon the Greek as if he were <br> A puny boy: he stood and kept his ground. <br> As, when a mountain boar, unterrified, <br> Waits in the wilderness the hunter-crew, <br> That come with mighty din, his bristly back <br> Rises, his eyes shoot fire, he whets his tusks, <br> And fiercely keeps both dogs and men at bay⁠— <br> So did Idomeneus, expert to wield <br> The spear, await Aeneas hastening on <br> With fury. Not a backward step he made, <br> But called upon his warrior-friends aloud, <br> Looking at Aphareus, Ascalaphus, <br> Deipyrus, Meriones, and last <br> Antilochus, all skilled in arts of war, <br> And thus exhorted them with wingèd words:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Haste hither, O my friends, and bring me aid. <br> I stand alone, in dread of the approach <br> Of swift Aeneas, who comes fiercely on, <br> Powerful to slay, and in his prime of youth, <br> The highest vigor of the human frame. <br> Yet, were our years the same, that chief or I <br> Would quickly triumph at the other’s cost.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake, and all with one accord drew near <br> And stood by him, with shields obliquely held <br> Upon their shoulders. On the other side <br> Aeneas cheered his comrades on. He fixed <br> His look on Paris, and Deïphobus, <br> And nobly born Agenor, who, like him, <br> Were leaders of the Trojans. After these <br> The soldiers followed, as the thronging flock <br> Follow the ram that leads them to the fount <br> From pasture, and the shepherd’s heart is pleased. <br> So was Aeneas glad at heart to see <br> The multitude of warriors following him.</p>
<p class="verse">Then mingled they in battle hand to hand <br> Around Alcathoüs, with their ponderous spears, <br> And fearfully upon their bosoms rang <br> The brass, as through the struggling crowd they aimed <br> Their weapons at each other. Two brave men, <br> Aeneas and Idomeneus, the peers <br> Of Mars, conspicuous o’er their fellows, strove <br> With cruel brass to rend each other’s limbs. <br> And first Aeneas cast his spear to smite <br> Idomeneus, who saw it as it came, <br> And shunned it. Plunging in the earth beyond, <br> It stood and quivered; it had left in vain <br> The Trojan’s powerful hand. Idomeneus <br> Next smote Oenomaüs: the spear brake through <br> His hollow corselet at the waist; it pierced <br> And drank the entrails: down amid the dust <br> He fell, and grasped the earth with dying hand. <br> Idomeneus plucked forth the massy spear, <br> But, pressed by hostile weapons, ventured not <br> To strip the sumptuous armor from the dead; <br> Since now no more the sinews of his feet <br> Were firm to bear him rushing to retake <br> His spear, or start aside from hostile spears. <br> Wherefore in standing fight he warded off <br> The evil hour, nor trusted to his feet <br> To bear him fleetly from the field. He moved <br> Slowly away, and now Deïphobus, <br> Who long had hated him and bitterly, <br> Aimed at him his bright spear; it missed its mark, <br> And struck Ascalaphus, the son of Mars. <br> The weapon cleft the shoulder of the Greek, <br> Who fell amid the dust, and clenched the earth.</p>
<p class="verse">Not yet the clamorous Mars, of passionate mood, <br> Had heard that in the fray his son was slain; <br> But on the summit of the Olympian mount <br> He sat, o’ercanopied by golden clouds, <br> Restrained from combat by the will of Jove, <br> With other gods, forbidden, like himself, <br> To aid the combatants. Meantime around <br> Ascalaphus the combat hand to hand <br> Still raged. Deïphobus had torn away <br> The slain man’s shining helm, when suddenly <br> Meriones sprang forward, spear in hand, <br> And smote him on the arm; the wounded limb <br> Let fall the helm, resounding as it fell, <br> And with a vulture’s leap Meriones <br> Rushed toward him, plucking out from the torn flesh <br> The spear, and falling back among the crowd. <br> Polites, brother of the wounded, threw <br> Both arms around his waist, and bore him off <br> From the loud din of conflict, till he reached <br> His swift-paced steeds, that waited in the rear <br> Of battle, with their chariot nobly wrought <br> And charioteer. These took him back to Troy, <br> Heavily groaning and in pain, the blood <br> Yet gushing from the newly wounded limb.</p>
<p class="verse">Still fought the other warriors, and the noise <br> Of a perpetual tumult filled the air. <br> Aeneas, rushing upon Aphareus, <br> Caletor’s son, who turned to face him, thrust <br> A sharp spear through his throat. With drooping head, <br> And carrying shield and helmet to the ground, <br> He fell, and rendered up his soul in death. <br> Antilochus, as Thoön turned away, <br> Attacked and smote him, cutting off the vein <br> That passes through the body to the neck. <br> This he divided sheer; the warrior fell <br> Backward, and lay in dust, with hands outstretched <br> To his beloved friends. Antilochus <br> Flew to the slain, and from his shoulders stripped <br> The armor, casting cautious glances round; <br> While toward him pressed the Trojans on all sides, <br> Striking the fair broad buckler with their darts, <br> Yet could not even score with pointed brass <br> The tender skin of Nestor’s son; for still <br> Neptune, the shaker of the sea-coast, kept <br> Watch o’er him while the weapons round him showered. <br> Yet he withdrew not from his foes, but moved <br> Among the crowd, nor idle was his spear, <br> But wielded right and left, and still he watched <br> With resolute mind the time to strike the foe <br> At distance, or assault him near at hand.</p>
<p class="verse">The son of Asius, Adamas, beheld <br> The hero meditating thus, and struck, <br> In close attack, the middle of his shield <br> With a sharp brazen spear. The dark-haired god <br> Who rules the deep denied to Adamas <br> The life he sought, and weakened the hard stroke. <br> Part of the Trojan’s weapon, like a stake <br> Hardened by fire, stood fixed within the shield, <br> Part lay on earth, and he who cast it slunk <br> Among his comrades to avoid his fate. <br> Meriones, pursuing with his spear, <br> Smote him between the navel and the groin, <br> Where deadliest are the wounds in battle given <br> To man’s unhappy race. He planted there <br> The cruel blade, and Adamas, who fell, <br> Writhed panting round it, as a bullock bound <br> By cowherds on the mountain with strong cords <br> Pants as they lead him off against his will. <br> So wounded, Adamas drew heavy breath, <br> And yet not long. The brave Meriones, <br> Approaching, plucked the weapon forth, and night <br> Came o’er the eyes of Adamas. At hand <br> Stood Helenus, and struck Deipyrus <br> Upon the temple with his ponderous sword, <br> Of Thracian make, and cut the three-coned helm <br> Away, and dashed it to the ground; it rolled <br> Between a Grecian warrior’s feet, who stooped <br> And took it up, while o’er its owner’s eyes <br> The darkness gathered. Grieved at this, the son <br> Of Atreus, Menelaus great in war, <br> Rushed forward, threatening royal Helenus. <br> He brandished his sharp spear; the Trojan drew <br> His bow; advancing, one to hurl a lance, <br> And one to send an arrow. Priam’s son <br> Let fly a shaft at Menelaus’ breast. <br> The bitter missile from the hollow mail <br> Glanced off. As when from the broad winnowing-fan <br> On some wide threshing-floor the swarthy beans, <br> Or vetches, bound before the whistling wind <br> And winnower’s force, so, bounding from the mail <br> Of gallant Menelaus, flew afar <br> The bitter shaft. Then Menelaus, great <br> In battle, smote the hand of Helenus <br> That held the polished bow; the brazen spear <br> Passed through the hand, and reached the bow, and there <br> Stood fixed, while Helenus, avoiding death, <br> Drew back among his comrades, with his hand <br> Held low, and trailing still the ashen stem. <br> Magnanimous Agenor from the wound <br> Drew forth the blade, and wrapped the hand in wool, <br> Carefully twisted, taken from a sling <br> Carried by an attendant of the chief.</p>
<p class="verse">To meet the glorious Menelaus sprang <br> Pisander, led by his unhappy fate <br> To perish, Menelaus! by thy hand <br> In that fierce conflict. When the two were near, <br> Advancing toward each other, Atreus’ son <br> Took aim amiss; his spear flew far aside. <br> Pisander smote the buckler on the arm <br> Of mighty Menelaus, yet drave not <br> The weapon through. The broad shield stopped its force, <br> And broke it at the neck; yet hoped he still <br> For victory, and exulted. Then the son <br> Of Atreus drew his silver-studded sword <br> And sprang upon his foe, who from beneath <br> His buckler took a brazen battle-axe, <br> With a long stem of polished olive-wood. <br> Both struck at once. Pisander hewed away, <br> Below the crest, the plumèd helmet-cone <br> Of Atreus’ son, who smote, above the nose, <br> Pisander’s forehead, crashing through the bones. <br> Both bleeding eyes dropped to the ground amid <br> The dust; he fell; he writhed; the conqueror, <br> Advancing, set his heel upon his breast, <br> And stripped the armor off, and, boasting, said:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Thus shall ye leave unharmed the fleet that brought <br> The knights of Greece, ye treaty-breaking sons <br> Of Ilium, never satisfied with war! <br> Yet lack ye not still other guilt and shame⁠— <br> Wrong done to me, ye dogs! Ye have not feared <br> The wrath of Hospitable Jove, who flings <br> The thunder, and will yet destroy your town, <br> With all its towers⁠—ye who, without a cause, <br> Bore off my youthful bride, and heaps of wealth, <br> When she had given you welcome as our guests. <br> And now ye seek to burn with fire the fleet <br> With which we cross the ocean, and to slay <br> The Grecian heroes. Ye shall yet be forced, <br> Eager for battle as ye are, to pause. <br> O Father Jupiter, who hast the praise <br> Of highest wisdom among gods and men! <br> All this is of thy ordering. How hast thou <br> Favored this arrogant crew of Troy, in love <br> With violence, who never have enough <br> Of war and all its many miseries! <br> All other things soon satisfy desire⁠— <br> Sleep, love, and song, and graceful dance, which most <br> Delight in more than warlike toils⁠—yet they <br> Of Troy are never satisfied with war.” <br> So spake the illustrious man, and, having stripped <br> The bloody armor from the dead, he gave <br> The spoil to his companions, and rejoined <br> The warriors in the van. Harpalion then, <br> A son of King Pytemenes, with whom <br> He left his home to join the war at Troy, <br> Assaulted him. He never saw again <br> His native land. Close to Atrides’ shield, <br> He struck it in the centre with his lance, <br> Yet could not drive the weapon through the brass, <br> And backward shrank, in fear of death, among <br> His comrades, looking round him lest some foe <br> Should wound him with the spear. Meriones <br> Let fly a brazen arrow after him, <br> Which, entering his right flank below the bone, <br> Passed through and cleft the bladder. Down he sank <br> Where the shaft struck him, breathing out his life <br> In the arms of his companions. Like a worm <br> He lay extended on the earth; his blood <br> Gushed forth, a purple stream, and steeped the soil. <br> The large-souled Paphlagonians came around, <br> And placed him in a chariot, sorrowing, <br> And bore him to the gates of sacred Troy. <br> The father followed weeping, but no hand <br> Was raised to avenge the slaughter of his son.</p>
<p class="verse">Yet deeply moved was Paris at his death, <br> For he had been Harpalion’s guest among <br> The Paphlagonians. Grieving for the slain, <br> He sent a brazen arrow from his bow. <br> Now there was one Euchenor, rich and brave, <br> The son of Polyidus, hoary seer; <br> His dwelling was in Corinth, and he came, <br> Forewarned and conscious of his fate, to Troy; <br> For often Polyidus, good old man, <br> Warned him that he within his palace halls <br> Should perish by a grievous malady, <br> Or else be slain by Trojan hands beside <br> The Grecian fleet. So, to escape at once <br> The censure of the Achaians and disease, <br> He came, lest he in after times might rue <br> His choice. And now between the jaw and ear <br> Did Paris smite him; from the warrior’s limbs <br> Life fled, and darkness gathered o’er his eyes.</p>
<p class="verse">And then they fought; like a devouring fire <br> That battle was; but Hector, dear to Jove, <br> Had not yet learned that on the left the Greeks <br> Made havoc of his men; for in that hour <br> The Greeks had almost made the victory theirs, <br> So greatly had the god who shakes the shores <br> Kindled their courage, and with his own arm <br> Brought timely aid. Still Hector, pressing on <br> Where first he leaped within the gates and wall, <br> Broke the close phalanxes of shielded Greeks. <br> There, ranged beside the hoary deep, the ships <br> Of Ajax and Protesilaüs lay. <br> The wall that guarded them was low, and there <br> Warriors and steeds in fiercest conflict met; <br> There the Boeotians, there in their long robes <br> The Iaonians, there the Locrians, there <br> The men of Phthia, and the Epeians famed <br> For valor, held back Hector, struggling on <br> To reach the ships, yet found they had no power <br> To drive the noble warrior from the ground, <br> For he was like a flame. The chosen men <br> Of Athens formed the van. Menestheus, son <br> Of Peteus, was their leader, after whom <br> Phidas and Stichius followed, and with them <br> The gallant Bias. Meges, Phyleus’ son, <br> With Dracius and Amphion, marshalled there <br> The Epeians; while the Phthian band were led <br> By Medon and Podarces, warlike chief. <br> And Medon was the great Oileus’ son, <br> And brother of the lesser Ajax, born <br> Without the tie of wedlock, and he dwelt <br> Far from his native land, in Phylacè; <br> For by his violent hand the brother died <br> Of Eryopis, whom Oileus made <br> His lawful spouse. Podarces was the son <br> Of Iphiclus, and dwelt in Phylacè. <br> These, at the head of Phthia’s valiant youth, <br> And cased in massive armor, fought beside <br> Boeotia’s warriors for the Grecian fleet.</p>
<p class="verse">But Ajax swift of foot, Oileus’ son, <br> From him of Telamon departed not as <br> Even for an instant. As when two black steers <br> Of equal vigor o’er a fallow draw <br> The strongly jointed plough, till near their horns <br> Streams the warm sweat; the polished yoke alone <br> Holds them asunder, as they move along <br> The furrow, and the share divides the soil <br> That lies between them;⁠—so the heroic twain <br> Kept near each other. Many men and brave <br> Followed to Troy the son of Telamon <br> As his companions, and, when weariness <br> Came o’er his sweaty limbs, relieved their chief <br> Of his broad buckler. But the Locrian host <br> Attended not Oileus’ great-souled son, <br> Nor could they ever venture to engage <br> In combat hand to hand. No brazen helms <br> Were theirs, with horse-hair plumes, no orbèd shields, <br> Nor ashen spears. They came with him to Troy, <br> Trusting in their good bows, and in their slings <br> Of twisted wool, from which they showered afar <br> Stones that dispersed the phalanxes of Troy. <br> The chieftains Ajax, warring in the van, <br> Clad in their shining armor, fought to check <br> The Trojans and their leader, brazen-mailed, <br> While in the rear the Locrians lurked unseen, <br> And sent their shafts, so that the men of Troy, <br> All order lost, were fain to cease from fight.</p>
<p class="verse">Then had the Trojans from the ships and tents <br> Turned back, and fled, with fearful loss of life, <br> To lofty Ilium, if Polydamas <br> Had not accosted valiant Hector thus:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Hector, thou hearkenest not to warning words. <br> Deem’st thou, because a god has given thee strength <br> Beyond all other men for feats of war, <br> That therefore thou art wiser than they all <br> In council? Think not for thyself to claim <br> All gifts at once. On one the god bestows <br> Prowess in war, upon another grace <br> In dance, upon another skill to touch <br> The harp and sing. In yet another, Jove <br> The Thunderer implants the prudent mind, <br> By which the many profit, and by which <br> Communities are saved; and well doth he <br> Who hath it know its worth. Now let me speak <br> What seems to me the wisest. Round thee flames <br> The encircling war; the valiant sons of Troy, <br> Since they have crossed the ramparts, stand aloof, <br> Armed as they are, or fight against large odds <br> Scattered among the galleys. Yield thou now <br> The ground, and, summoning the chiefs, decide <br> What plan to follow⁠—whether we shall storm <br> The well-oared galleys, should the God vouchsafe <br> The victory to us⁠—or else depart <br> In safety from the fleet. I greatly fear <br> The Achaians may repay to us the debt <br> Of yesterday. There yet is at the fleet <br> One who, I think, no longer will refrain <br> Wholly from battle.” Thus Polydamas <br> Spake, and the sage advice pleased Hector well, <br> Who, leaping from his chariot to the ground, <br> With all his weapons, said these wingèd words:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Remain with all the bravest warriors here, <br> Polydamas, while I depart to give <br> The due commands, and instantly return.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake, and with a shout he rushed away, <br> Seen from afar, like a snow-mountain’s peak, <br> And flew among the Trojans and allies, <br> Who crowded round the brave Polydamas, <br> The son of Panthoüs, at Hector’s call. <br> Among the foremost combatants he sought <br> Deïphobus, and mighty Helenus, <br> The king; he looked for Adamas, the son <br> Of Asius, and for Asius of the house <br> Of Hyrtacus. Some not unharmed he found, <br> Yet not o’ercome; while others lay in death <br> Beneath the galley sterns, where Grecian hands <br> Had slain them; others on the wall, struck down <br> By missiles, or in combat hand to hand. <br> There on the left of that disastrous fray <br> He met the noble Alexander, spouse <br> Of fair-haired Helen, as he cheered his men, <br> And rallied them to battle. Hector thus <br> Addressed his brother with reproachful words:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Accursed Paris! Noble but in form, <br> Effeminate seducer! Where are now <br> Deïphobus, and mighty Helenus? <br> And Adamas, the son of Asius, where? <br> And Asius, son of Hyrtacus? and where <br> Orthryoneus? Now towering Ilium sinks <br> From her high summit, and thy fate is sure.” <br> And then the godlike Paris answered thus:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Since it hath pleased thee, Hector, thus to cast <br> Reproach on me, though innocent, I may <br> Another day neglect the toils of war, <br> Although in truth my mother brought me forth <br> Not quite unapt for combat. Since the hour <br> When thou didst lead the battle to the ships <br> With thy companions, we have held our ground, <br> Here on this spot, contending with the Greeks. <br> Three chiefs for whom thou askest have been slain. <br> Deïphobus and mighty Helenus, <br> Both wounded in the hand by massive spears, <br> Have left the field; the son of Saturn saved <br> Their lives. Now lead us wheresoe’er thou wilt, <br> And we will follow thee with resolute hearts, <br> Nor deem that thou wilt find in us a lack <br> Of valor while our strength of arm remains. <br> The boldest cannot fight beyond his strength.”</p>
<p class="verse">With such persuasive words the warrior calmed <br> His brother’s anger, and they went where raged <br> The hottest conflict round Cebriones, <br> Phalces, Ortbasus, and the excellent <br> Polydamas, with Palmys at his side, <br> And Polyphoetes, godlike in his form, <br> And where Ascanius and Morys fought, <br> Sons of Hippotion. They the day before <br> Came marching from Ascania’s fertile fields, <br> Moved by the will of Jove to share the war. <br> All these swept on, as when a hurricane, <br> A thunder-gust, from Father Jupiter <br> Buffets the plain, and mingles with the deep, <br> In mighty uproar, and the billows rise <br> All over the resounding brine, and swell, <br> Whitening with foam, and chase each other on. <br> So moved the Trojans on, man after man, <br> In close array, all armed in glittering brass, <br> Following their generals. Hector, Priam’s son, <br> And peer of Mars in battle, led the van, <br> His round shield held before him, tough with hides <br> And overlaid with brass. Upon his brow <br> The gleaming helmet nodded as he moved. <br> On every side he tried the phalanxes, <br> If haply they might yield to his assault, <br> Made from beneath that buckler; but the Greeks <br> In spirit or in order wavered not. <br> And Ajax, striding forth, defied him thus:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Draw nearer, friend! Think’st thou to frighten thus <br> The Greeks? We are not quite so inexpert <br> In war, although so cruelly chastised <br> By Jupiter. Thou thinkest in thy heart <br> That thou shalt make our ships thy spoil; but we <br> Have also our strong arms to drive thee back, <br> And far more soon the populous town of Troy, <br> Captured and sacked, shall fall by Grecian hands. <br> And now I warn thee that the hour is near <br> When, fleeing, thou shalt pray to Father Jove <br> And all the immortals, that thy long-maned steeds, <br> Bearing thee townward mid a cloud of dust <br> Along the plain, may be more swift than hawks.”</p>
<p class="verse">As thus he spake, an eagle, to the right, <br> High in the middle heaven, flew over him, <br> And, gladdened by the omen, all the Greeks <br> Shouted; but then illustrious Hector spake:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Babbler and boaster, what wild words are these? <br> O Ajax! Would that I were but as sure <br> To be the child of aegis-bearing Jove, <br> Brought forth by Juno the august, and held <br> In honor everywhere like that which crowns <br> Apollo and Minerva, as I know <br> That to the Greeks this very day will bring <br> Destruction, and that thou shalt also lie <br> Slain with the others, if thou dare abide <br> The stroke of my long spear, which yet shall tear <br> Thy dainty flesh, and thou, with thy full limbs, <br> Shalt be the feast of Trojan dogs and birds, <br> Unburied by the galleys of the Greeks.”</p>
<p class="verse">So Hector spake, and led his warriors on. <br> They followed with a mighty shout; the rear <br> Sent up as loud a cry. On the other side <br> Shouted the Greeks, nor intermitted now <br> Their wonted valor, but stood firm to breast <br> The onset of the chosen men of Troy. <br> The mingled clamor of both hosts went up <br> To heaven, and to the shining seat of Jove.</p>
