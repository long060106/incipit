<p class="lede">The valor of Diomed, aided by Minerva⁠—He is wounded by Pandarus, and healed by the Goddess, who forbids him to fight with any of the immortals, save Venus⁠—His combat with Pandarus and Aeneas⁠—Pandarus slain, and Aeneas, wounded and in great danger, rescued by Venus, who in the act is wounded by Diomed, and leaves Aeneas to the care of Apollo⁠—Descent of Mars to the field in aid of Hector⁠—Return of Aeneas to the field⁠—Descent of Juno and Minerva to resist Mars, who is wounded by Diomed⁠—Return of the gods to heaven.</p>
<p class="verse">Then Pallas to Tydides Diomed <br> Gave strength and courage, that he might appear <br> Among the Achaians greatly eminent, <br> And win a glorious name. Upon his head <br> And shield she caused a constant flame to play, <br> Like to the autumnal star that shines in heaven <br> Most brightly when new-bathed in ocean tides. <br> Such light she caused to beam upon his crest <br> And shoulders, as she sent the warrior forth <br> Into the thick and tumult of the fight.</p>
<p class="verse">Among the Trojans, Dares was the priest <br> Of Vulcan, rich and blameless. His two sons <br> Were Phegeus and Idaus, trained in all <br> The arts of war. They left the host and came <br> To meet Tydides⁠—on the chariot they, <br> And he on foot; and now, as they drew near, <br> First Phegeus hurled his massive lance. It flew <br> O’er Diomed’s left shoulder and struck not. <br> Tydides cast his spear, and not in vain; <br> It smote the breast of Phegeus in the midst, <br> And dashed him from his seat. Idaeus leaped <br> To earth, and left the sumptuous car, nor dared <br> To guard the slain, yet would have met his death <br> If Vulcan had not borne him swiftly thence <br> Concealed in darkness, that he might not leave <br> The aged man, his father, desolate. <br> The son of Tydeus took the steeds, and bade <br> His comrades lead them to the fleet. Aghast <br> The valiant sons of Troy beheld the sons <br> Of Dares, one in flight, the other slain.</p>
<p class="verse">Meantime the blue-eyed Pallas took the hand <br> Of Mars, and thus addressed the fiery god:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Mars, Mars, thou slayer of men, thou steeped in blood, <br> Destroyer of walled cities! Should we not <br> Leave both the Greeks and Trojans to contend, <br> And Jove to crown with glory whom he will, <br> While we retire, lest we provoke his wrath?”</p>
<p class="verse">Thus having said, she led the violent Mars <br> From where the battle raged, and made him sit <br> Beside Scamander, on its grassy bank. <br> And then the Achaians put the sons of Troy <br> To flight: each leader slew a foe; and first <br> The king of men, Atrides, from his car <br> Struck down the huge-limbed Hodius, who was chief <br> Among the Halizonians. As he turned <br> To flee, the Achaian, smiting him between <br> The shoulders, drove the javelin through his breast. <br> Heavily clashed his armor as he fell.</p>
<p class="verse">Then by Idomeneus was Phaestus slain, <br> Son of Meonian Borus, who had come <br> From Tarna, rich in harvests. As he sprang <br> Into his car, Idomeneus, expert <br> To wield the ponderous javelin, thrust its blade <br> Through his right shoulder. From the car he fell, <br> And the dark night of death came over him. <br> The Achaian warriors following spoiled the slain.</p>
<p class="verse">The son of Atreus, Menelaus, slew <br> With his sharp spear Scamandrius, the son <br> Of Strophius, practised in the forest chase, <br> A mighty hunter. Him had Dian taught <br> To strike whatever beast the woody wild <br> Breeds on the hills; but now availed him not <br> The favor of Diana, archer-queen, <br> Nor skill to throw the javelin afar; <br> For Menelaus, mighty with the spear, <br> Followed him as he fled, and in the back <br> Smote him, between the shoulder-blades, and drave <br> The weapon through. He fell upon the ground <br> Headlong, his armor clashing as he fell. <br> And then Meriones slew Phereclus, <br> Son of Harmonius, the artificer, <br> Who knew to shape all works of rare device, <br> For Pallas loved him. It was he who built <br> The fleet for Paris⁠—cause of many woes <br> To all the Trojans and to him⁠—for ill <br> He understood the oracles of heaven. <br> Him did Meriones, pursuing long, <br> O’ertake, and, smiting him on the right hip, <br> Pierced through the part beneath the bone and near <br> The bladder. On his knees with sad lament so <br> He fell, and death involved him in its shade.</p>
<p class="verse">And then by Meges was Pedseus slain, <br> Antenor’s base-born son, whose noble wife, <br> Theano, reared him with as fond a care <br> As her own children, for her husband’s sake. <br> And now the mighty spearman, Phyleus’ son, <br> Drew near and smote him with his trenchant lance <br> Where meet the head and spine, and pierced the neck <br> Beneath the tongue; and forth the weapon came <br> Between the teeth. He fell, and in the fall <br> Gnashed with his teeth upon the cold bright blade.</p>
<p class="verse">Then did Evaemon’s son Eurypylus <br> Strike down Hypsenor, nobly born, the son <br> Of great Dolopion, Scamander’s priest, <br> Whom all the people honored as a god. <br> Evaemon’s gallant son, o’ertaking him <br> In flight; with one stroke of his falchion hewed <br> His brawny arm away. The bloody limb <br> Dropped to the ground, and the dark night of death <br> Came o’er his eyes: so cruel fate decreed.</p>
<p class="verse">Thus toiled the heroes in that stubborn fight. <br> Nor would you now have known to which array⁠— <br> Trojan or Greek⁠—Tydides might belong; <br> For through the field he rushed with furious speed, <br> Like a swollen river when its current takes <br> The torrent’s swiftness, scattering with a sweep <br> The bridges; nor can massive dikes withstand <br> Its fury, nor embankments raised to screen <br> The grassy meadows, while the rains of Jove <br> Fall heavily, and harvests, late the joy <br> Of toiling youth, are beaten to the ground. <br> Thus by Tydides the close phalanxes <br> Of Troy were scattered, nor could they endure, <br> All numerous as they were, his strong assault. <br> As Pandarus, Lycaon’s eminent son, <br> Beheld Tydides rush athwart the field, <br> Breaking the ranks, he drew his crooked bow <br> And smote the chief’s left shoulder as he came, <br> Striking the hollow corselet. The sharp point <br> Broke through, and blood came gushing o’er the mail. <br> Then called aloud Lycaon’s eminent son:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Brave Trojans, great in mastery of steeds, <br> Press on; the bravest of the Grecian host <br> Is smitten, nor, I think, can long survive <br> The grievous wound, if it be true that I, <br> At the command of Phoebus, son of Jove, <br> Have left my home upon the Lycian shore.”</p>
<p class="verse">Thus boastfully he spake; but his swift shaft <br> Slew not Tydides, who had now withdrawn. <br> And, standing by his steeds and chariot, spake <br> To Sthenelus, the son of Capaneus:⁠— <br> “Haste down, kind Sthenelus, and with thy hand <br> Draw the sharp arrow from my shoulder here.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake, and Sthenelus at once leaped down, <br> Stood by his side, and from his shoulder drew <br> The wingèd arrow deeply fixed within. <br> The blood flowed forth upon the twisted rings <br> Of mail, while Diomed, the valiant, prayed:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Hear me, O child of aegis-bearing Jove, <br> Goddess invincible! If ever thou <br> Didst aid me or my father in the heat <br> Of battle, aid me, Pallas, yet again. <br> Give me to slay this Trojan; bring him near, <br> Within my javelin’s reach, who wounded me, <br> And now proclaims⁠—the boaster⁠—that not long <br> Shall I behold the brightness of the sun.”</p>
<p class="verse">So prayed he, and Minerva heard his prayer <br> And lightened all his limbs⁠—his feet, his hands⁠— <br> And, standing near him, spake these wingèd words:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“War boldly with the Trojans, Diomed; <br> For even now I breathe into thy frame <br> The ancestral might and fearless soul that dwelt <br> In Tydeus, peerless with the steed and shield. <br> Lo! I remove the darkness from thine eyes, <br> That thou mayst well discern the gods from men; <br> And if a god should tempt thee to the fight, <br> Beware to combat with the immortal race; <br> Only, should Venus, child of Jupiter, <br> Take part in battle, wound her with thy spear.”</p>
<p class="verse">The blue-eyed Pallas spake, and disappeared; <br> And Diomed went back into the field <br> And mingled with the warriors. If before <br> His spirit moved him fiercely to engage <br> The men of Troy, a threefold courage now <br> Inspired him. As a lion who has leaped <br> Into a fold⁠—and he who guards the flock <br> Has wounded but not slain him⁠—feels his rage <br> Waked by the blow;⁠—the affrighted shepherd then <br> Ventures not near, but hides within the stalls, <br> And the forsaken sheep are put to flight, <br> And, huddling, slain in heaps, till o’er the fence <br> The savage bounds into the fields again;⁠— <br> Such was Tydides midst the sons of Troy. <br> Astynoüs first he slew, Hypenor next, <br> The shepherd of the people. One he pierced <br> High on the bosom with his brazen spear, <br> And smote the other on the collar-bone <br> With his good sword, and hewed from neck and spine <br> The shoulder. There he left the dead, and rushed <br> To Abas and to Polyeidus, sons <br> Of old Eurydamas, interpreter <br> Of visions. Ill the aged man had read <br> His visions when they joined the war. They died, <br> And Diomed, the valiant, spoiled the slain. <br> Xanthus and Thoön he encountered next, <br> The sons of Phaenops, born in his old age. <br> No other child had he, to be his heir, <br> And he was worn with length of years. These two <br> Tydides smote and took their lives, and left <br> Grief to their father and regretful cares, <br> Since he no more should welcome their return <br> From war, and strangers should divide his wealth. <br> Then smote he Chromius and Echemon, sons <br> Of Dardan Priam, in one chariot both. <br> As on a herd of beeves a lion springs <br> While midst the shrubs they browse, and breaks their necks⁠— <br> Heifer or ox⁠—so sprang he on the twain <br> And struck them, vainly struggling, from their car, <br> And spoiled them of their arms, and took their steeds, <br> And bade his comrades lead them to the fleet.</p>
<p class="verse">Aeneas, who beheld him scattering thus <br> The embattled ranks before him, straightway went <br> Through the thick fight, amid encountering spears, <br> In search of godlike Pandarus. He found <br> Lycaon’s blameless and illustrious son, <br> And stood before him, and addressed him thus:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Where is thy bow, O Pandarus, and where <br> Thy wingèd arrows? Where the old renown <br> In which no warrior here can vie with thee, <br> And none upon the Lycian shore can boast <br> That he excels thee? Hasten, and lift up <br> Thy hands in prayer to Jupiter, and send <br> An arrow at this man, whoe’er he be, <br> Who thus prevails, and thus afflicts our host, <br> And makes the knees of many a strong man weak. <br> Strike him⁠—unless he be some god incensed <br> At Troy for sacrifice withheld, since hard <br> It is to bear the anger of a god.”</p>
<p class="verse">Lycaon’s son, the far-renowned, replied:⁠— <br> “Aeneas, leader of the Trojans mailed <br> In brass, to me this man in all things seems <br> Like warlike Diomed. I know his shield, <br> High helm, and steeds, and yet I may not say <br> That this is not a god. But if he be <br> The chief of whom I speak, the warlike son <br> Of Tydeus, not thus madly would he fight, <br> Without some god to aid him. By his side <br> Is one of the immortals, with a cloud <br> About his shoulders, turning from its aim <br> The swiftly flying arrow. ’Twas but late <br> I aimed a shaft that pierced the hollow mail <br> On his left shoulder, and I thought him sent <br> To Pluto, but I slew him not. Some god <br> Must be offended with me. I have here <br> No steeds or car to mount. Far off at home <br> There stand within Lycaon’s palace-walls <br> Eleven chariots, fair and fresh and new: <br> Each has an ample cover, and by each <br> Are horses yoked in pairs, that champ their oats <br> And their white barley. When I left my home, <br> Lycaon, aged warrior, counselled me, <br> Within his sumptuous halls, that with my steeds <br> And chariot I should lead the sons of Troy <br> In the fierce battle. I obeyed him not: <br> Far better if I had. I wished to spare <br> My horses, lest, so largely fed at home, <br> They might want food in the beleaguered town. <br> So, leaving them, I came on foot to Troy, <br> Confiding in my bow, which yet was doomed <br> To avail me little, for already I <br> Have smitten with my arrows the two chiefs, <br> Tydides and Atrides, and from both <br> Drew the red blood, but only made their rage <br> To flame the fiercer. In an evil hour <br> I took my bow and quiver from the wall <br> And came to lead the Trojans for the sake <br> Of Hector. But if ever I return <br> To see my native country and my wife <br> And my tall spacious mansion, may some foe <br> Strike off my head if with these hands I fail <br> To break my bow in pieces, casting it <br> Into the flames, a useless weapon now.”</p>
<p class="verse">The Trojan chief Aeneas, answering, said:⁠— <br> “Nay, talk not so; it cannot but be thus, <br> Until upon a chariot, and with steeds, <br> We try our prowess with this man in war. <br> Haste, mount my chariot here, and thou shalt see <br> How well are Trojan horses trained to range <br> The field of battle, in the swift pursuit <br> Hither and thither, or in rapid flight; <br> And they shall bring us safely to the town <br> Should Jove a second time bestow the meed <br> Of glory on Tydides. Haste, and take <br> The lash and well-wrought reins, while I descend <br> To fight on foot; or haply thou wilt wait <br> The foe’s advance while I direct the steeds.”</p>
<p class="verse">Then spake again Lycaon’s eminent son:⁠— <br> “Keep thou the reins, Aeneas, and still guide <br> The horses. With their wonted charioteer, <br> The better shall they bear away the car <br> Should we be forced to fly before the arm <br> Of Diomed; lest, taking flight, they range <br> Unmastered when they hear thy voice no more, <br> Nor bear us from the combat, and the son <br> Of Tydeus, having slain us, shall lead thence <br> Thy firm-hoofed coursers. Therefore guide them still, <br> Them and the chariot, while, with this keen spear, <br> I wait the Greek, as he is rushing on.”</p>
<p class="verse">They spake, and, climbing the magnificent car, <br> Turned toward Tydides the swift-footed steeds. <br> The noble son of Capaneus beheld, <br> And said in wingèd words to Diomed:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Tydides Diomed, most dear of men! <br> I see two warriors, strong, immensely strong, <br> Coming to combat with thee. Pandarus <br> Is one, the skilled in archery, who boasts <br> To be Lycaon’s son; and by his side <br> There comes Aeneas, glorying that he sprang <br> From the large-souled Anchises⁠—borne to him <br> By Venus. Mount we now our car and leave <br> The ground, nor in thy fury rush along <br> The van of battle, lest thou lose thy life.”</p>
<p class="verse">The brave Tydides, with a frown, replied:⁠— <br> “Speak not of flight; thou canst not yet persuade <br> My mind to that. To skulk or shrink with fear <br> In battle ill becomes me, and my strength <br> Is unexhausted yet. It suits me not <br> To mount the chariot; I will meet the foe <br> Just as I am. Minerva will not let <br> My spirit falter. Ne’er shall those swift steeds <br> Bear the two warriors hence⁠—if even one <br> Escapes me. One thing more have I to say; <br> And keep it well in mind. Should Pallas deign⁠— <br> The wise, forecasting Pallas⁠—to bestow <br> On me the glory of o’ercoming both, <br> Stop thy swift horses, and tie fast the reins <br> To our own chariot, and make haste to seize <br> The horses of Aeneas, guiding them <br> Hence from the Trojan to the Grecian host; <br> For they are of the stock which Jupiter <br> The Thunderer gave to Tros. It was the price <br> He paid for Ganymede, and they, of all <br> Beneath the eye of morning and the sun, <br> Are of the choicest breed. The king of men, <br> Anchises, stealthily and unobserved, <br> Brought to the coursers of Laomedon <br> His brood-mare, and obtained the race. Six colts, <br> Their offspring, in his courts were foaled. Of these, <br> Four for himself he kept, and in his stalls <br> Reared them, and two of them, both apt for war, <br> He gave Aeneas. If we make them ours, <br> The exploit will bring us honor and renown.”</p>
<p class="verse">Thus they conferred. Meantime their foes drew near, <br> Urging their fiery coursers on, and first <br> Lycaon’s eminent son addressed the Greek:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“My weapon, swift and sharp, the arrow, failed <br> To slay thee; let me try the javelin now, <br> And haply that, at least, may reach its mark.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake, and, brandishing his massive spear, <br> Hurled it against the shield of Diomed. <br> The brazen point broke through, and reached the mail. <br> Then shouted with loud voice Lycaon’s son:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Ha! Thou art wounded in thy flank; my spear <br> Bites deep; nor long, I think, canst thou survive, <br> And great will be my glory gained from thee.”</p>
<p class="verse">But thus the valiant Diomed replied. <br> Incapable of fear: “Thy thought is wrong. <br> I am not wounded, and I well perceive <br> That ye will never give the conflict o’er <br> Till one of you, laid low amid the dust, <br> Pour out his blood to glut the god of war.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake, and cast his spear. Minerva kept <br> The weapon faithful to its aim. It struck <br> The nose, and near the eye; then passing on <br> Betwixt the teeth, the unrelenting edge <br> Cleft at its root the tongue; the point came out <br> Beneath the chin. The warrior from his car <br> Fell headlong; his bright armor, fairly wrought, <br> Clashed round him as he fell; his fiery steeds <br> Started aside with fright; his breath and strength <br> Were gone at once, Aeneas, with his shield <br> And his long spear, leaped down to guard the slain, <br> That the Achaians might not drag him thence. <br> There, lion-like, confiding in his strength, <br> He stalked around the corpse, and over it <br> Held his round shield and lance, prepared to slay <br> Whoever came, and shouting terribly.</p>
<p class="verse">Tydides raised a stone⁠—a mighty weight, <br> Such as no two men living now could lift; <br> But he, alone, could swing it round with ease. <br> With this he smote Aeneas on the hip, <br> Where the thigh joins its socket. By the blow <br> He brake the socket and the tendons twain, <br> And tore the skin with the rough, jagged stone. <br> The hero fell upon his knees, but stayed <br> His fall with his strong palm upon the ground; <br> And o’er his eyes a shadow came like night.</p>
<p class="verse">Then had the king of men, Aeneas, died, <br> But for Jove’s daughter, Venus, who perceived <br> His danger instantly⁠—his mother, she <br> Who bore him to Anchises when he kept <br> His beeves, a herdsman. Round her son she cast <br> Her white arms, spreading over him in folds <br> Her shining robe, to be a fence against <br> The weapons of the foe, lest some Greek knight <br> Should at his bosom aim the steel to take <br> His life. And thus the goddess bore away <br> From that fierce conflict her beloved son.</p>
<p class="verse">Nor did the son of Capaneus forget <br> The bidding of the warlike Diomed, <br> But halted his firm-footed steeds apart <br> From the great tumult, with the long reins stretched <br> And fastened to the chariot. Next, he sprang <br> To seize the horses with fair-flowing manes, <br> That drew the chariot of Aeneas. These <br> He drave away, far from the Trojan host, <br> To the well-greaved Achaians, giving them <br> In charge, to lead them to the hollow ships, <br> To his beloved friend Deipylus, <br> Whom he of all his comrades honored most, <br> As likest to himself in years and mind. <br> And then he climbed his car and took the reins, <br> And, swiftly drawn by his firm-footed steeds, <br> Followed Tydides, who with cruel steel <br> Sought Venus, knowing her unapt for war, <br> And all unlike the goddesses who guide <br> The battles of mankind, as Pallas does, <br> Or as Bellona, ravager of towns. <br> O’ertaking her at last, with long pursuit, <br> Amid the throng of warring men, the son <br> Of warlike Tydeus aimed at her his spear, <br> And wounded in her hand the delicate one <br> With its sharp point. It pierced the ambrosial robe, <br> Wrought for her by the Graces, at the spot <br> Where the palm joins the wrist, and broke the skin, <br> And drew immortal blood⁠—the ichor⁠—such <br> As from the blessed gods may flow; for they <br> Eat not the wheaten loaf, nor drink dark wine; <br> And therefore they are bloodless, and are called <br> Immortal. At the stroke the goddess shrieked, <br> And dropped her son. Apollo in his arms <br> Received and in a dark cloud rescued him, <br> Lest any of the Grecian knights should aim <br> A weapon at his breast to take his life. <br> Meantime the brave Tydides cried aloud:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Leave wars and battle, goddess. Is it not <br> Enough that thou delude weak womankind? <br> Yet, if thou ever shouldst return, to bear <br> A part in battle, thou shalt have good cause <br> To start with fear, when war is only named.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake; and she departed, wild with pain, <br> For grievously she suffered. Instantly <br> Fleet-footed Iris took her by the hand <br> And led her from the place, her heart oppressed <br> With anguish and her fair cheek deathly pale. <br> She found the fiery Mars, who had withdrawn <br> From that day’s combat to the left, and sat, <br> His spear and his swift coursers hid from sight, <br> In darkness. At his feet she fell, and prayed <br> Her brother fervently, that he would lend <br> His steeds that stood in trappings wrought of gold:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Dear brother, aid me; let me have thy steeds <br> To bear me to the Olympian mount, the home <br> Of gods, for grievously the wound I bear <br> Afflicts me. ’Twas a mortal gave the wound⁠— <br> Tydides, who would even fight with Jove.”</p>
<p class="verse">She spake; and Mars resigned to her his steeds <br> With trappings of bright gold. She climbed the car, <br> Still grieving, and, beside her, Iris took <br> Her seat, and caught the reins and plied the lash. <br> On flew the coursers, on, with willing speed, <br> And soon were at the mansion of the gods <br> On high Olympus. There the active-limbed, <br> Fleet Iris stayed them, loosed them from the car, <br> And fed them with ambrosial food. Meanwhile, <br> The goddess Venus at Dione’s feet <br> Had cast herself. The mother round her child <br> Threw tenderly her arms, and with her hand <br> Caressed her brow, and spake, and thus inquired:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Which of the dwellers of the skies, dear child, <br> Has dealt thus cruelly with thee, as one <br> Caught in the doing of some flagrant wrong?”</p>
<p class="verse">And thus did Venus, queen of smiles, reply:⁠— <br> “The son of Tydeus, arrogant Diomed, <br> Wounded me as I sought to bear away <br> From battle’s dangers my beloved son <br> Aeneas, dear beyond all other men: <br> For now no longer does the battle rage <br> Between the Greeks and Trojans, but the Greeks <br> Venture to combat even with the gods.”</p>
<p class="verse">Dione, great among the goddesses, <br> Rejoined: “Submit, my daughter, and endure, <br> Though inly grieved; for many of us who dwell <br> Upon the Olympian mount have suffered much <br> From mortals, and have brought great miseries <br> Upon each other. First, it was the fate <br> Of Mars to suffer, when Aloëus’ sons, <br> Otus and mighty Ephialtes, made <br> Their fetters fast upon his limbs. He lay <br> Chained thirteen months within a brazen cell; <br> And haply there the god, whose thirst of blood <br> Is never cloyed, had perished, but for aid <br> Which Eriboea gave, the beautiful, <br> His step-mother. She made his miseries known <br> To Mercury, who set him free by stealth, <br> Withered and weak with long imprisonment. <br> And Juno suffered when Amphitryon’s son, <br> The valiant, dared to plant in her right breast <br> A three-pronged arrow, and she writhed with pain. <br> And Pluto suffered, when the hero-son <br> Of aegis-bearing Jove, with a swift shaft, <br> Smote him beside the portals of the dead, <br> And left him filled with pain. He took his way <br> To high Olympus and the home of Jove, <br> Grieving and racked with pain, for deep the dart <br> Had pierced his brawny shoulder, torturing him. <br> There Paean with his pain-dispelling balms <br> Healed him, for he was not of mortal race. <br> O daring man and reckless, to make light <br> Of such impieties and violate <br> The sacred persons of the Olympian gods! <br> It was the blue-eyed Pallas who stirred up <br> Tydides to assail thee thus. The fool! <br> He knew not that the man who dares to meet <br> The gods in combat lives not long. No child <br> Shall prattling call him father when he comes <br> Returning from the dreadful tasks of war. <br> Let then Tydides, valiant though he be, <br> Beware lest a more potent foe than thou <br> Encounter him, and lest the nobly-born <br> Aegialeia, in some night to come⁠— <br> Wise daughter of Adrastus, and the spouse <br> Of the horse-tamer Diomed⁠—call up <br> The servants of her household from their sleep, <br> Bewailing him to whom in youth she gave <br> Her maiden troth⁠—the bravest of the Greeks.”</p>
<p class="verse">She spake, and wiped the ichor from the hand <br> Of Venus; at her touch the hand was healed <br> And the pain left it. Meantime Pallas stood, <br> With Juno, looking on, both teasing Jove <br> With words of sarcasm. Blue-eyed Pallas thus <br> Addressed the god: “O Father Jupiter, <br> Wilt thou be angry at the word I speak?⁠— <br> As Venus, wheedling some Achaian dame <br> To join the host she loves, the sons of Troy, <br> Caressed the fair, arrayed in gay attire, <br> A golden buckle scratched her tender hand.”</p>
<p class="verse">As thus she spake, the Father of the gods <br> And mortals, calling golden Venus near, <br> Said, with a smile: “Nay, daughter, not for thee <br> Are tasks of war; be gentle marriage-rites <br> Thy care; the labors of the battle-field <br> Pertain to Pallas and the fiery Mars.”</p>
<p class="verse">Thus with each other talked the gods, while still <br> The great in battle, Diomed, pursued <br> Aeneas, though he knew that Phoebus stretched <br> His arm to guard the warrior. Small regard <br> Had he for the great god, and much he longed <br> To strike Aeneas down and bear away <br> The glorious arms he wore; and thrice he rushed <br> To slay the Trojan, thrice Apollo smote <br> Upon his glittering shield. But when he made <br> The fourth assault, as if he were a god, <br> The archer of the skies, Apollo, thus <br> With menacing words rebuked him: “Diomed, <br> Beware; desist, nor think to make thyself <br> The equal of a god. The deathless race <br> Of gods is not as those who walk the earth.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake; the son of Tydeus, shrinking back, <br> Gave way before the anger of the god <br> Who sends his shafts afar. Then Phoebus bore <br> Aeneas from the tumult to the height <br> Of sacred Pergamus, where stands his fane; <br> And there Latona and the archer-queen, <br> Diana, in the temple’s deep recess, <br> Tended him and brought back his glorious strength. <br> Meantime the bowyer-god, Apollo, formed <br> An image of Aeneas, armed like him, <br> Round which the Trojans and Achaians thronged <br> With many a heavy weapon-stroke that fell <br> Upon the huge orbs of their ox-hide shields <br> And lighter bucklers. Now to fiery Mars <br> Apollo spake: “Mars, Mars, thou plague of men, <br> Thou steeped in blood, destroyer of walled towns! <br> Wilt thou not force this man to leave the field? <br> Wilt thou not meet in arms this daring son <br> Of Tydeus, who would even fight with Jove? <br> Already has he wounded, in close fight, <br> The goddess Venus at the wrist, and since <br> Assaulted me as if he were a god.”</p>
<p class="verse">He said, and on the heights of Pergamus <br> Sat down, while the destroyer Mars went forth <br> Among the embattled Trojan ranks, to rouse <br> Their valor. In the form of Acamus, <br> The gallant Thracian leader, he bespake <br> The sons of Jove-descended Priam thus:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“O sons of Priam, him who claims descent <br> From Jupiter! How long will ye submit <br> To see your people slaughtered by the Greeks? <br> Is it until the battle-storm shall reach <br> Your city’s stately portals? Even now <br> A hero whom we honor equally <br> With the great Hector, our Aeneas, son <br> Of the large-souled Anchises, is struck down. <br> Haste, let us rescue our beloved friend.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake, and into every heart his words <br> Carried new strength and courage. In that hour <br> Sarpedon chid the noble Hector thus:⁠— <br> “Where is the prowess, Hector, which was thine <br> So lately? Thou hast said that thou alone, <br> Thy kindred and thy brothers, could defend <br> The city, without armies or allies. <br> Now I see none of these; they all, like hounds <br> Before a lion, crouch and slink away, <br> While the confederates bear the brunt of war. <br> I am but an auxiliar come from far, <br> From Lycia, where the eddying Xanthus runs. <br> There left I a beloved wife, and there <br> An infant child, and large possessions, such <br> As poor men covet. Yet do I exhort <br> My Lycians to the combat, and myself <br> Would willingly engage this foe of Troy, <br> Although I here have nothing which the Greeks <br> Might bear or drive away. Thou standest still, <br> Meanwhile, nor dost thou bid the rest to keep <br> Their ground and bear the battle for their wives. <br> Yet have a care, lest, as if caught at length <br> In the strong meshes of a mighty net, <br> Ye find yourselves the captives and the prey <br> Of enemies, who quickly will destroy <br> Your nobly-peopled city. These are thoughts <br> That should engage thy mind by night and day, <br> And thou shouldst beg the chiefs of thine allies, <br> Called to thy aid from far, that manfully <br> They meet the foe, and foil his fierce attack, <br> And take the cause of this reproach away.”</p>
<p class="verse">Sarpedon spake; and Hector, all in arms, <br> Stung by his words, and leaping from his car, <br> Brandished his spears, and went among the hosts <br> And rallied them to battle. Terrible <br> The conflict that ensued. The men of Troy <br> Made head against the Greeks: the Greeks stood firm, <br> Nor ever thought of flight. As when the wind <br> Strews chaff about the sacred threshing-floors <br> While wheat is winnowed, and before the breeze <br> The yellow Ceres separates the grain <br> From its light husk, which gathers in white heaps⁠— <br> Even so the Greeks were whitened o’er with dust <br> Raised in that tumult by the horses’ hoofs <br> And rising to the brazen firmament, <br> As toward the fight the charioteers again <br> Urged on their coursers. Yet the Greeks withstood <br> The onset, and struck forward with strong arms. <br> Meantime the furious Mars involved the field <br> In darkness, to befriend the sons of Troy, <br> And went through all the ranks, and well fulfilled <br> The mandate which Apollo gave the god <br> Who wields the golden falchion, bidding him <br> Kindle the courage of the Trojan host <br> Whene’er he saw the auxiliar of the Greeks, <br> Minerva, leave the combat. Then the god <br> Brought from the sanctuary’s inner shrine <br> Aeneas⁠—filling with recovered strength <br> That shepherd of the people. He beside <br> His comrades placed himself, and they rejoiced <br> To see him living and unharmed and strong <br> As ever; yet they questioned not; their task <br> Was different, set them by the god who bears <br> The silver bow, and Mars the slayer of men, <br> And raging Strife that never is appeased.</p>
<p class="verse">The Ajaces and Ulysses and the son <br> Of Tydeus roused the Achaians to the fight. <br> For of the strength and clamor of the foe <br> They felt no fear, but calmly stood, to bide <br> The assault; as stand in air the quiet clouds <br> Which Saturn’s son upon the mountaintops <br> Piles in still volumes when the north wind sleeps, <br> And every ruder breath of blustering air <br> That drives the gathered vapors through the sky. <br> Thus calmly waited they the Trojan host, <br> Nor thought of flight. And now Atrides passed <br> In haste along their ranks, and gave command:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“O friends, be men, and let your hearts be strong, <br> And let no warrior in the heat of fight <br> Do what may bring him shame in others’ eyes; <br> For more of those who shrink from shame are safe <br> Than fall in battle, while with those who flee <br> Is neither glory nor reprieve from death.”</p>
<p class="verse">So spake the king, and hurled his spear and smote <br> Deïcoön, the son of Pergasis, <br> A chief, and a companion in the war <br> Of the great-souled Aeneas. He in Troy <br> Was honored as men honored Priam’s sons, <br> For he was ever foremost in the fight. <br> The weapon struck his shield, yet stopped not there, <br> But, breaking through its folds and through the belt, <br> Transfixed the part beneath. The Trojan fell <br> To earth, his armor clashing with his fall.</p>
<p class="verse">Aeneas slew the sons of Diodes⁠— <br> Orsilochus and Crethon, eminent Greeks. <br> Their father dwelt in Pherae nobly built, <br> Amid his riches. From Alpheius he <br> Derived his race⁠—a river whose long stream <br> Flows through the meadows of the Pylian land. <br> Orsilochus was to Alpheius born, <br> Lord over many men, and he became <br> The father of great Diocles, to whom <br> Twin sons were born, well trained in all the arts <br> Of warfare⁠—Crethon and Orsilochus. <br> These, in the prime of youth, with their black ships <br> Followed the Argives to the coast of Troy <br> Famed for its generous steeds. They left their home <br> To vindicate the honor of the sons <br> Of Atreus⁠—Agamemnon, king of men, <br> And Menelaus⁠—but they found their death.</p>
<p class="verse">As two young lions, nourished by their dam <br> Amid the thickets of some mighty wood, <br> Seizing the beeves and fattened sheep, lay waste <br> The stables, till at length themselves are slain <br> By trenchant weapons in the shepherd’s hand, <br> So by the weapons of Aeneas died <br> These twain; they fell as lofty fir-trees fall. <br> But now, when Menelaus saw their fate, <br> The mighty warrior, deeply sorrowing, rushed <br> Among the foremost, armed in glittering brass, <br> And brandishing his spear; for Mars had roused <br> His soul to fury, trusting he would meet <br> Aeneas, and would perish by his hand. <br> Antilochus, the generous Nestor’s son, <br> Came also to the van, for anxiously <br> He feared mischance might overtake the king, <br> To make the toils of their long warfare vain; <br> And there he found the combatants prepared <br> For battle, with their trusty spears in hand, <br> And standing face to face. At once he took <br> His stand beside the monarch of the Greeks. <br> At sight of the two warriors side by side, <br> All valiant as he was, Aeneas shunned <br> The encounter. They, when they had drawn the dead <br> Among the Grecian ranks, and to their friends <br> Given up the hapless brothers, turned to take <br> Their place among the foremost in the fight. <br> Then, too, Pykemenes, a chief like Mars, <br> And leader of the Paphlagonian host⁠— <br> A valiant squadron armed with shields⁠—was slain. <br> Atrides Menelaus, skilled to wield <br> The javelin, gave his death-wound. He transfixed <br> The shoulder at the collar-bone. Meanwhile <br> Antilochus against his charioteer, <br> Mydon, the brave son of Atymnias, hurled <br> A stone that smote his elbow as he wheeled <br> His firm-paced steeds in flight. He dropped the reins, <br> Gleaming with ivory as they trailed in dust. <br> Antilochus leaped forward, smiting him <br> Upon the temples with his sword. He fell <br> Gasping amidst the sand, his head immersed <br> Up to his shoulders⁠—for the sand was deep⁠— <br> And there remained till he was beaten down <br> Before the horses’ hoofs. Antilochus, <br> Lashing the horses, drave them to the Greeks.</p>
<p class="verse">Hector beheld, and, springing with loud shouts, <br> Stood mid the wavering ranks. The phalanxes <br> Of the brave Trojans followed him, for Mars <br> And terrible Bellona led them on⁠— <br> Bellona bringing Tumult in her train, <br> And Mars with brandished lance⁠—a mighty weight⁠— <br> Now stalking after Hector, now before.</p>
<p class="verse">Him when the valiant Diomed beheld, <br> He trembled; and, as one who, journeying <br> Along a way he knows not, having crossed <br> A place of drear extent, before him sees <br> A river rushing swiftly toward the deep, <br> And all its tossing current white with foam, <br> And stops and turns, and measures back his way, <br> So then did Diomed withdraw, and spake:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“O friends, how greatly must we all admire <br> This noble Hector, mighty with the spear <br> And terrible in war. There is some god <br> Forever near him, warding off the stroke <br> Of death; beside him yonder even now <br> Stands Mars in semblance of a mortal man. <br> Yield, then, and with your faces toward the foe <br> Fall back, and strive not with the gods of heaven.”</p>
<p class="verse">Even as he spake, the Trojan host drew near, <br> And Hector slew two warriors trained to arms⁠— <br> Menesthes and Anchialus⁠—who came <br> Both in one chariot to the war. Their fall <br> Ajax, the son of Telamon, beheld, <br> And pitied, and drew near, and stood, and hurled <br> His glittering spear. It smote Ampheius, son <br> Of Selagus, who, rich in lands and goods, <br> Abode in Paesus. In an evil hour <br> He joined the cause of Priam and his sons. <br> Him at the belt the spear of Ajax smote, <br> And pierced the bowels. With a crash he fell, <br> Then hastened mighty Ajax to strip off <br> The armor, but the Trojans at him cast <br> Their pointed spears that glittered as they flew, <br> And many struck his shield. He pressed his heel <br> Against the slain, and from the body drew <br> His brazen spear, but could not from the breast <br> Loose the bright mail, so thick the weapons came, <br> And such the wary dread with which he saw <br> The bravest of the Trojans closing round, <br> Many and fierce, and all with spears outstretched; <br> And he, though strong and valiant and renowned, <br> Driven from the ground, gave way to mightier force.</p>
<p class="verse">So toiled the warriors through that stubborn fight, <br> When cruel fate urged on Tlepoemus, <br> The great and valiant son of Hercules, <br> To meet Sarpedon, mighty as a god. <br> And now as each to each advanced⁠—the son <br> And grandson of the cloud-compeller Jove⁠— <br> Thus first Tlepolemus addressed his foe:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Sarpedon, Lycian monarch, what has brought <br> Thee hither, trembling thus, and inexpert <br> In battle? Lying flatterers are they <br> That call thee son of Jupiter who bears <br> The aegis; for unlike the heroes thou, <br> Born to the Thunderer in times of old, <br> Nor like my daring father, Hercules <br> The lion-hearted, who once came to Troy <br> To claim the coursers of Laomedon. <br> With but six ships, and warriors but a few, <br> He laid the city waste and made its streets <br> A desolation. Thou art weak of heart, <br> And round thee are thy people perishing; <br> Yet, even wert thou brave, thy presence here <br> From Lycia’s coast would prove of small avail <br> To Troy; for, slain in combat here by me, <br> Thou to the gates of Hades shalt go down.”</p>
<p class="verse">Sarpedon, leader of the Lycians, thus <br> Made answer: “True it is, Tlepolemus, <br> That he laid waste the sacred city of Troy <br> For the base dealings of Laomedon, <br> The monarch who with railing words repaid <br> His great deservings, and kept back the steeds <br> For which he came so far. But thou⁠—thy fate <br> Is slaughter and black death from this my spear; <br> And fame will come to me, and one more soul <br> Go down to Hades.” As Sarpedon spake, <br> Tlepolemus upraised his ashen spear, <br> And from the hands of both the chiefs at once <br> Their massive weapons flew. Sarpedon smote <br> Full in the throat his foe; the cruel point <br> Passed through the neck, and night came o’er his eyes. <br> Tlepolemus, in turn, on the left thigh <br> Had struck Sarpedon with his ponderous lance. <br> The weapon, cast with vigorous hand and arm, <br> Pierced deep, and touched the bone; but Jupiter <br> Averted from his son the doom of death.</p>
<p class="verse">His noble comrades raised and bore away <br> The great Sarpedon from the battle-field, <br> Trailing the long spear with them. Bitter pain <br> It gave him; in their haste they marked it not, <br> Nor thought to draw the ashen weapon forth, <br> That he might mount the car; so eagerly <br> His anxious bearers hurried from the war.</p>
<p class="verse">On the other side the well-armed Greeks took up <br> The slain Tlepolemus, to bear him thence. <br> The great Ulysses, large of soul, beheld, <br> And felt his spirit moved, as anxiously <br> He pondered whether to pursue the son <br> Of Jove the Thunderer, or turn and take <br> The life of many a Lycian. Yet to slay <br> Jove’s mighty son was not his destiny, <br> And therefore Pallas moved him to engage <br> The crowd of Lycian warriors. Then he slew <br> Coeranus and Alastor, Chromius, <br> Alcander, Halius, and Prytanis <br> Noëmon; and yet more the noble Greek <br> Had slain, if crested Hector, mighty chief, <br> Had not perceived the havoc and, arrayed <br> In shining armor, hurried to the van <br> Of battle, carrying terror to the hearts <br> Of the Achaians. As he saw him near, <br> Sarpedon was rejoiced, yet sadly said:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“O son of Priam, leave me not a prey <br> To these Achaians. Aid me, let me breathe <br> My latest breath in Troy, since I no more <br> Can hope, returning to my native land, <br> To gladden my dear wife and little son.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake, and crested Hector answered not, <br> Still pressing forward, eager to drive back <br> The Greeks in quick retreat, and take the life <br> Of many a foe. Then did the noble band <br> Who bore the great Sarpedon lay him down <br> Beneath a shapely beech, a tree of Jove <br> The Aegis-bearer. There stout Pelagon, <br> His well-beloved comrade, from his thigh <br> Drew forth the sharp blade of the ashen spear. <br> Then the breath left him, and his eyes were closed <br> In darkness; but the light came back again <br> As, breathing over him, the fresh north wind <br> Revived the spirit in his laboring breast.</p>
<p class="verse">But not for Mars nor Hector mailed in brass <br> Fled the Achaians to their fleet; nor yet <br> Advanced they on the foe, but step by step <br> Gave way before him, for they had perceived <br> The god of war was with the sons of Troy.</p>
<p class="verse">Whom first, whom last did Hector, Priam’s son, <br> And iron Mars lay low? The godlike chief <br> Teuthras, and⁠—great among the Grecian knights⁠— <br> Orestes, and the Aetolian Trechus, famed <br> As spearman, and Oenomaüs, and the son <br> Of Oenops, Helemes, and after these <br> Belted Oresbius, who in Hyla made <br> His home, intent on gathering wealth beside <br> The Lake Cephissus, on whose borders dwelt <br> Boeotians many, lords of fertile lands.</p>
<p class="verse">The white-armed goddess Juno, when she saw <br> The Argives falling in that cruel fray, <br> Addressed Minerva with these wingèd words:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“O thou unconquerable goddess, born <br> To Jove the Aegis-bearer! What is this? <br> It was an idle promise that we made <br> To Menelaus, that he should behold <br> Troy, with its strong defences, overthrown, <br> And reach his home again, if thus we leave <br> Mars the destroyer to his ravages. <br> Come, let us bring our friends effectual aid.”</p>
<p class="verse">So spake she, and her bidding was obeyed <br> By blue-eyed Pallas. Juno the august, <br> Daughter of mighty Saturn, laid in haste <br> The harness, with its ornaments of gold, <br> Upon the horses. Hebe rolled the wheels, <br> Each with eight spokes, and joined them to the ends <br> Of the steel axle⁠—fellies wrought of gold, <br> Bound with a brazen rim to last for aye⁠— <br> A wonder to behold. The hollow naves <br> Were silver, and on gold and silver cords <br> Was slung the chariot’s seat; in silver hooks <br> Rested the reins, and silver was the pole <br> Where the fair yoke and poitrels, all of gold, <br> Were fastened. Juno, eager for the strife, <br> Led the swift-footed steeds beneath the yoke.</p>
<p class="verse">Then Pallas, daughter of the god who bears <br> The aegis, on her father’s palace-floor <br> Let fall in dainty folds her flowing robe <br> Of many colors, wrought by her own hand, <br> And, putting on the mail of Jupiter <br> The Cloud-compeller, stood arrayed in arms <br> For the stern tasks of war. Her shoulder bore <br> The dreadful aegis with its shaggy brim <br> Bordered with Terror. There was Strife, and there <br> Was Fortitude, and there was fierce Pursuit, <br> And there the Gorgon’s head, a ghastly sight, <br> Deformed and dreadful, and a sign of woe <br> When borne by Jupiter. Upon her head <br> She placed a golden helmet with four crests <br> And fair embossed, of strength that might withstand <br> The armed battalions of a hundred towns; <br> Then stepped into her shining car, and took <br> Her massive spear in hand, heavy and huge, <br> With which whole ranks of heroes are o’erthrown <br> Before the daughter of the Mighty One <br> Incensed against them. Juno swung the lash <br> And swiftly urged the steeds. Before their way, <br> On sounding hinges, of their own accord, <br> Flew wide the gates of heaven, which evermore <br> The Hours are watching⁠—they who keep the mount <br> Olympus and the mighty heaven, with power <br> To open or to close their cloudy veil. <br> Thus through the gates they drave the obedient steeds, <br> And found Saturnius, where he sat apart <br> From other gods, upon the loftiest height <br> Of many-peaked Olympus. Juno there, <br> The white-armed goddess, stayed her chariot-wheels, <br> And, thus accosting Jove, she questioned him:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“O Father Jupiter, does not thy wrath <br> Rise at those violent deeds of Mars? Thou seest <br> How many of the Achaians he has slain, <br> And what brave men. Nay, thus it should not be. <br> Great grief is mine; but Venus and the god <br> Phoebus, who bears the silver bow, rejoice <br> To see this lawless maniac range the field, <br> And urge him on. O Father Jupiter, <br> Wilt thou be angry with me if I drive <br> Mars, sorely wounded, from the battle-field?”</p>
<p class="verse">The cloud-compelling Jupiter replied:⁠— <br> “Thou hast my leave; but send to encounter him <br> Pallas the spoiler, who has many a time <br> Brought grievous troubles on the god of war.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake, and white-armed Juno instantly <br> Obeyed him. With the scourge she lashed the steeds, <br> And not unwillingly they flew between <br> Earth and the starry heaven. As much of space <br> As one who gazes on the dark-blue deep <br> Sees from the headland summit where he sits⁠— <br> Such space the coursers of immortal breed <br> Cleared at each bound they made with sounding hoofs; <br> And when they came to Ilium and its streams, <br> Where Simoïs and Scamander’s channels meet, <br> The white-armed goddess Juno stayed their speed, <br> And loosed them from the yoke, and covered them <br> With darkness. Simoïs ministered, meanwhile, <br> The ambrosial pasturage on which they fed.</p>
<p class="verse">On went the goddesses, with step as light <br> As timid doves, and hastened toward the field <br> To aid the Achaian army. When they came <br> Where fought the bravest warriors in a throng <br> Around the great horse-tamer Diomed, <br> Like ravenous lions or wild boars whose rage <br> Is terrible, the white-armed goddess stood, <br> And called aloud⁠—for now she wore the form <br> Of gallant Stentor, in whose brazen voice <br> Was heard a shout like that of fifty men:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Shame on you, Argives⁠—wretches, who in form, <br> And form alone, are heroes. While we yet <br> Had great Achilles in the war, the men <br> Of Ilium dared not pass beyond their gates, <br> So much they feared his mighty spear; but now <br> They push the battle to our hollow ships, <br> Far from the town.” As thus the goddess spake, <br> New strength and courage woke in every breast.</p>
<p class="verse">Then blue-eyed Pallas hastened to the son <br> Of Tydeus. By his steeds she found the king, <br> And by his chariot, as he cooled the wound <br> Made by the shaft of Pandarus. The sweat <br> Beneath the ample band of his round shield <br> Had weakened him, and weary was his arm. <br> He raised the band, and from the wounded limb <br> Wiped off the clotted blood. The goddess laid <br> Her hand upon the chariot-yoke, and said:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Tydeus hath left a son unlike himself; <br> For he, though low in stature, was most brave; <br> And when he went, an envoy and alone, <br> To Thebes, the populous Cadmean town, <br> And I, enjoining him to keep aloof <br> From wars and rash encounters, bade him sit <br> Quietly at the feasts in palace-halls, <br> Still, to his valiant temper true, he gave <br> Challenges to the Theban youths, and won <br> The prize with ease in all their games, such aid <br> I gave him. Now I stand by thee in turn, <br> Protect thee, and exhort thee manfully <br> To fight against the Trojans; but today <br> Either the weariness of toil unnerves <br> Thy frame, or withering fear besets thy heart. <br> Henceforth we cannot deem thee, as of late, <br> The offspring of Oenides skilled in war.”</p>
<p class="verse">And then the valiant Diomed replied:⁠— <br> “I know thee, goddess, daughter of great Jove <br> The Aegis-bearer; therefore will I speak <br> Freely and keep back nothing. No base fear <br> Unmans me, nor desire of ease; but well <br> I bear in mind the mandate thou hast given. <br> Thou didst forbid me to contend with gods, <br> Except that if Jove’s daughter, Venus, joined <br> The battle, I might wound her with my spear. <br> But now I have withdrawn, and given command <br> That all the Greeks come hither; for I see <br> That Mars is in the field and leads the war.”</p>
<p class="verse">Again the blue-eyed Pallas, answering, said:⁠— <br> “Tydides Diomed, most dear of men, <br> Nay, fear thou nothing from this Mars, nor yet <br> From any other of the gods; for I <br> Will be thy sure defence. First urge thy course <br> Full against Mars, with thy firm-footed steeds. <br> Engage him hand to hand; respect him not, <br> The fiery, frantic Mars, the unnatural plague <br> Of man, the fickle god, who promised me <br> And Juno, lately, to take part with us <br> Against the Trojans and befriend the Greeks. <br> Now he forgets, and joins the sons of Troy.”</p>
<p class="verse">She spake, and laid her hand on Sthenelus, <br> To draw him from the horses; instantly <br> He leaped to earth; the indignant deity <br> Took by the side of Diomed her place; <br> The beechen axle groaned beneath the weight <br> Of that great goddess and that man of might. <br> Then Pallas seized the lash and caught the reins, <br> And, urging the firm-footed coursers, drave <br> Full against Mars, who at that moment slew <br> Huge Periphas, the mightiest one of all <br> The Aetolian band⁠—Ochesius’ famous son. <br> While bloody-handed Mars was busy yet <br> About the slain, Minerva hid her face <br> In Pluto’s helmet, that the god might fail <br> To see her. As that curse of humankind <br> Beheld the approach of noble Diomed, <br> He left the corpse of Periphas unspoiled <br> Where he had fallen, and where he breathed his last, <br> And came in haste to meet the Grecian knight. <br> And now, when they were near, and face to face, <br> Mars o’er the chariot-yoke and horses’ reins <br> First hurled his brazen spear, in hope to take <br> His enemy’s life; but Pallas with her hand <br> Caught it and turned it, so that it flew by <br> And gave no wound. The valiant Diomed <br> Made with his brazen spear the next assault, <br> And Pallas guided it to strike the waist <br> Where girded by the baldric. In that part <br> She wounded Mars, and tore the shining skin, <br> And drew the weapon back. The furious god <br> Uttered a cry as of nine thousand men, <br> Or of ten thousand, rushing to the fight. <br> The Greeks and Trojans stood aghast with fear, <br> To hear that terrible cry of him whose thirst <br> Of bloodshed never is appeased by blood.</p>
<p class="verse">As when, in time of heat, the air is filled <br> With a black shadow from the gathering clouds <br> And the strong-blowing wind, so furious Mars <br> Appeared to Diomed, as in a cloud <br> He rose to the broad heaven and to the home <br> Of gods on high Olympus. Near to Jove <br> He took his seat in bitter grief, and showed <br> The immortal blood still dropping from his wound, <br> And thus, with wingèd words, complaining said:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“O Father Jupiter! Does not thy wrath <br> Rise at these violent deeds? ’Tis ever thus <br> That we, the gods, must suffer grievously <br> From our own rivalry in favoring man; <br> And yet the blame of all this strife is thine, <br> For thou hast a mad daughter, ever wrong, <br> And ever bent on mischief. All the rest <br> Of the immortals dwelling on this mount <br> Obey thee and are subject to thy will. <br> Her only thou hast never yet restrained <br> By word or act, but dost indulge her freaks <br> Because the pestilent creature is thy child. <br> And now she moves the insolent Diomed <br> To raise his hand against the immortal gods. <br> And first he wounded Venus in the wrist, <br> Contending hand to hand; and then he sought <br> To encounter me in arms, as if he were <br> The equal of a god. My own swift feet <br> Carried me thence, else might I long have lain, <br> In anguish, under heaps of carcasses, <br> Or helplessly been mangled by his sword.”</p>
<p class="verse">The Cloud-compeller, Jove, replied, and frowned: <br> “Come not to me, thou changeling, to complain. <br> Of all the gods upon the Olympian mount <br> I like thee least, who ever dost delight <br> In broils and wars and battles. Thou art like <br> Thy mother Juno, headstrong and perverse. <br> Her I can scarcely rule by strict commands, <br> And what thou sufferest now, I deem, is due <br> To her bad counsels. Yet ’tis not my will <br> That thou shouldst suffer longer, who dost share <br> My lineage, whom thy mother bore to me. <br> But wert thou born, destroyer as thou art, <br> To any other god, thou hadst long since <br> Lain lower than the sons of Uranus.”</p>
<p class="verse">So spake he, and to Paeon gave command <br> To heal the wound; and Paeon bathed the part <br> With pain-dispelling balsams, and it healed; <br> For Mars was not to die. As, when the juice <br> Of figs is mingled with white milk and stirred, <br> The liquid gathers into clots while yet <br> It whirls with the swift motion, so was healed <br> The wound of violent Mars. Then Hebe bathed <br> The god, and robed him richly, and he took <br> His seat, delighted, by Saturnian Jove.</p>
<p class="verse">Now, having forced the curse of nations, Mars <br> To pause from slaughter, Argive Juno came, <br> With Pallas, her invincible ally, <br> Back to the mansion of imperial Jove.</p>
