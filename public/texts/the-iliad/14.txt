<p class="lede">Consultation of Agamemnon with Nestor, Diomed, and Ulysses⁠—Proposal of Agamemnon to withdraw from Troy by night opposed by Ulysses⁠—Visit made by these wounded chiefs to the battlefield, in order to encourage the army⁠—The cestus of Venus borrowed by Juno, who decoys Jupiter to her chamber, where he falls asleep⁠—Neptune meanwhile actively aids the Greeks, who commit great slaughter⁠—Hector wounded by Ajax.</p>
<p class="verse">The mighty uproar was not unperceived <br> By Nestor’s ear, who, sitting at the wine, <br> Addressed the son of Aesculapius thus:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Noble Machaon, what will happen now? <br> Bethink thee: for the clamor grows more loud <br> From our young warriors at the ships. Stay here <br> And drink the purple wine, while for thy limbs <br> The fair-haired Hecamede warms the bath <br> And washes the dark blood away, and I <br> Will climb the watch-tower, and will know the worst.</p>
<p class="verse">He spake, and took a buckler, fairly wrought, <br> Glittering with brass, and left within the tent <br> By Thrasymedes, his own knightly son, <br> Who to the war had borne his father’s shield; <br> He grasped a ponderous spear, with brazen blade, <br> And stood without the tent, and saw a sight <br> Of shame⁠—the routed Greeks, and close behind <br> The haughty Trojans putting them to flight, <br> And the Greek wall o’erthrown. As when the face <br> Of the great deep grows dark with weltering waves, <br> That silently forbode the swift descent <br> Of the shrill blast, the yet uncertain seas <br> Roll not to either side, till from the seat <br> Of Jupiter comes down the violent wind⁠— <br> So paused the aged chief, uncertain yet <br> Of purpose⁠—whether he should join the throng <br> Of Greeks, with their swift coursers, or repair <br> To sovereign Agamemnon, Atreus’ son. <br> This to his thought seemed wiser, and he went <br> To seek Atrides. Meantime both the hosts <br> Urged on the work of slaughter; still they fought, <br> And still the solid brass upon their limbs <br> Rang, smitten with the swords and two-edged spears.</p>
<p class="verse">Then, coming from the fleet, the wounded kings, <br> Nurslings of Jove, met Nestor; toward him came <br> Tydides, and Ulysses, and the son <br> Of Atreus, Agamemnon. On the beach <br> Of the gray deep their ships were ranged afar <br> From that fierce conflict. There the Greeks had drawn, <br> To the plain’s edge, the first that touched the land, <br> And built a rampart at their sterns. Though long <br> The shore-line, it sufficed not to contain <br> The galleys, and the host had scanty room; <br> Wherefore they drew the galleys up in rows, <br> Row behind row, and filled the shore’s wide mouth <br> Between the promontories. There the kings <br> Walked, leaning on their lances, to behold <br> The tumult and the fight, and inly grieved. <br> The sight of aged Nestor startled them, <br> And thus the royal Agamemnon spake:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Neleian Nestor, glory of the Greeks, <br> Why hast thou left the murderous fray, and why <br> Come hither? Much I fear the fiery chief, <br> Hector, will make the menace good which once <br> He uttered, speaking to the men of Troy⁠— <br> Not to return to Ilium from the fleet <br> Till he had burned our ships with fire, and slain <br> Us also; thus he spake, and now fulfils <br> His menace. O ye gods! The other Greeks, <br> And not Achilles only, cherish hate <br> Against me in their hearts, and now refuse <br> To combat even where our galleys lie.”</p>
<p class="verse">And Nestor, the Gerenian knight, replied:⁠— <br> “Thus is the threat accomplished, nor can Jove <br> The Thunderer reverse the event. The wall <br> In which we trusted as impregnable, <br> Our fleet’s defence and ours, is overthrown; <br> But obstinately still the Greeks maintain <br> The combat at the ships, nor couldst thou now <br> Distinguish with thy sharpest sight where most <br> The ranks are routed, so confusedly <br> They fall, and the wild uproar reaches heaven. <br> Meantime consult we what may yet be done, <br> If counsel aught avail; yet can I not <br> Advise to mingle in the strife again. <br> It is not meet that wounded men should fight.”</p>
<p class="verse">And then the royal Agamemnon said:⁠— <br> “Since at our ships, beneath their very sterns, <br> The combat rages; since the wall we built <br> Avails not, nor the trench, at which the Greeks <br> Labored and suffered, hoping it might be <br> A sure defence for us and for our fleet, <br> Certain it is that to Almighty Jove <br> It hath seemed good that here the Greeks, afar <br> From Argos, should be shamefully cut off; <br> For well was I aware when he designed <br> To aid the Greeks, and well can I perceive <br> That he is honoring now the men of Troy <br> Like to the blessed gods, and fettering <br> Our valor and our hands. Hear my advice, <br> And follow it. Let us draw down the ships <br> Nearest the sea, and launch them on the deep, <br> And moor them, anchored, till the lonely night <br> Shall come, when, if the Trojans pause from war, <br> Haply we may draw down the other barques; <br> For he who flees from danger, even by night, <br> Deserves no blame; and better is his fate <br> Who flees from harm than his whom harm o’ertakes.”</p>
<p class="verse">Then wise Ulysses, with stern look, replied:⁠— <br> “What words, Atrides, have escaped thy lips? <br> Unhappy man, thou shouldst have held command <br> O’er some effeminate army, and not ours⁠— <br> Ours to whom Jupiter, from youth to age, <br> Hath granted to accomplish difficult wars, <br> Until we pass away. And wouldst thou then <br> Depart from Troy, the city of broad streets, <br> For which we have endured so much and long? <br> Nay, be thou silent, lest the other Greeks <br> Hear words that never should be said by one <br> Who knows to speak with wisdom, and who bears <br> The sceptre, and who rules so many Greeks <br> As thou dost. I contemn with my whole soul <br> The counsel thou hast given, commanding us, <br> While yet the battle rages, to draw down <br> Our good ships to the sea, that so the foe <br> May see his wish more easily fulfilled, <br> Even in the hour of triumph, and our fate <br> Be certain ruin; for the Greeks no more <br> Will combat when they draw their galleys down, <br> But, looking backward to the shore, will leave <br> The battle there; and thus, O king of men! <br> Will mischief flow from what thou counsellest.”</p>
<p class="verse">And Agamemnon, king of men, rejoined:⁠— <br> “Thou touchest me, Ulysses, to the heart <br> With thy harsh censure; yet I did not give <br> Command to drag our good ships to the sea, <br> Against the will of the Greeks. And would there were <br> Some other, young or old, to counsel them <br> More prudently, for that would please me well.”</p>
<p class="verse">Then spake the great in battle, Diomed:⁠— <br> “The man is here, nor have ye far to look <br> If ye will be persuaded, and refrain <br> To blame me angrily, because my years <br> Are fewest midst you all. I too can boast <br> Of noble birth; my father, Tydeus, lies <br> Buried beneath a mound of earth at Thebes. <br> To Portheus three illustrious sons were born, <br> Who dwelt in Pleuron, and in Calydon <br> The lofty⁠—Agrius, Melas, and the knight, <br> My father’s father, Oeneus, eminent <br> Among the rest for valor; he remained <br> At home, but, wandering thence, my father went <br> To Argos, for the will of Jove was such⁠— <br> Jove and the other gods. He wedded there <br> A daughter of Adrastus, and he dwelt <br> Within a mansion filled with wealth; broad fields <br> Fertile in corn were his, and many rows <br> Of trees and vines around him; large his flocks, <br> And great his fame as one expert to wield, <br> Beyond all other Greeks, the spear in war. <br> This should ye know, for this is true; nor yet <br> Contemn my counsel given with careful thought <br> And for your good, nor deem it comes from one <br> Unwarlike and low-born. Now let us join <br> The battle, wounded as we are, for much <br> It needs our presence, keeping carefully <br> Beyond the reach of weapons, to avoid <br> Wound upon wound, and, cheering on the rest, <br> Send back into the combat those who stand <br> Apart, indulgent to their weariness.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake: they hearkened, and with hasty steps <br> Went on, King Agamemnon at their head.</p>
<p class="verse">Nor was the glorious power that shakes the earth <br> Unmindful of his charge. He went among <br> The warriors in the semblance of a man <br> Stricken in years, and, seizing the right hand <br> Of Agamemnon, spake these wingèd words:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“O son of Atreus, the revengeful heart <br> Of Peleus’ son must leap within his breast <br> For joy, to see the slaughter and the rout <br> Of the Achaians, since with him there dwells <br> No touch of pity. May he perish too, <br> Like us, and may some god o’erwhelm his name <br> With infamy. With thee the blessed gods <br> Are not so far incensed, and thou shalt see <br> The Trojan chiefs and princes of their host <br> Raising the dust-clouds on the spacious plain <br> In fleeing from our ships and tents to Troy.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake, and, shouting, strode across the field. <br> As loud a cry as from nine thousand men, <br> Or from ten thousand hurrying to engage <br> In battle, such the cry that ocean’s king <br> Uttered from his deep lungs. It woke anew <br> Invincible resolve in every heart <br> Among the Greeks to combat to the end.</p>
<p class="verse">Now, Juno of the golden throne beheld <br> As, standing on the Olympian height, she cast <br> Downward her eyes to where her brother moved, <br> Bearing his part with glory in the fray; <br> And inly she rejoiced. She also saw <br> Jove on the peak of Ida, down whose side <br> Glide many brooks, and greatly was displeased. <br> Then the majestic goddess with large eyes <br> Mused how to occupy the mind of him <br> Who bears the aegis. This at length seemed best: <br> To deck herself in fair array, and haste <br> To Ida, that the God might haply yield <br> To amorous desire, and in that hour <br> Her hand might pour into his lids, and o’er <br> His watchful mind, a soft and pleasant sleep. <br> She went to her own chamber, which her son <br> Vulcan had framed, with massive portals made <br> Fast to the lintels by a secret bolt, <br> Which none but she could draw. She entered in <br> And closed the shining doors; and first she took <br> Ambrosial water, washing every stain <br> From her fair limbs, and smoothed them with rich oil, <br> Ambrosial, soft, and fragrant, which, when touched <br> Within Jove’s brazen halls, perfumed the air <br> Of earth and heaven. When thus her shapely form <br> Had been anointed, and her hands had combed <br> Her tresses, she arranged the lustrous curls, <br> Ambrosial, beautiful, that clustering hung <br> Round her immortal brow. And next she threw <br> Around her an ambrosial robe, the work <br> Of Pallas, all its web embroidered o’er <br> With forms of rare device. She fastened it <br> Over the breast with clasps of gold, and then <br> She passed about her waist a zone which bore <br> Fringes an hundred-fold, and in her ears <br> She hung her three-gemmed ear-rings, from whose gleam <br> She won an added grace. Around her head <br> The glorious goddess drew a flowing veil, <br> Just from the loom, and shining like the sun; <br> And, last, beneath her bright white feet she bound <br> The shapely sandals. Gloriously arrayed <br> In all her ornaments, she left her bower, <br> And calling Venus to herself, apart <br> From all the other gods, addressed her thus:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Wilt thou, dear child, comply with what I ask? <br> Or, angered that I aid the Greeks, while thou <br> Dost favor Troy, wilt thou deny my suit?”</p>
<p class="verse">And thus Jove’s daughter, Venus, made reply:⁠— <br> “O Juno, whom I reverence, speak thy thought, <br> Daughter of mighty Saturn! For my heart <br> Commands me to obey thy wish in all <br> That I can do, and all that can be done.”</p>
<p class="verse">And thus imperial Juno, planning guile, <br> Rejoined: “Give me the charm and the desire <br> With which thou overcomest gods and men. <br> I go to the far end of this green earth, <br> To visit Ocean, father of the gods, <br> And Mother Tethys, who, receiving me <br> From Rhea, cherished me, and brought me up <br> In their abodes, when Jove the Thunderer <br> Cast Saturn down to lie beneath the earth <br> And barren sea. I go to visit them, <br> And end their hateful quarrel. For too long <br> Have they been strangers to the marriage-bed. <br> But if my words persuade them, and bring back <br> Their hearts to their old love, my name will be <br> Honored by them, and dear throughout all time.”</p>
<p class="verse">And laughter-loving Venus answered thus:⁠— <br> “What thou desirest should not be denied, <br> And shall not, for thou sleepest in the arms <br> Of Jupiter, the mightiest of the gods.”</p>
<p class="verse">She spake, and from her bosom drew the zone, <br> Embroidered, many-colored, and instinct <br> With every winning charm⁠—with love, desire, <br> Dalliance, and gentle speech⁠—that stealthily <br> O’ercomes the purpose of the wisest mind, <br> And, placing it in Juno’s hands, she said:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“This many-colored zone, and all that dwells <br> Within it, take, and in thy bosom hide, <br> And thou, I deem, wilt not return and leave <br> Thy purpose unfulfilled.” As thus she spake, <br> The large-eyed stately Juno smiled and took, <br> And, smiling, in her bosom placed the zone, <br> While Venus, daughter of the Thunderer, <br> Went to the palace. Juno took her way <br> From high Olympus o’er Pieria’s realm <br> And rich Emathia, o’er equestrian Thrace, <br> With snowy peaks exceeding high; her feet <br> Touched not the ground. From Athos suddenly <br> She stooped upon the tossing deep, and came <br> To Lemnos, seat of Thoas the divine. <br> And there she met Death’s brother, Sleep, and took <br> His hand in hers, and thus accosted him:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“O Sleep, whose sway is over all the gods <br> And all mankind, if ever thou didst heed <br> My supplication, hearken to me now, <br> And I shall be forever grateful. Close <br> The glorious eyes of Jove beneath his lids <br> Midst our embracings, and for thy reward <br> Thou shalt possess a sumptuous throne of gold <br> Imperishable. Vulcan, my lame son, <br> Shall forge it for thee, and adorn its sides, <br> And place below a footstool, upon which <br> Thy shining feet shall rest in banqueting.”</p>
<p class="verse">Then gentle Sleep made answer, speaking thus:⁠— <br> “Great Saturn’s daughter, Juno the august, <br> On any other of the deathless gods <br> Could I bring slumber⁠—even on the tides <br> Of the swift Ocean, parent of them all; <br> Yet may I not approach Saturnian Jove <br> If he command me not. Already once <br> He made me quail with fright before his threats, <br> When his magnanimous son, Alcides, sailed <br> From Troy, which he had ravaged. Then I lulled <br> The senses of the Aegis-bearer, Jove, <br> Wrapping myself around him, while thy mind <br> Was planning mischiefs for his son, and thou <br> Didst wake the blasts of all the bitter winds <br> To sweep the ocean, and to bear away <br> The hero on its billows from his friends <br> To populous Cos. When Jupiter awoke <br> His anger rose; he seized and flung the gods <br> Hither and thither; me he chiefly sought, <br> And would have cast me to destruction, down <br> From the great heavens into the deep, if Night, <br> Whose power o’ercomes the might of gods and men, <br> Had not preserved me, fleeing to her shade. <br> So Jove refrained, indignant as he was, <br> For much he feared to offend the swift-paced Night. <br> And now thou bid’st me tempt my fate again.”</p>
<p class="verse">Imperial, large-eyed Juno thus rejoined:⁠— <br> “Why rise such thoughts, O Sleep, within thy heart? <br> Deem’st thou that Jove the Thunderer favors Troy <br> As much as he was angered for the sake <br> Of Hercules, his son? Do what I ask, <br> And thou shalt have from me a wedded spouse. <br> One of the younger Graces shall be thine⁠— <br> Pasithea, whom thou hast desired so long.”</p>
<p class="verse">She spake, and Sleep, delighted, answered thus:⁠— <br> “Swear now to me, O goddess, by the Styx, <br> The inviolable river. Lay one hand <br> Upon the food-producing earth, and place <br> The other on the glimmering sea, that all <br> The gods below, round Saturn, may attest <br> Thy promise⁠—that thou wilt bestow on me <br> One of the younger Graces for my bride⁠— <br> Pasithea, whom I have desired so long.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake, and white-armed Juno willingly <br> Complied; she took the oath, and called on all <br> The gods who dwell in Tartarus below, <br> And bear the name of Titans. When the oath <br> Was taken, and the accustomed rites performed, <br> From Lemnos and from Imbrus forth they went, <br> Shrouded in mist; and swiftly moving on <br> Toward Ida, seamed with rivulets and nurse <br> Of savage beasts, they came to Lectos first, <br> And there they left the sea. Their way was now <br> Over the land, and underneath their feet <br> The forest summits shook. Sleep halted there <br> Ere yet the eye of Jupiter descried <br> His coming, and upon a lofty fir, <br> The tallest growing on the Idaean mount, <br> High in the air among the clouds of heaven, <br> Springing from earth, he took his perch within <br> The screen of branches, like the shrill-voiced bird, <br> Called Chalcis by the immortals, and by men <br> Cymindis, haunting the high mountain-side.</p>
<p class="verse">And Juno hastened on to Gargarus, <br> The peak of lofty Ida. Jupiter, <br> The Cloud-compeller, saw her, and at once <br> Love took possession of his mighty heart, <br> As when they first were wedded, and withdrew <br> From their dear parents’ sight. The God drew near <br> And stood before her, and addressed her thus:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Why art thou hastening from Olympus thus, <br> And whither; yet without thy steeds and car?”</p>
<p class="verse">And Juno answered with dissembled guile:⁠— <br> “To the far ends of the green earth I go, <br> To visit Ocean, father of the gods, <br> And Mother Tethys, in whose palace halls <br> They nourished me, and brought me up. I go <br> To end their hateful quarrels, for too long <br> Have they been strangers to the marriage-bed, <br> Incensed against each other. Now my steeds, <br> Waiting to bear me over land and sea, <br> Stand at the foot of Ida seamed with rills, <br> And now I come to thee, lest thou perchance <br> Be wroth if I unknown to thee repair <br> To where old Ocean dwells amid his deeps.”</p>
<p class="verse">The Cloud-compeller, Jupiter, rejoined:⁠— <br> “Hereafter, Juno, there will be a time <br> For such a journey; meantime let us give <br> This hour to rest and dalliance. Never yet <br> Did love of goddess or of mortal maid <br> Possess and overcome my heart as now; <br> Not even when I loved Ixion’s dame, <br> Who bore Pirithoüs, prudent as a god <br> Among the counsellors; nor when I loved <br> Acrisius’ daughter with the dainty feet, <br> Danae, who brought forth Perseus, eminent <br> Above the other warrior-chiefs; nor when <br> I carried off from Phoenix the renowned <br> His daughter, who bore Minos afterward, <br> And Rhadamanthus. Never so I loved <br> Semele, nor Alcmena who in Thebes <br> Brought forth to me the great-souled Hercules, <br> My valiant son, while Bacchus, the delight <br> Of men, was born of Semele; nor yet <br> So loved I Ceres, fair-haired queen, nor yet <br> Latona, gloriously beautiful, <br> Nor even thee, as now I love, and yield <br> My spirit to the sweetness of desire.”</p>
<p class="verse">Imperial Juno artfully replied:⁠— <br> “Importunate Saturnius, what is this <br> That thou hast said? If on this summit height <br> Of Ida we recline, where all around <br> Is open to the sight, how will it be <br> Should any of the ever-living gods <br> Behold us sleeping, and to all the rest <br> Declare it? I could never, rising thence, <br> Enter again thy palace, save with shame. <br> Yet if thou truly speakest thy desire, <br> Thou hast a marriage-chamber of thine own, <br> Which Vulcan, thy beloved son, for thee <br> Framed, fitting to its posts the solid doors; <br> And thither let us go to take our rest <br> Within it, since thou hast declared thy will.”</p>
<p class="verse">Then spake again the Cloud-compeller Jove:⁠— <br> “O Juno! Fear thou not that any god <br> Or man will look upon us. I shall throw <br> A golden cloud around us, which the Sun <br> Himself cannot look through, although his eye <br> Is piercing, far beyond all other eyes.”</p>
<p class="verse">The son of Saturn spake, and took his wife <br> Into his arms, while underneath the pair <br> The sacred Earth threw up her freshest herbs⁠— <br> The dewy lotus, and the crocus-flower, <br> And thick and soft the hyacinth. All these <br> Upbore them from the ground. Upon this couch <br> They lay, while o’er them a bright golden cloud <br> Gathered, and shed its drops of glistening dew.</p>
<p class="verse">So slumbered on the heights of Gargarus <br> The All-Father, overcome by sleep and love, <br> And held his consort in his arms. Meanwhile <br> The gentle Sleep made haste to seek the fleet <br> Of Greece. He bore a message to the god <br> Neptune, who shakes the shores, and, drawing near, <br> He thus accosted him with wingèd words:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Now, Neptune, give the Greeks thy earnest aid, <br> And though it be but for a little space, <br> While Jupiter yet slumbers, let them win <br> The glory of the day; for I have wrapt <br> His senses in a gentle lethargy, <br> To which he is betrayed by Juno’s wiles.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake, and took his way, departing thence <br> Among the tribes of men. These words inflamed <br> The god’s desire to aid the Greeks; he sprang <br> Far on among the foremost, and exclaimed:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“O Greeks! Do ye again submit to yield <br> The victory to Hector, Priam’s son. <br> That he may seize our fleet and bear away <br> The glory of the day? This is his hope, <br> And this his boast, since now Achilles lies <br> Inactive at his ships, in sullen wrath. <br> Yet little should we need him, if the rest <br> Stood bravely by each other. Hear me now, <br> And do what I advise. Let all of us, <br> The best and bravest, bearing shields, and capped <br> With glittering helms, and wielding in our hands <br> The longest spears, advance, and I will lead <br> The charge; nor do I think that Hector, son <br> Of Priam, daring as he seems, will yet <br> Abide our onset. Who so has the heart <br> To make a stand with me, and yet who bears <br> A narrow shield, let it be given to one <br> Less warlike, and a broader shield be found.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake; they hearkened and obeyed. The kings <br> Tydides, and Ulysses, and the son <br> Of Atreus, Agamemnon, though their wounds <br> Still galled them, marshalled and reviewed the ranks, <br> And changed their arms; they made the braver wear <br> The better armor, and the worse they gave <br> To the less warlike. Now, when o’er their breasts <br> The burnished mail was girded, they began <br> Their march; the great earth-shaker, Neptune, led <br> The onset, grasping in his sinewy hand <br> A sword of fearful length and flashing blade, <br> Like lightning. No man dared encounter it <br> In combat; every arm was stayed by fear.</p>
<p class="verse">Right opposite, illustrious Hector ranged <br> His Trojans. Dark-haired Neptune and the son <br> Of Priam now engaged in desperate strife, <br> One on the side of Troy, and one for Greece. <br> The sea swelled upward toward the Grecian tents <br> And fleet, while both the armies flung themselves <br> Against each other with a loud uproar. <br> Not with such noise the ocean-billows lash <br> The mainland, when the violent north wind <br> Tumbles them shoreward; not with such a noise <br> Roar the fierce flames within the mountain glen, <br> When leaping upward to consume the trees; <br> And not so loudly howls the hurricane <br> Among the lofty branches of the oaks <br> When in its greatest fury, as now rose <br> The din of battle from the hosts that rushed <br> Against each other with terrific cries.</p>
<p class="verse">At Ajax glorious Hector cast his spear, <br> As face to face they stood. It missed him not, <br> But struck him where two belts upon his breast <br> O’erlapped each other⁠—that which held the shield <br> And that which bore the silver-studded sword. <br> These saved the tender muscles. Hector, vexed <br> That thus his weapon should have flown in vain, <br> Retreated toward his comrades, shunning death. <br> As he drew back, the Telamonian hurled <br> A stone⁠—for stones in multitude, that propped <br> The galleys, lay around, and rolled among <br> The feet of those who struggled. One of these <br> He lifted, smiting Hector on the breast, <br> Above the buckler’s orb and near the neck. <br> He sent it spinning like a top; it fell <br> And whirled along the ground. As when beneath <br> The stroke of Father Jupiter an oak <br> Falls broken at the root, and from it fumes <br> A stifling smell of sulphur, and the heart <br> Of him who stands and sees it sinks with dread⁠— <br> For fearful is the bolt of mighty Jove⁠— <br> So dropped the valiant Hector to the earth <br> Amid the dust; his hand let fall the spear; <br> His shield and helm fell with him, and his mail <br> Of shining brass clashed round him. Then the Greeks <br> Rushed toward him, yelling fiercely, for they hoped <br> To drag him thence; and many a lance they cast; <br> But none by javelin or by thrust could wound <br> The shepherd of the people, for there came <br> Around him all the bravest of his host⁠— <br> Polydamas, Aeneas, and the great <br> Agenor, and Sarpedon, he who led <br> The Lycian bands, and Glaucus the renowned; <br> These flung themselves into the strife, while none <br> Of all the rest refrained, but firmly held <br> Their broad round shields before him. Then his friends <br> Lifted him in their arms, and bore him off, <br> Out of the conflict, to his fiery steeds <br> That waited for him in the battle’s rear, <br> With charioteer and sumptuous car; and these <br> Bore him to Ilium, sorely suffering.</p>
<p class="verse">But when they now had reached the crossing-place <br> Of Xanthus, full of eddies, pleasant stream, <br> The progeny of ever-living Jove, <br> They lifted out the hero from the car, <br> And laid him on the ground, and on him poured <br> Water, at which his breath and sight returned. <br> He sat upon his knees, and from his throat <br> Gave forth the purple blood, and then he fell <br> Back to the ground, and darkness veiled his eyes, <br> For still his senses felt the stunning blow.</p>
<p class="verse">The Greeks saw Hector leave the field, and pressed <br> The foe more hotly, and bethought themselves <br> Of their old valor. Then the swift of foot, <br> Oilean Ajax, darted to the van, <br> And with his fir-tree spear smote Satnius, son <br> Of Enops, whom a Naiad eminent <br> For beauty among all the nymphs brought forth <br> To Enops, when on Satnio’s banks he kept <br> His flocks. Oileus’ son, expert to wield <br> The spear, drew near, and pierced him in the flank. <br> Prostrate he fell, and suddenly the Greeks <br> And Trojans gathered round in desperate fray. <br> Polydamas, the mighty spearman, son <br> Of Panthoüs, coming to avenge him, smote <br> On the right shoulder Prothoenor, son <br> Of Areilochus. The pitiless spear <br> Passed through, and falling in the dust he grasped <br> The earth with dying hands. Polydamas <br> Shouted aloud, exulting over him:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Not vainly, as I think, hath flown the spear <br> From the strong hand of the magnanimous son <br> Of Panthoüs. Some Achaian hath received <br> The weapon in his side, to lean upon <br> In going down to Pluto’s dim abode.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake; the Achaians chafed to hear his boast, <br> And most the warlike son of Telamon; <br> For the slain Greek fell near him. Instantly, <br> Just as the Trojan moved away, he hurled <br> His shining lance. Polydamas, to escape <br> The death-stroke, sprang aside. Archilochus, <br> Antenor’s son, received the blow: the gods <br> Had doomed him to be slain. It pierced the spine <br> Where the head joins the neck, and severed there <br> The tendons on each side. His head and mouth <br> And nostrils struck the ground before his knees.</p>
<p class="verse">And thus to excellent Polydamas <br> Did Ajax shout in turn: “Bethink thee now, <br> And tell me truly, was not this a man <br> Worthy to die for Prothoenor’s sake? <br> No man of mean repute or meanly born <br> He seems, but either brother to the knight <br> Antenor, or his son; for certainly <br> His looks declare him of Antenor’s race.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake; but well he knew the slain. Meanwhile <br> The Trojans heard and grieved. Then Acamas, <br> Stalking around his fallen brother, slew <br> Promachus, the Boeotian, with his spear, <br> While dragging off the dead man by the feet.</p>
<p class="verse">Then o’er the fallen warrior, Acamas <br> Boasted aloud: “O measureless in threats! <br> Bowmen of Argos! Not to us alone <br> Shall woe and mourning come; ye also yet <br> Will perish. See your Promachus o’erthrown, <br> And by my spear, that so my brother’s death <br> May not be unrequited. Every man <br> Should wish a brother left to avenge his fall.”</p>
<p class="verse">He ended, and the Greeks were vexed to hear <br> His boast; the brave Peneleus most of all <br> Was angered, and he rushed on Acamas, <br> Who waited not the onset of the king, <br> And in his stead was Ilioneus slain, <br> The son of Phorbas, who was rich in flocks, <br> Whom Mercury, of all the sons of Troy, <br> Loved most, and gave him ample wealth; his wife <br> Brought Ilioneus forth, and only him; <br> And him Peneleus smote beneath the brow <br> In the eye’s socket, forcing out the ball; <br> The spear passed through, and reappeared behind. <br> Down sat the wounded man with arms outstretched, <br> While, drawing his sharp sword, Peneleus smote <br> The middle of his neck, and lopped away <br> The helmèd head, which fell upon the ground, <br> The spear still in the eye. He lifted it <br> As one would lift a poppy up, and thus <br> He shouted, boasting, to the Trojan host:⁠—</p>
<p class="verse">“Go now, ye Trojans, and inform from me <br> The father and the mother of the slain <br> That they may mourn within their palace walls <br> Illustrious Ilioneus. After this <br> Shall the sad wife of Promachus, the son <br> Of Alegenor, never hasten forth <br> To meet her husband with glad looks, when we <br> The Greeks return from Ilium with our fleet.”</p>
<p class="verse">He spake; the Trojans all grew pale with fear, <br> And gazed around for an escape from death.</p>
<p class="verse">Say, Muses, ye who on the Olympian height <br> Inhabit, who was first among the Greeks <br> To gather bloody spoil, when now the power <br> That shakes the shores had turned the tide of war.</p>
<p class="verse">First, Ajax, son of Telamon, struck down <br> Hyrtius, the leader of the Mysian band, <br> And son of Gyrtias, while Antilochus <br> Spoiled Mermerus and Phalces. Morys next, <br> Slain by the weapon of Meriones, <br> Fell with Hippotion. Teucer overthrew <br> Prothous and Periphoetes. Atreus’ son <br> Smote Hyperenor, prince among his tribe, <br> Upon the flank; the trenchant weapon drank <br> The entrails, and the soul, driven forth, escaped <br> Through the deep wound, and darkness veiled his eyes. <br> But Ajax swift of foot, Oileus’ son, <br> O’erthrew the most, for none could equal him <br> In swift pursuit when Jove ordained a flight.</p>
