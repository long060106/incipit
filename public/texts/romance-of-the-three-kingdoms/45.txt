<p>The dying message which the Lady Wu recalled to Sun Chʻüan’s memory was, “For internal matters consult Chang Chao; for external policy Chou Yü.”</p>
<p>Wherefore Chou Yü was summoned.</p>
<p>But he was already on the way. He had been training his naval forces on Lake Pʻoyang when he heard of the approach of Tsʻao Tsʻao’s hosts and had started for Chʻaisengchün without loss of time. So, before the messenger ordered to call him could start, he had already arrived. As he and Lu Su were close friends the latter went to welcome him and told him of all that had happened.</p>
<p>“Have no anxiety,” said Chou Yü, “I shall be able to decide this. But go quickly and beg Kʻung-ming to come to see me.”</p>
<p>So Lu Su went to seek out Kʻung-ming. Chou Yü had many other visitors. First came Chang Chao and his faction to find out what might be afoot. They were received, and after the exchange of the usual commonplaces, Chang Chao said, “Have you heard of our terrible danger?”</p>
<p>“I have heard nothing,” said Chou Yü.</p>
<p>“Tsʻao Tsʻao and his hordes are encamped up the river. He has just sent letters asking our lord to hunt with him in Chianghsia. He may have a desire to absorb this country but, if so, the details of his designs are still secret. We prayed our master to give in his submission and so avoid the horrors of war, but now Lu Su has returned bringing with him the Commander-in-Chief of Liu Pei’s army, Chuko Liang. He, desiring to avenge himself for the recent defeat, has talked our lord into a mind for war and Lu Su persists in supporting him. They only await your final decision.”</p>
<p>“Are you and yours unanimous in your opinions?”</p>
<p>“We are perfectly unanimous,” said Chang.</p>
<p>Chou said, “The fact is I have also desired to submit for a long time. I beg you to leave me now and to-morrow we will see our master and I shall make up his mind for him.”</p>
<p>So they took their leave. Very soon came the military party led by Chʻêng Pʻu. They were admitted and duly enquired after their host’s health. Then the leader said, “Have you heard that our country is about to pass under another’s government?” [460]</p>
<p>“No; I have heard nothing,” replied the host.</p>
<p>“We helped General Sun to establish his authority here and carve out this kingdom, and to gain that end we fought many a battle before we conquered the country. Now our lord lends his ear to his civil officers and desires to submit himself to Tsʻao Tsʻao. This is a most shameful and pitiful course and we would rather die than follow it, so we hope you will decide to fight and you may depend upon our struggling to the last man.”</p>
<p>“And are you unanimous, Generals?” asked Chou Yü.</p>
<p>Huang Kai suddenly started up and smote his forehead saying, “They may take my head but I swear never to surrender.”</p>
<p>“Not one of us is willing to surrender,” cried all the others.</p>
<p>“My desire also is to decide matters with Tsʻao Tsʻao on the battlefield. How could we think of submission? Now I pray you retire, Generals, and when I see our lord I will settle his doubts.”</p>
<p>So the war party left. They were quickly succeeded by Chuko Ching and his faction. They were brought in and, after the usual courtesies, Ching said, “My brother has come down the river saying that Liu Pei desires to ally himself with our lord against Tsʻao Tsʻao. The civil and military hold different opinions as to the course to be pursued, but as my brother is so deeply concerned I am unwilling to say much on either side. We are awaiting your decision.”</p>
<p>“And what do you think about it?” asked Chou Yü.</p>
<p>“Submission is an easy road to tranquillity, while the result of war is hard to foretell.”</p>
<p>Chou Yü smiled, “I shall have my mind made up. Come to-morrow to the palace and the decision shall be announced.”</p>
<p>The trimmers took their leave. But soon after came Lü Mêng with his supporters, also desirous of discussing the same thing, and they told him that opinions differed greatly, some being for peace and others for war. One party constantly disputed with the other.</p>
<p>“I must not say much now,” replied Chou Yü, “but you will see to-morrow in the palace, when the matter will be fully debated.”</p>
<p>They went away leaving Chou Yü smiling cynically.</p>
<p>About eventide Lu Su and Kʻung-ming came and Chou Yü went out to the main gate to receive them. When they had taken their proper seats, Lu Su spoke first, saying, “Tsʻao Tsʻao has come against the south with a huge army. Our master cannot decide whether to submit or give battle and waits for your decision. What is your opinion?”</p>
<p>Chou Yü replied, “We may not oppose Tsʻao Tsʻao when he acts at the command of the Emperor. Moreover, he is very strong and to attack him is to take serious risks. In my opinion, opposition would mean defeat and, since submission [461]means peace, I have decided to advise our lord to write and offer surrender.”</p>
<p>“But you are wrong,” stammered Lu Su. “This country has been under the same rule for three generations and cannot be suddenly abandoned to some other. San Tsʻê said that you were to be consulted on matters beyond the border and we depended upon you to keep the country as secure and solid as Mount Tʻai. Now you adopt the view of the weaklings and propose to yield! I cannot believe you mean it.”</p>
<p>Replied Chou Yü, “The six districts contain countless people. If I am the means of bringing upon them the misery of war they will hate me. So I have decided to advise submission.”</p>
<p>“But do you not realise our lord’s might and the strength of our country? If Tsʻao does attack it is very uncertain that he will realise his desire.”</p>
<p>The two wrangled for a long time, while Kʻung-ming sat smiling with folded arms. Presently Chou Yü asked why he smiled thus and he replied, “I am smiling at no other than your opponent Lu Su, who knows nothing of the affairs of the day.”</p>
<p>“Master,” said Lu Su, “What do you mean?”</p>
<p>“Why, this intention to submit is perfectly reasonable; it is the one proper thing.”</p>
<p>“There!” exclaimed Chou Yü, “Kʻung-ming knows the times perfectly well and he agrees with me.”</p>
<p>“But, both of you, why do you say this?” said Lu.</p>
<p>Said Kʻung-ming, “Tsʻao is an excellent commander, so good that no one dares oppose him. Only very few have ever attempted it and they have been exterminated; the world knows them no more. The only exception is Liu Pei, who did not understand the conditions and vigorously contended against him, with the result that he is now at Chianghsia in a very parlous state. To submit is to secure the safety of wives and children, to be rich and honoured. But the dignity of the country would be left to chance and fate—— However, that is not worth consideration.”</p>
<p>Lu Su interrupted angrily, “Would you make our lord crook the knee to such a rebel as he?”</p>
<p>“Well,” replied Kʻung-ming, “there is another way, and a cheaper; there would be no need to ‘lead the sheep and shoulder wine pots’ for presents, nor any need to yield territory and surrender seals of office. It would not even be necessary to cross the river yourselves. All you would require is a simple messenger and a little boat to ferry a couple of people across the river. If Tsʻao only got these two persons under his hand, his hordes and legions would just drop their weapons, furl their banners and silently vanish away.”</p>
<p>“What two persons could cause Tsʻao Tsʻao to go away as you say?” asked Chou Yü. [462]</p>
<p>“Two persons who could be easily spared from this populous country. They would not be missed any more than a leaf from a tree or a grain of millet from a granary. But if Tsʻao could only get them, would he not go away rejoicing!”</p>
<p>“But who are the two?” asked Chou Yü again.</p>
<p>“When I was living in the country they told me that Tsʻao was building a pavilion on the Chang River; it was to be named the Bronze Bird Pavilion. It is an exceedingly handsome building and he has sought throughout all the world for the most beautiful women to live in it. For Tsʻao really is a sensualist.”</p>
<p>“Now there are two very famous beauties in Chiantung, born of the Chʻiao family. So beautiful are they that birds alight and fishes drown, the moon hides her face and the flowers blush for shame at sight of them. Tsʻao has declared with an oath that he only wants two things in this world, the imperial throne in peace and the sight of those two women on the Bronze Bird Terraces. Given these two he would go down to his grave without regret. This expedition of his, his huge army that threatens this country, has for its real aim these two women. Why do you not buy these two from their father for any sum however large and send them over the river? The object of the army being attained, it will simply be marched away. This is the use that Fan Li made of the famous beauty Hsi Shih.”</p>
<p>“How do you know he so greatly desires these two?” said Chou Yü.</p>
<p>“Because his son, who is an able writer, at the command of his father wrote a poem ‘An Ode to the Bronze Bird Terrace,’ the theme only allowing allusions to the family fitness for the throne. He has sworn to possess these two women. I think I can remember the poem, if you wish to hear it. I admire it greatly.”</p>
<p>“Try,” said Chou Yü.</p>
<p>So he recited the poem:—</p>
<p>Let me follow in the footsteps of the enlightened ruler that I may rejoice,</p>
<p>And ascend the storied terrace that I may gladden my heart,</p>
<p>That I may see the wide extent of the palace,</p>
<p>That I may gaze upon the plans of the virtuous one.</p>
<p>He has established the exalted gates high as the hills,</p>
<p>He has built the lofty towers piercing the blue vault.</p>
<p>He has set up the beautiful building in the midst of the heavens,</p>
<p>Whence the eye can range over the cities of the west.</p>
<p>On the banks of the rolling River Chang he planned it,</p>
<p>Whence abundance of fruits could be looked for in his gardens.</p>
<p>The two towers rise, one on either flank,</p>
<p>This named Golden Phoenix, that Jade Dragon.</p>
<p>He would seize the two Chʻiao; these beautiful ladies of the south-east,</p>
<p>That he might rejoice with them morning and evening.</p>
<p>Look down; there is the grand beauty of an imperial city,</p>
<p>And the rolling vapours lie floating beneath. [463]</p>
<p>He will rejoice in the multitude of scholars that assemble,</p>
<p>Answering to the felicitous dream of King Wên.</p>
<p>Look up; and there is the gorgeous harmony of spring-time,</p>
<p>And the singing of many birds delighting the ear;</p>
<p>The lofty sky stands over all.</p>
<p>The house desires success in its double undertaking,</p>
<p>That the humane influence may be poured out over all the world,</p>
<p>That the perfection of reverence may be offered to the Ruler.</p>
<p>Only the richly prosperous rule of Huan and Wên</p>
<p>Could compare with that of the sacred understanding.</p>
<p>What fortune! what beauty!</p>
<p>The gracious kindness spreads afar,</p>
<p>The imperial family is supported,</p>
<p>Peace reigns over all the world,</p>
<p>Bounded only by the universe.</p>
<p>Bright as the glory of the sun and moon,</p>
<p>Ever honourable and ever enduring,</p>
<p>The Ruler shall live to the age of the eastern emperor,</p>
<p>The dragon banner shall wave to the farthest limit.</p>
<p>His glorious chariot shall be guided with perfect wisdom,</p>
<p>His thoughts shall reform all the world,</p>
<p>Felicitous produce shall be abundant,</p>
<p>And the people shall rest firm.</p>
<p>My desire is that these towers shall endure for ever,</p>
<p>And that joy shall never cease through all the ages.</p>
<p>Chou Yü listened to the end but then suddenly jumped up in a tremendous rage. Turning to the north and pointing with his finger he cried, “You old rebel; this insult is too deep!”</p>
<p>Kʻung-ming hastily rose too and, as if to soothe him, said, “But remember Shan Yü. The emperor gave him a princess of the family to wife although he had made many incursions into our territory. That was the price of peace. You surely would not grudge two more women from among the people.”</p>
<p>“You do not know, Sir,” replied Chou Yü. “Of those two women you mentioned, the elder is the widow of Sun Tsʻê, our late ruler, and the younger is my wife.”</p>
<p>Kʻung-ming feigned the greatest astonishment and said, “No indeed; I did not know. I blundered; a deadly fault; a deadly fault!”</p>
<p>“One of us two has to go, either the old rebel or I; we shall not both live. I swear that,” cried Chou Yü.</p>
<p>“However, such a matter needs a good deal of thought,” replied Kʻung-ming. “We must not make any mistake.”</p>
<p>Chou Yü replied, “I hold a sacred trust from my late lord, Sun Tsʻê; I would not bow the knee to any such as Tsʻao. What I said just now was to see how you stood. I left the lake with the intention of attacking the north, and nothing can change that intention, not even the sword at my breast or the axe on my neck. But I trust you will lend an arm and we will smite Tsʻao Tsʻao together.”</p>
<p>“Should I be happy enough not to be rejected I would render such humble service as I could. Perhaps presently I might be able to offer a plan to oppose him.” [464]</p>
<p>“I am going to see my lord to-morrow to discuss this matter,” said Chou Yü.</p>
<p>Kʻung-ming and Lu Su then left. Next day at dawn Sun Chʻüan went to the council chamber, where his officials, civil and military, were already assembled. They numbered about three score in all. The civil, with Chang Chao at their head, were on the right; the military, with Chʻêng Pʻu as their leader, were ranged on the left. All were in full ceremonial dress and the swords of the soldiers clanked on the pavement. Soon Chou Yü entered and, when Sun Chʻüan had finished the usual gracious remarks, he said, “I hear that Tsʻao Tsʻao is encamped on the river and has sent a despatch to you, my lord; I would ask what your opinion is.”</p>
<p>Thereupon the despatch was produced and handed to Chou Yü. After reading it through he said, smiling, “The old thief thinks there are no men in this land that he writes in this contemptuous strain.”</p>
<p>“What do you think, Sir?” asked Sun Chʻüan.</p>
<p>“Have you discussed this with the officials?” asked Chou Yü.</p>
<p>“We have been discussing this for days. Some counsel surrender and some advise fight. I am undecided and therefore I have asked you to come and decide the point.”</p>
<p>“Who advise surrender?” asked Chou Yü.</p>
<p>“Chang Chao and his party are firmly set in this opinion.”</p>
<p>Chou Yü then turned to Chang Chao and said, “I should be pleased to hear why you are for surrender, Master.”</p>
<p>Then Chang Chao replied, “Tsʻao Tsʻao has been attacking all opponents in the name of the Emperor, who is entirely in his hands. He does everything in the name of the government. Lately he has taken Chingchou and thereby increased his prestige. Our defence against him was the river, but now he also has a large fleet and can attack by water. How can we withstand him? Wherefore I counsel submission till some chance shall offer.”</p>
<p>“This is but the opinion of an ill-advised student,” said Chou Yü. “How can you think of abandoning this country that we have held for three generations?”</p>
<p>“That being so,” said Sun Chʻüan, “where is a plan to come from?”</p>
<p>“Though Tsʻao Tsʻao assumes the name of a Minister of the empire, he is at heart a rebel. You, O General, are able in war and brave. You are the heir to your father and brother. You command brave and tried soldiers, and you have plentiful supplies. You are able to overrun the whole country and rid it of every evil. There is no reason why you should surrender to a rebel. Moreover, Tsʻao has undertaken this expedition in defiance of all the rules of war. The north is unsubdued and Ma Tʻêng and Han Sui threaten his rear and yet he persists in his southern march. This is the first point against Tsʻao. [465]The northern men are unused to fighting on the water; Tsʻao is relinquishing his well-tried cavalry and trusting to ships. That is the second point against him. Again, we are now in full winter and the weather is at its coldest so there is no food for the horses. That is the third point against. Soldiers from the central state marching in a wet country among lakes and rivers will find themselves in an unaccustomed climate and suffer from malaria. That is the fourth point against. Now when Tsʻao’s armies have all these points against them, defeat is certain, however numerous they may be, and you can take Tsʻao captive just as soon as you wish. Give me a few companies of veterans and I will go and destroy him.”</p>
<p>Sun Chʻüan started up from his place saying, “The rebellious old rascal has been wanting to overthrow the Hans and set up himself for years. He has rid himself of all those he feared, save only myself, and I swear that one of us two shall go now. Both of us cannot live. What you say, noble friend, is just what I think, and Heaven has certainly sent you to my assistance.”</p>
<p>“Thy servant will fight a decisive battle,” said Chou Yü, “and shrink not from any sacrifice. Only, General, do not hesitate.”</p>
<p>Sun Chʻüan drew the sword that hung at his side and slashed the table in front of him, exclaiming, “Let any other man mention surrender and he shall be served as I have served this table.”</p>
<p>Then he handed the sword to Chou Yü, at the same time giving him a commission as Commander-in-Chief, Chʻêng Pʻu being second in command. Lu Su was also nominated as assistant.</p>
<p>In conclusion Sun said, “With this sword you will slay any officer who may disobey your commands.”</p>
<p>Chou Yü took the sword and turning to the assembly said, “You have heard our lord’s charge to me, to lead you to destroy Tsʻao; you will all assemble to-morrow at the river-side camp to receive my orders. Should any be late or fail, then the full rigour of military law, the seven prohibitions and the fifty-four penalties there provided, will be enforced.”</p>
<p>He took leave of Sun Chʻüan and left the chamber; the various officers also went their several ways. When Chou Yü reached his own place he sent for Kʻung-ming to consult over the business in hand. He told him of the decision that had been taken and asked for a plan of campaign.</p>
<p>“But your master has not yet made up his mind,” said Kʻung-ming. “Till he has, no plan can be decided upon.”</p>
<p>“What do you mean?”</p>
<p>“In his heart he is still fearful of Tsʻao’s numbers and frets over the inequality of the two armies. You will have to explain away those numbers and bring him to a final decision before anything can be effected.” [466]</p>
<p>“What you say is excellent,” said Chou Yü and he went to the palace that night to see his master. Sun Chʻüan said, “You must have something of real importance to say if you come like this at night.”</p>
<p>Chou Yü said, “I am making my dispositions to-morrow; you have quite made up your mind?”</p>
<p>“The fact is,” said Sun Chʻüan, “I still feel nervous about the disparity of numbers. Surely we are too few. That is really all I feel doubtful about.”</p>
<p>“It is precisely because you have this one remaining doubt that I am come. And I will explain. Tsʻao’s letter speaks of a hundred legions of marines, and so you feel doubts and fears and do not wait to consider the real truth. Let us examine the case thoroughly. We find that he has of Central State soldiers, say, fifteen or sixteen legions, and many of them are sick. He only got seven or eight legions from the Yüans and many of those are of doubtful loyalty. Now these sick men and these men of doubtful loyalty seem a great many but they are not at all fearsome. I could smash them with five legions. So, my lord, have no further anxiety.”</p>
<p>Sun Chʻüan patted his general on the back saying, “You have explained my difficulty and relieved my doubts. Chang Chao is an old fool who constantly baulks my expeditions. Only you and Lu Su have any real understanding of my heart. To-morrow you and he and Chʻêng Pʻu will start and I shall have a strong reserve ready with plentiful supplies to support you. If anything goes agley you can at once send for me and I will engage with my own men.”</p>
<p>Chou Yü left; but in his innermost heart he said to himself, “If that Kʻung-ming can gauge my master’s thoughts so very accurately he is too clever for me and will be a danger. He will have to be put out of the way.”</p>
<p>He sent a messenger over to Lu Su to talk over this last scheme. When he had laid it bare, Lu Su did not favour it. “No, no,” said he, “it is self-destruction to make away with your ablest officer before Tsʻao shall have been destroyed.”</p>
<p>“But Kʻung-ming will certainly help Liu Pei to our disadvantage.”</p>
<p>“Try what his brother Chuko Chin can do to persuade him. It would be an excellent thing to have these two in our service.”</p>
<p>“Yes, indeed,” replied Chou Yü.</p>
<p>Next morning at dawn Chou Yü went to his camp and took his seat in the council tent. The lictors took up their stations right and left and the officers ranged themselves in lines to listen to the orders.</p>
<p>Now Chʻêng Pʻu, who was older than Chou Yü, was very angry at being passed over, so he made a pretence of indisposition and stayed away from this assembly. But he sent his son to represent him. Chou Yü addressed the gathering, [467]saying, “The law knows no partiality and you will all have to attend to your several duties. Tsʻao is now more absolute than ever was Tung Cho, and the Emperor is really a prisoner in Hsüchʻang, guarded by the most cruel soldiers. We have a command to destroy Tsʻao and with your willing help we shall advance. The army must cause no hardship to the people anywhere. Rewards for good service and punishments for faults shall be given impartially.”</p>
<p>Having delivered this charge he told off Han Tang and Huang Kai as leaders of the van, and ordered the ships under his own command to get under way and go to Sanchiangkʻou. They would get orders by and bye. Then he appointed five armies with two officers over each while Lü Fan and Chu Chih were appointed inspectors, to move from place to place and keep the various units up to their work and acting with due regard to the general plan. Land and marine forces were to move simultaneously. The expedition would soon start.</p>
<p>Having received their orders each returned to his command and busied himself in preparation. Chʻêng Pʻu’s son returned and told his father what arrangements had been made and Chʻêng Pʻu was amazed at their skill. Said he, “I have always despised Chou Yü as a mere student who would never be a general, but this shows that he has a leader’s talent. I must support him.” So he went over to the quarters of the Commander-in-Chief and confessed his fault. He was received kindly and all was over.</p>
<p>Next Chou Yü sent for Chuko Chin and said to him, “Evidently your brother is a genius, a man born to be a king’s counsellor. Why then does he serve Liu Pei? Now that he is here I wish you to use every effort to persuade him to stay with us. Thus our lord would gain able support and you two brothers would be together, which would be pleasant for you both. I wish you success.”</p>
<p>Chin replied, “I am ashamed of the little service I have rendered since I came here and I can do no other than obey your command to the best of my ability.”</p>
<p>Thereupon he went away to his brother, whom he found in the guest-house. The younger brother received him and when he had reached the inner rooms Kʻung-ming bowed respectfully and, weeping, told his experiences since they parted and his sorrow at their separation. Then Chin, weeping also, said, “Brother, do you remember the story of Po I and Shu Chʻi, the brothers who would not be separated?”</p>
<p>“Ah, Chou Yü has sent him to talk me over,” thought Kʻung-ming. So he replied, “They were two of the noble people of old days; yes, I know.”</p>
<p>“Those two, although they perished of hunger near the Shouyang Hills, yet never separated. You and I, born of the same mother and suckled at the same breast, yet serve different [468]masters and never meet. Are you not ashamed when you think of such examples as Po I and Shu Chʻi?”</p>
<p>Kʻung-ming replied, “You are talking now of love, but what I stand for is duty. We are both men of Han and Liu Pei is of the family. If you, brother, could leave Wu and join me in serving the rightful branch, then on the one side we should be honoured as Ministers of Han and on the other we should be together as people of the same flesh and blood should be. Thus love and duty would both receive their proper meed. What do you think of it, my brother?”</p>
<p>“I came to persuade him and lo! it is I who am being talked over,” thought Chuko Chin. He had no fitting reply to make so he rose and took his leave. Returning to Chou Yü he related the story of the interview.</p>
<p>“What do you think?” asked Chou Yü.</p>
<p>“General Sun has treated me with great kindness and I could not turn my back on him,” replied Chin.</p>
<p>“Since you decide to remain loyal, there is no need to say much; I think I have a plan to win over your brother.”</p>
<p>The wisest men see eye to eye.</p>
<p>For each but sees the right;</p>
<p>But should their several interests clash,</p>
<p>They all the fiercer fight.</p>
<p>The means by which Chou Yü tried to get the support of Kʻung-ming will be described in the next chapter. [469]</p>
