<p>In the last chapter Chou Yü was seized with sudden illness as he watched the fleets of his enemy. He was borne to his tent and his officers came in multitudes to enquire after him. They looked at each other saying, “What a pity our general should be taken ill when Tsʻao’s legions threaten so terribly! What would happen if he attacked?”</p>
<p>Messengers with the evil tidings were sent to Wu while the physicians did their best for the invalid. Lu Su was particularly sad at the illness of his patron and went to see Kʻung-ming to talk it over.</p>
<p>“What do you make of it?” said Kʻung-ming.</p>
<p>“Good luck for Tsʻao Tsʻao; bad for us,” said Lu.</p>
<p>“I could cure him,” said Kʻung-ming laughing.</p>
<p>“If you could the State would be very fortunate,” said Lu.</p>
<p>He prayed Kʻung-ming to go to see the sick man. They went, and Lu Su entered first. Chou Yü lay in bed, his head covered by a quilt.</p>
<p>“How are you, General?” said Lu.</p>
<p>“My heart pains me; every now and again I feel faint and dizzy.”</p>
<p>“Have you taken any remedies?”</p>
<p>“My gorge rises at the thought; I could not.”</p>
<p>“I saw Kʻung-ming just now and he says he could heal you. He is just outside and I will call him if you like.”</p>
<p>“Ask him to come in.”</p>
<p>Chou bade his servants help him to a sitting position and Kʻung-ming entered.</p>
<p>“I have not seen you for days,” said he. “How could I guess that you were unwell?”</p>
<p>“How can any one feel secure? We are constantly the playthings of luck, good or bad.</p>
<p>“Yes; Heaven’s winds and clouds are not to be measured. No one can reckon their comings and goings, can they?”</p>
<p>Chou Yü turned pale and a low groan escaped him, while his visitor went on, “You feel depressed, do you not? As though troubles were piling up in your heart?”</p>
<p>“That is exactly how I feel.”</p>
<p>“You need cooling medicine to dissipate this sense of oppression.” [509]</p>
<p>“I have taken a cooling draught, but it has done no good.”</p>
<p>“You must get the humours into good order before the drugs will have any effect.”</p>
<p>Chou Yü began to think Kʻung-ming knew what was really the matter and resolved to test him. “What should be taken to produce a favourable temper?”</p>
<p>“I know one means of producing a favourable temper,” replied Kʻung-ming.</p>
<p>“I wish you would tell me.”</p>
<p>Kʻung-ming got out writing materials, sent away the servants and then wrote a few words:—“One should burn out Tsʻao; all is ready, but there is no east wind,” this he gave to the sick general, saying “That is the origin of your illness.”</p>
<p>Chou Yü read the words with great surprise and it confirmed his secret opinion that Kʻung-ming really was rather more than human. He decided that the only course was to be open and tell him all. So he said, “Since you know the cause of the disease, what do you recommend as treatment? The need of a remedy is very urgent.”</p>
<p>“I have no great talent,” said Kʻung-ming, “but I have had to do with men of no ordinary gifts from whom I have received certain magical books. I can call the winds and summon the rains. Since you need a south-east breeze, General, you must build an altar on the Nanping Mountains, the Altar of the Seven Stars. It must be nine feet high, with three steps, surrounded by a guard of one hundred and twenty men bearing flags. On this altar I will work a spell to procure a strong south-east gale for three days and three nights. Do you approve?”</p>
<p>“Never mind three whole days;” said Chou Yü, “one day of strong wind will serve my purpose. But it must be done at once and without delay.”</p>
<p>“I will sacrifice for a wind for three days from the twentieth day of the moon; will that suit you?”</p>
<p>Chou Yü was delighted and hastily rose from his couch to give the necessary orders. He commanded that five hundred men should be sent to the mountains to build the altar and he told off the guard of one hundred and twenty to bear the flags and be at the orders of Kʻung-ming.</p>
<p>Kʻung-ming took his leave, went forth and rode off with Lu Su to the mountains where they measured out the ground. He bade the soldiers build the altar of red earth from the south-east quarter. It was two hundred and forty feet in circuit, square in shape, and of three tiers, each of three feet, in all nine feet high. On the lowest tier he placed the flags of the twenty-eight “houses” of the heavens; on the east seven, with blue flags, on the north seven, with black flags, on the west seven, with white flags, and on the south seven, with red flags. Around the second tier he placed sixty-four yellow [510]flags, corresponding to the number of the diagrams of the Book of Divination, in eight groups of eight. Four men were stationed on the highest platform, each wearing a Taoist headdress and a black silk robe embroidered with the phoenix and confined with wide sashes. They wore scarlet boots and square-cut skirts. On the left front stood a man supporting a tall pole bearing at its top a plume of light feathers to show by their least movement the wind’s first breathing. On the right front was a man holding a tall pole whereon was a flag with the symbol of the seven stars to show the direction and force of the wind. On the left rear stood a man with a sword, and on the right rear a man with a censer. Below the altar were two score and four men holding flags, umbrellas, spears, lances, yellow banners, white axes, red bannerols and black ensigns. And these were spaced about the altar.</p>
<p>On the appointed day Kʻung-ming, having chosen a propitious moment, bathed his body and purified himself. Then he robed himself as a Taoist, loosened his locks and approached the altar.</p>
<p>He bade Lu Su retire saying, “Return to the camp and assist the General in setting out his forces. Should my prayers avail not, do not wonder.”</p>
<p>So Lu Su left him. Then he commanded the guards on no account to absent themselves, to maintain strict silence and to be reverent; death would be the penalty of disobedience.</p>
<p>Next with solemn steps he ascended the altar, faced the proper quarter, lighted the incense and sprinkled the water in the basins. This done he gazed into the heavens and prayed silently. The prayer ended he descended and returned to his tent. After a brief rest he allowed the soldiers by turns to go away to eat.</p>
<p>Thrice that day he ascended the altar and thrice descended; but there was no sign of the wind.</p>
<p>Here it may be related that Chou Yü with Chʻêng Pʻu and Lu Su and a certain number of military officials on duty, sat waiting in the tent till the wished-for wind should blow and the attack could be launched. Messengers were also sent to Sun Chʻüan to prepare to support the forward movement.</p>
<p>Huang Kai had his fire ships ready, a score of them. The fore parts of the ships were thickly studded with large nails, and they were loaded with dry reeds, wood soaked in fish oil and covered with sulphur, saltpetre and other inflammables. The ships were covered in with black oiled cloth. In the prow of each was a black dragon flag with indentations. A fighting ship was attached to the stern of each to propel it forward. All were ready and awaited orders to move.</p>
<p>Meanwhile Tsʻao Tsʻao’s two spies, the brothers Tsʻai, were being guarded carefully in an outer camp far from the river bank and daily entertained with feasting. They were not [511]allowed to know of the preparations. The watch was so close that not a trickle of information reached the prisoners.</p>
<p>Presently, while Chou Yü was anxiously awaiting in his tent for the desired wind, a messenger came to say that Sun Chʻüan had anchored at a place eighty-five <i>li</i> from the camp, where he awaited news. Lu Su was sent to warn all the various commanders to be ready, the ships and their weapons, sails and oars, all for instant use, and to impress upon them the penalties of being caught unprepared. The soldiers were indeed ready for the fight and yearning for the fray.</p>
<p>But the sky remained obstinately clear and as night drew nigh no breath of air stirred.</p>
<p>“We have been cajoled,” said Chou Yü. “Indeed what possibility is there of a south-east wind in mid-winter?”</p>
<p>“Kʻung-ming would not use vain and deceitful words,” replied Lu Su.</p>
<p>Towards the third watch the sound of a movement arose in the air. Soon the flags fluttered out. And when the general went out to make sure he saw they were flowing toward the north-west. In a very short time the south-east wind was in full force.</p>
<p>Chou Yü was, however, frightened at the power of the man whose help he had invoked. “Really the man has power over the heavens and authority over the earth; his methods are incalculable, beyond the ken of god or devil. He cannot be allowed to live to be a danger to our land of Wu. We must slay him soon to fend off later evils.”</p>
<p>So he resolved to commit a crime to remove his dangerous rival. He called two of the captains of his guard, Ting Fêng and Hsü Shêng, and bade each take a party, one along the river, the other along the road, to the altar on the mountains. As soon as they got there, without asking questions or giving reasons, they were to seize and behead Kʻung-ming. They might expect a solid reward if they brought his head.</p>
<p>The two went off on their nefarious errand, one leading dagger- and axe-men going as fast as oars could propel them along the river, the other at the head of archers and bowmen on horseback. The south-east wind buffeted them as they went on their way.</p>
<p>High was raised the Seven Stars’ Altar,</p>
<p>On it prayed the Sleeping Dragon</p>
<p>For an eastern wind, and straightway</p>
<p>Blew the wind. Had not the wizard</p>
<p>Exercised his mighty magic</p>
<p>Nought had Chou Yü’s skill availed.</p>
<p>Ting Fêng first arrived. He saw the guards with their flags, dropped off his steed and marched to the altar, sword in hand. But he found no Kʻung-ming. He asked the guards; [512]they told him he had just gone down. Ting Fêng ran down the hill to search. There he met his fellow and they joined forces. Presently a simple soldier told them that the evening before a small, fast boat had anchored there near a sand spit and Kʻung-ming had been seen to go on board. Then the boat had gone up river. So they divided their party into two, one to go by water, the other by land.</p>
<p>Hsü Shêng bade his boatmen put on all sail and take every advantage of the wind. Before very long he saw the fugitive’s boat ahead and when near enough, stood in the prow of his own and shouted, “Do not flee, O Instructor of the Army! The General requests your presence.”</p>
<p>Kʻung-ming, who was seated in the stern of his boat, just laughed aloud, “Return and tell the General to make good use of his men. Tell him I am going up river for a spell and will see him again another day.”</p>
<p>“Pray wait a little while,” cried Hsü. “I have something most important to tell you.”</p>
<p>“I knew all about it, that he would not let me go and that he wanted to kill me. That is why Chao Yün was waiting for me. You had better not approach nearer.”</p>
<p>Seeing the other ship had no sail, Hsü Shêng thought he would assuredly come up with it and so maintained the pursuit. Then when he got too close Chao Yün fitted an arrow to the bowstring and, standing up in the stern of his boat, cried, “You know who I am and I came expressly to escort the Instructor. Why are you pursuing him? One arrow would kill you, only that would cause a breach of the peace between two houses. I will shoot and just give you a specimen of my skill.”</p>
<p>With that he shot, and the arrow whizzed overhead cutting the rope that held up the sail. Down came the sail trailing in the water and the boat swung round. Then Chao Yün’s boat hoisted its sail and the fair wind speedily carried it out of sight.</p>
<p>On the bank stood Ting Fêng. He bade his comrade come to the shore and said, “He is too clever for any man; and Chao Yün is bravest of the brave. You remember what he did at Tangyang Slope. All we can do is to return and report.”</p>
<p>So they returned to camp and told their master about the preparations that Kʻung-ming had made to ensure safety. Chou Yü was indeed puzzled at the depth of his rival’s insight. “I shall have no peace day or night while he lives,” said he.</p>
<p>“At least wait till Tsʻao is done with,” said Lu Su.</p>
<p>And Chou Yü knew he spake wisely. Having summoned the leaders to receive orders, first he bade Kan Ning take with him the false deserter Tsʻai Chung and his soldiers and go along the south bank, showing the flags of Tsʻao Tsʻao, till they [513]reached the “Black” Forest (Wu Lin) just opposite the enemy’s main store of grain and forage. Then they were to penetrate as deeply as possible into the enemy’s lines and light a torch as a signal. Tsʻai Chung’s brother was to be kept in camp for another purpose.</p>
<p>The next order was for Tʻaishih Tzŭ; he was to lead two companies as quickly as possible to Huangchou and cut the enemy’s communications with Hofei. When near the enemy he was to give a signal and if he saw a red flag he would know that Sun Chʻüan was at hand with reinforcements.</p>
<p>These two had the farthest to go and started first. Then Lü Mêng was sent into Wu Lin (the “Black” Forest) with three companies as a support. Kan Ning was ordered to set fire to the camp. A fourth party was to go to the borders of Iling and attack as soon as the signal from the forest was seen. A fifth party of three companies went to Hanyang to fall upon the enemy along the river. Their signal was a white flag and a sixth division supported them.</p>
<p>When these six parties had gone off, Huang Kai got ready his fire ships and sent a soldier with a note to tell Tsʻao Tsʻao that he was coming over that evening. Four fighting ships were told off to support Huang Kai.</p>
<p>The four squadrons, each of three hundred ships, were placed under four commanders, Han Tang, Chou Tʻai, Chiang Chʻin and Chʻên Wu. The score of fire ships preceded them. Chou Yü and Chʻêng Pʻu went on board one of the large ships to direct the battle. Their guards were Ting Fêng and Hsü Shêng. Lu Su, Kʻan Tsê and the advisers were left to guard the camp. Chʻêng Pʻu was greatly impressed with Chou Yü’s ordering of the attack.</p>
<p>Then came a messenger bearing a mandate from Sun Chʻüan making Lu Hsün leader of the van. He was ordered to go to Chʻihuang. The Marquis himself would support him. Chou Yü also sent a man to the western hills to make signals and to hoist flags on the Nanping Mountains.</p>
<p>So all being prepared they waited for dusk.</p>
<p>Here it is necessary to diverge from the direct narrative to say that Yüan-tê was at Hsiakʻou anxiously awaiting the return of his adviser. Then appeared a fleet, led by Liu Chʻi, who had come to find out how matters were progressing. Yüan-tê sent to call him to the battle tower and told him of the south-east wind that had begun to blow and that Chao Yün had gone to meet Kʻung-ming. Not long after a single sail was seen coming up before the wind and he knew it was Kʻung-ming, the Instructor of the Army. So he and Liu Chʻi went down to meet the boat. Soon the vessel reached the shore and Kʻung-ming and Chao Yün disembarked.</p>
<p>Yüan-tê was very glad and after they had enquired after each other’s well-being Kʻung-ming said, “There is no time to [514]tell of any other things now. Are the soldiers and ships ready?”</p>
<p>“They have long been ready,” replied Yüan-tê. “They only await you to direct how they are to be used.”</p>
<p>The three then went to the tent and took their seats. Kʻung-ming at once began to issue orders. “Chao Yün, with three companies is to cross the river and go to the Wu Lin by the minor road. He will choose a dense jungle and prepare an ambush. To-night, after the fourth watch, Tsʻao Tsʻao will hurry along that way. When half his men have passed, the jungle is to be fired. Tsʻao Tsʻao will not be wholly destroyed but many will perish.”</p>
<p>“There are two roads,” said Chao Yün. “One leads to the southern districts and the other to Chingchou. I do not know by which he will come.”</p>
<p>“The south road is too dangerous; Tsʻao Tsʻao will certainly pass along the Chingchou road, so that he may get away to Hsüchʻang.”</p>
<p>Then Chao Yün went away. Next Kʻung-ming said to Chang Fei, “You will take three companies over the river to cut the road to Iling. You will ambush in the Hulu Valley. Tsʻao Tsʻao, not daring to go to South Iling, will go to North Iling. To-morrow, after the rain, he will halt to refresh his men. As soon as the smoke is seen to rise from their cooking fires you will fire the hill side. You will not capture Tsʻao Tsʻao but you will render excellent service.”</p>
<p>So Chang Fei left. Next was called Mi Chu, Mi Fang and Liu Fêng. They were to take command of three squadrons and go along the river to collect the weapons that the beaten soldiers would throw away.</p>
<p>The three left. Then Kʻung-ming said to Liu Chʻi, “The country around Wuchʻang is very important and I wish you to take command of your own troops and station them at strategic points. Tsʻao Tsʻao, being defeated, will flee thither, and you will capture him. But you are not to leave the city without the best of reasons.”</p>
<p>And Liu Chʻi took leave.</p>
<p>Then Kʻung-ming said to Yüan-tê, “I wish you to remain quietly and calmly in Fankʻou while Chou Yü works out his great scheme this night.”</p>
<p>All this time Kuan Yü has been silently waiting his turn but Kʻung-ming said no word to him. When he could bear this no longer he cried, “Since I first followed my brother to battle many years ago I have never been left behind. Now that great things are afoot is there no work for me? What is meant by it?”</p>
<p>“You should not be surprised. I wanted you for service at a most important point only that there was a something [515]standing in the way that prevented me from sending you,” said Kʻung-ming.</p>
<p>“What could stand in the way? I wish you would tell me.”</p>
<p>“You see Tsʻao Tsʻao was once very kind to you and you cannot help feeling grateful. Now when his soldiers have been beaten he will have to flee along the Huayung road and if I sent you to guard it you would have to let him pass. So I will not send you.”</p>
<p>“You are most considerate, Instructor. But though it is true that he treated me well, yet I slew two of his most redoubtable opponents by way of repayment, beside raising a siege. If I happened upon him on this occasion I should hardly let him go.”</p>
<p>“But what if you did?”</p>
<p>“You could deal with me by military rules.”</p>
<p>“Then put that in writing.”</p>
<p>So Kuan Yü wrote a formal undertaking and gave the document to Kʻung-ming.</p>
<p>“What happens if Tsʻao Tsʻao does not pass that way?” said Kuan Yü.</p>
<p>“I will give you a written engagement that he will pass.” Then he continued, “On the hills by the Huayung Valley you are to raise a heap of wood and grass to make a great column of smoke and mislead Tsʻao Tsʻao into coming.”</p>
<p>“If Tsʻao Tsʻao sees a smoke he will suspect an ambush and will not come.” said Kuan Yü.</p>
<p>“You are very simple,” said Kʻung-ming. “Do you not know more of war’s ruses than that? Tsʻao Tsʻao is an able leader but you can deceive him this time. When he sees the smoke he will take it as a subterfuge and risk going that way. But do not let your kindness of heart rule your conduct.”</p>
<p>Thus was his duty assigned Kuan Yü and he left, taking his adopted son, Pʻing, Chou Tsʻang and a half company of swordsmen.</p>
<p>Said Liu Pei, “His sense of rectitude is very profound; I fear if Tsʻao Tsʻao should come that way that my brother will let him pass.”</p>
<p>“I have consulted the stars and Tsʻao the rebel is not fated to come to his end yet. I have purposely designed this manifestation of kindly feeling for Kuan Yü to accomplish and so act handsomely.”</p>
<p>“Indeed there are few such far-seeing men as you are,” said Yüan-tê.</p>
<p>The two then went to Fankʻou whence they might watch Chou Yü’s evolutions. Sun Chʻüan and Chien Yung were left on guard.</p>
<p>Tsʻao Tsʻao was in his great camp in conference with his advisers and awaiting the arrival of Huang Kai. The south-east wind was very strong that day and Chʻêng Yü was [516]insisting on the necessity for precaution. But Tsʻao laughed saying, “The Winter Solstice depends upon the sun and nothing else; there is sure to be a south wind at some one or other of its recurrences. I see nothing to wonder at.”</p>
<p>Just then they announced the arrival of a small boat from the other shore with a letter from Huang Kai. The bearer of the letter was brought in and presented it. It stated that Chou Yü had kept such strict watch that there had been no chance of escape. But now some grain was coming down river and Huang Kai had been named as escort commander, which would give him the opportunity he desired. He would slay one of the known captains and bring his head as an offering when he came. That evening at the third watch, if boats were seen with dragon toothed flags, they would be the grain boats.</p>
<p>This letter delighted Tsʻao Tsʻao who, with his officers, went to the naval camp and boarded a great ship to watch for the arrival of Huang Kai.</p>
<p>In Chiangtung, when evening fell, Chou Yü sent for Tsʻao Ho and bade the soldiers bind him. The unhappy man protested that he had committed no crime but Chou Yü said, “What sort of a fellow are you, think you, to come and pretend to desert to my side? I need a small sacrifice for my flag and your head will serve my purpose. So I am going to us it.”</p>
<p>Tsʻai Ho being at the end of his tether unable to deny the charge suddenly cried, “Two of your own side, Kʻan Tsê and Kan Ning, are also in the plot!”</p>
<p>“Under my directions,” said Chou Yü.</p>
<p>Tsʻai Ho was exceedingly repentant and sad, but Chou Yü bade them take him to the river bank where the black standard had been set up and there, after the pouring of a libation and the burning of paper, he was beheaded, his blood being a sacrifice to the flag.</p>
<p>This ceremony over the ships started and Huang Kai took his place on the third ship. He merely wore breast armour and carried a keen blade. On his flag were written four large characters “Van Leader Huang Kai”. With a fair wind his fleet sailed toward Chʻihpʻi, or Red Wall.</p>
<p>The wind was strong and the waves ran high. Tsʻao Tsʻao in the midst of the central squadron eagerly scanned the river which rolled down under the bright moon like a silver serpent writhing in innumerable folds. Letting the wind blow full in his face Tsʻao Tsʻao laughed aloud for was he not now to obtain his desire?</p>
<p>Then a soldier pointing to the river said, “The whole south is one mass of sails and they are coming up on the wind.”</p>
<p>Tsʻao Tsʻao went to a higher point and gazed at the sails intently and his men told him that the flags were black and [517]dragon shaped, and indented, and among them there flew one very large banner on which was a name Huang Kai.</p>
<p>“That is my friend the deserter,” said he joyfully. “Heaven is on my side to-day.”</p>
<p>As the ships drew closer Chʻêng Yü said, “Those ships are treacherous. Do not let them approach the camp.”</p>
<p>“How know you that?” asked Tsʻao Tsʻao.</p>
<p>And Chʻêng Yü replied, “If they were laden with grain they would lie deep in the water. But these are light and float easily. The south-east wind is very strong and if they intend treachery, how can we defend ourselves?”</p>
<p>Tsʻao Tsʻao began to understand. Then he asked who would go out to stop them, and Wên Pʻing volunteered. “I am well used to the waters,” said he.</p>
<p>Thereupon he sprang into a small light craft and sailed out, followed by a half score cruisers which came at his signal. Standing in the prow of his ship he called out to those advancing toward them, “You southern ships are not to approach; such are the orders of the Minister. Stop there in mid stream.”</p>
<p>The soldiers all yelled to them to lower their sails. The shout had not died away when a bowstring twanged and Wên Pʻing rolled down into the ship with an arrow in the left arm. Confusion reigned on his ship and all the others harried back to their camp.</p>
<p>When the ships were about a couple of <i>li</i> distant, Huang Kai waved his sword and the leading ships broke forth into fire, which, under the force of the strong wind, soon gained strength and the ships became as fiery arrows. Soon the whole twenty dashed into the naval camp.</p>
<p>All Tsʻao Tsʻao’s ships were gathered there and as they wore firmly chained together not one could escape from the others and flee. There was a roar of bombs and fireships came on from all sides at once. The face of the three rivers was speedily covered with fire which flew before the wind from one ship to another. It seemed as if the universe was filled with flame.</p>
<p>Tsʻao Tsʻao hastened toward the shore. Huang Kai, with a few men at his back, leaped into a small boat, dashed through the fire and sought Tsʻao Tsʻao. He, seeing the imminence of the danger, was making for the land, Chang Liao got hold of a small boat into which he helped his master; none too soon, for the ship was burning. They got Tsʻao Tsʻao out of the thick of the fire and dashed for the bank.</p>
<p>Huang Kai seeing a handsomely robed person get into a small boat guessed it must be Tsʻao Tsʻao and pursued. He drew very near and he held his keen blade ready to strike, crying out, “You rebel! do not flee. I am Huang Kai.”</p>
<p>Tsʻao Tsʻao howled in the bitterness of his distress. Chang Liao fitted an arrow to his bow and aimed at the pursuer, [518]shooting at short range. The roaring of the gale and the flames kept Huang Kai from hearing the twang of the string and he was wounded in the shoulder. He fell and rolled over into the water.</p>
<p>He fell in peril of water</p>
<p>When flames were high;</p>
<p>Ere cudgel bruises had faded,</p>
<p>An arrow struck.</p>
