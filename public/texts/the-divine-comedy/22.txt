<p class="lede">The Fifth Bolgia: peculators⁠—The elder of Santa Zita⁠—Malebranche.</p>
<p class="verse">From bridge to bridge thus, speaking other things<sup>301</sup> <br> Of which my Comedy cares not to sing,<sup>302</sup> <br> We came along, and held the summit, when <br> We halted to behold another fissure <br> Of Malebolge and other vain laments; <br> And I beheld it marvellously dark. <br> As in the Arsenal of the Venetians<sup>303</sup> <br> Boils in the winter the tenacious pitch <br> To smear their unsound vessels o’er again, <br> For sail they cannot; and instead thereof <br> One makes his vessel new, and one recaulks <br> The ribs of that which many a voyage has made; <br> One hammers at the prow, one at the stern, <br> This one makes oars, and that one cordage twists, <br> Another mends the mainsail and the mizzen; <br> Thus, not by fire, but by the art divine, <br> Was boiling down below there a dense pitch <br> Which upon every side the bank belimed. <br> I saw it, but I did not see within it <br> Aught but the bubbles that the boiling raised, <br> And all swell up and resubside compressed. <br> The while below there fixedly I gazed, <br> My Leader, crying out: “Beware, beware!” <br> Drew me unto himself from where I stood. <br> Then I turned round, as one who is impatient <br> To see what it behoves him to escape, <br> And whom a sudden terror doth unman, <br> Who, while he looks, delays not his departure; <br> And I beheld behind us a black devil, <br> Running along upon the crag, approach. <br> Ah, how ferocious was he in his aspect! <br> And how he seemed to me in action ruthless, <br> With open wings and light upon his feet! <br> His shoulders, which sharp-pointed were and high, <br> A sinner did encumber with both haunches, <br> And he held clutched the sinews of the feet. <br> From off our bridge, he said: “O Malebranche,<sup>304</sup> <br> Behold one of the elders of Saint Zita;<sup>305</sup> <br> Plunge him beneath, for I return for others <br> Unto that town, which is well furnished with them. <br> All there are barrators, except Bonturo;<sup>306</sup> <br> No into Yes for money there is changed.” <br> He hurled him down, and over the hard crag <br> Turned round, and never was a mastiff loosened <br> In so much hurry to pursue a thief. <br> The other sank, and rose again face downward;<sup>307</sup> <br> But the demons, under cover of the bridge, <br> Cried: “Here the Santo Volto has no place!<sup>308</sup> <br> Here swims one otherwise than in the Serchio;<sup>309</sup> <br> Therefore, if for our gaffs thou wishest not, <br> Do not uplift thyself above the pitch.” <br> They seized him then with more than a hundred rakes; <br> They said: “It here behoves thee to dance covered, <br> That, if thou canst, thou secretly mayest pilfer.” <br> Not otherwise the cooks their scullions make <br> Immerse into the middle of the cauldron <br> The meat with hooks, so that it may not float. <br> Said the good Master to me: “That it be not <br> Apparent thou art here, crouch thyself down <br> Behind a jag, that thou mayest have some screen; <br> And for no outrage that is done to me <br> Be thou afraid, because these things I know, <br> For once before was I in such a scuffle.”<sup>310</sup> <br> Then he passed on beyond the bridge’s head, <br> And as upon the sixth bank he arrived, <br> Need was for him to have a steadfast front. <br> With the same fury, and the same uproar, <br> As dogs leap out upon a mendicant, <br> Who on a sudden begs, where’er he stops, <br> They issued from beneath the little bridge, <br> And turned against him all their grappling-irons; <br> But he cried out: “Be none of you malignant! <br> Before those hooks of yours lay hold of me, <br> Let one of you step forward, who may hear me, <br> And then take counsel as to grappling me.” <br> They all cried out: “Let Malacoda go”; <br> Whereat one started, and the rest stood still, <br> And he came to him, saying: “What avails it?” <br> “Thinkest thou, Malacoda, to behold me <br> Advanced into this place,” my Master said, <br> “Safe hitherto from all your skill of fence, <br> Without the will divine, and fate auspicious? <br> Let me go on, for it in Heaven is willed <br> That I another show this savage road.” <br> Then was his arrogance so humbled in him, <br> That he let fall his grapnel at his feet, <br> And to the others said: “Now strike him not.” <br> And unto me my Guide: “O thou, who sittest <br> Among the splinters of the bridge crouched down, <br> Securely now return to me again.” <br> Wherefore I started and came swiftly to him; <br> And all the devils forward thrust themselves, <br> So that I feared they would not keep their compact. <br> And thus beheld I once afraid the soldiers <br> Who issued under safeguard from Caprona,<sup>311</sup> <br> Seeing themselves among so many foes. <br> Close did I press myself with all my person <br> Beside my Leader, and turned not mine eyes <br> From off their countenance, which was not good. <br> They lowered their rakes, and “Wilt thou have me hit him,” <br> They said to one another, “on the rump?” <br> And answered: “Yes; see that thou nick him with it.” <br> But the same demon who was holding parley <br> With my Conductor turned him very quickly, <br> And said: “Be quiet, be quiet, Scarmiglione”; <br> Then said to us: “You can no farther go <br> Forward upon this crag, because is lying <br> All shattered, at the bottom, the sixth arch. <br> And if it still doth please you to go onward, <br> Pursue your way along upon this rock;<sup>312</sup> <br> Near is another crag that yields a path.<sup>313</sup> <br> Yesterday, five hours later than this hour,<sup>314</sup> <br> One thousand and two hundred sixty-six <br> Years were complete, that here the way was broken.<sup>315</sup> <br> I send in that direction some of mine <br> To see if any one doth air himself; <br> Go ye with them; for they will not be vicious. <br> Step forward, Alichino and Calcabrina,” <br> Began he to cry out, “and thou, Cagnazzo; <br> And Barbariccia, do thou guide the ten. <br> Come forward, Libicocco and Draghignazzo, <br> And tusked Ciriatto and Graffiacane, <br> And Farfarello and mad Rubicante; <br> Search ye all round about the boiling pitch; <br> Let these be safe as far as the next crag,<sup>316</sup> <br> That all unbroken passes o’er the dens.” <br> “O me! what is it, Master, that I see? <br> Pray let us go,” I said, “without an escort, <br> If thou knowest how, since for myself I ask none. <br> If thou art as observant as thy wont is, <br> Dost thou not see that they do gnash their teeth, <br> And with their brows are threatening woe to us?” <br> And he to me: “I will not have thee fear; <br> Let them gnash on, according to their fancy, <br> Because they do it for those boiling wretches.” <br> Along the left-hand dike they wheeled about; <br> But first had each one thrust his tongue between<sup>317</sup> <br> His teeth towards their leader for a signal; <br> And he had made a trumpet of his rump.</p>
