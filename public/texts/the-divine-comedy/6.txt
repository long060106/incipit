<p class="lede">The Second Circle⁠—Minos⁠—The wanton⁠—The infernal hurricane⁠—Francesca da Rimini.</p>
<p class="verse">Thus I descended out of the first circle<sup>73</sup> <br> Down to the second, that less space begirds,<sup>74</sup> <br> And so much greater dole, that goads to wailing. <br> There standeth Minos horribly, and snarls;<sup>75</sup> <br> Examines the transgressions at the entrance; <br> Judges, and sends according as he girds him. <br> I say, that when the spirit evil-born <br> Cometh before him, wholly it confesses; <br> And this discriminator of transgressions <br> Seeth what place in Hell is meet for it; <br> Girds himself with his tail as many times <br> As grades he wishes it should be thrust down. <br> Always before him many of them stand; <br> They go by turns each one unto the judgment; <br> They speak, and hear, and then are downward hurled. <br> “O thou, that to this dolorous hostelry <br> Comest,” said Minos to me, when he saw me, <br> Leaving the practice of so great an office, <br> “Look how thou enterest, and in whom thou trustest; <br> Let not the portal’s amplitude deceive thee.” <br> And unto him my Guide: “Why criest thou too?<sup>76</sup> <br> Do not impede his journey fate-ordained; <br> It is so willed there where is power to do <br> That which is willed; and ask no further question.” <br> And now begin the dolesome notes to grow <br> Audible unto me; now am I come <br> There where much lamentation strikes upon me. <br> I came into a place mute of all light,<sup>77</sup> <br> Which bellows as the sea does in a tempest, <br> If by opposing winds ’tis combated. <br> The infernal hurricane that never rests <br> Hurtles the spirits onward in its rapine; <br> Whirling them round, and smiting, it molests them. <br> When they arrive before the precipice, <br> There are the shrieks, the plaints, and the laments, <br> There they blaspheme the puissance divine. <br> I understood that unto such a torment <br> The carnal malefactors were condemned, <br> Who reason subjugate to appetite. <br> And as the wings of starlings bear them on <br> In the cold season in large band and full, <br> So doth that blast the spirits maledict; <br> It hither, thither, downward, upward, drives them; <br> No hope doth comfort them forevermore, <br> Not of repose, but even of lesser pain. <br> And as the cranes go chanting forth their lays, <br> Making in air a long line of themselves, <br> So saw I coming, uttering lamentations, <br> Shadows borne onward by the aforesaid stress. <br> Whereupon said I: “Master, who are those <br> People, whom the black air so castigates?”<sup>78</sup> <br> “The first of those, of whom intelligence <br> Thou fain wouldst have,” then said he unto me, <br> “The empress was of many languages. <br> To sensual vices she was so abandoned, <br> That lustful she made licit in her law, <br> To remove the blame to which she had been led. <br> She is Semiramis, of whom we read <br> That she succeeded Ninus, and was his spouse; <br> She held the land which now the Sultan rules. <br> The next is she who killed herself for love,<sup>79</sup> <br> And broke faith with the ashes of Sichaeus; <br> Then Cleopatra the voluptuous.” <br> Helen I saw, for whom so many ruthless <br> Seasons revolved; and saw the great Achilles,<sup>80</sup> <br> Who at the last hour combated with Love. <br> Paris I saw, Tristan; and more than a thousand<sup>81</sup> <br> Shades did he name and point out with his finger, <br> Whom Love had separated from our life. <br> After that I had listened to my Teacher, <br> Naming the dames of eld and cavaliers,<sup>82</sup> <br> Pity prevailed, and I was nigh bewildered. <br> And I began: “O Poet, willingly <br> Speak would I to those two, who go together, <br> And seem upon the wind to be so light.” <br> And he to me: “Thou’lt mark, when they shall be <br> Nearer to us; and then do thou implore them <br> By love which leadeth them, and they will come.” <br> Soon as the wind in our direction sways them, <br> My voice uplift I: “O ye weary souls! <br> Come speak to us, if no one interdicts it.” <br> As turtledoves, called onward by desire, <br> With open and steady wings to the sweet nest <br> Fly through the air by their volition borne, <br> So came they from the band where Dido is, <br> Approaching us athwart the air malign, <br> So strong was the affectionate appeal. <br> “O living creature gracious and benignant, <br> Who visiting goest through the purple air<sup>83</sup> <br> Us, who have stained the world incarnadine, <br> If were the King of the Universe our friend, <br> We would pray unto him to give thee peace, <br> Since thou hast pity on our woe perverse. <br> Of what it pleases thee to hear and speak, <br> That will we hear, and we will speak to you, <br> While silent is the wind, as it is now. <br> Sitteth the city, wherein I was born,<sup>84</sup> <br> Upon the seashore where the Po descends <br> To rest in peace with all his retinue.<sup>85</sup> <br> Love, that on gentle heart doth swiftly seize, <br> Seized this man for the person beautiful <br> That was ta’en from me, and still the mode offends me. <br> Love, that exempts no one beloved from loving,<sup>86</sup> <br> Seized me with pleasure of this man so strongly,<sup>87</sup> <br> That, as thou seest, it doth not yet desert me; <br> Love has conducted us unto one death; <br> Caïna waiteth him who quenched our life!”<sup>88</sup> <br> These words were borne along from them to us. <br> As soon as I had heard those souls tormented, <br> I bowed my face, and so long held it down <br> Until the Poet said to me: “What thinkest?” <br> When I made answer, I began: “Alas! <br> How many pleasant thoughts, how much desire, <br> Conducted these unto the dolorous pass!” <br> Then unto them I turned me, and I spake, <br> And I began: “Thine agonies, Francesca, <br> Sad and compassionate to weeping make me. <br> But tell me, at the time of those sweet sighs, <br> By what and in what manner Love conceded,<sup>89</sup> <br> That you should know your dubious desires?” <br> And she to me: “There is no greater sorrow<sup>90</sup> <br> Than to be mindful of the happy time <br> In misery, and that thy Teacher knows. <br> But, if to recognise the earliest root <br> Of love in us thou hast so great desire, <br> I will do even as he who weeps and speaks. <br> One day we reading were for our delight <br> Of Launcelot, how Love did him enthrall. <br> Alone we were and without any fear. <br> Full many a time our eyes together drew <br> That reading, and drove the color from our faces; <br> But one point only was it that o’ercame us. <br> Whenas we read of the much longed-for smile <br> Being by such a noble lover kissed, <br> This one, who ne’er from me shall be divided, <br> Kissed me upon the mouth all palpitating. <br> Galeotto was the book and he who wrote it. <br> That day no farther did we read therein.” <br> And all the while one spirit uttered this, <br> The other one did weep so, that, for pity, <br> I swooned away as if I had been dying, <br> And fell, even as a dead body falls.<sup>91</sup></p>
