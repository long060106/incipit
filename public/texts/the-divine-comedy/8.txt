<p class="lede">The Fourth Circle⁠—Plutus⁠—The avaricious and the prodigal⁠—Fortune and her wheel⁠—The Fifth Circle⁠—Styx⁠—The irascible and the sullen.</p>
<p class="verse">“Papë Satàn, Papë Satàn, Aleppë!”<sup>101</sup> <br> Thus Plutus with his clucking voice began; <br> And that benignant Sage, who all things knew, <br> Said, to encourage me: “Let not thy fear <br> Harm thee; for any power that he may have <br> Shall not prevent thy going down this crag.” <br> Then he turned round unto that bloated lip, <br> And said: “Be silent, thou accursed wolf; <br> Consume within thyself with thine own rage. <br> Not causeless is this journey to the abyss; <br> Thus is it willed on high, where Michael wrought<sup>102</sup> <br> Vengeance upon the proud adultery.” <br> Even as the sails inflated by the wind <br> Together fall involved when snaps the mast, <br> So fell the cruel monster to the earth. <br> Thus we descended into the fourth chasm, <br> Gaining still farther on the dolesome shore <br> Which all the woe of the universe insacks. <br> Justice of God, ah! who heaps up so many <br> New toils and sufferings as I beheld? <br> And why doth our transgression waste us so? <br> As doth the billow there upon Charybdis, <br> That breaks itself on that which it encounters, <br> So here the folk must dance their roundelay.<sup>103</sup> <br> Here saw I people, more than elsewhere, many, <br> On one side and the other, with great howls, <br> Rolling weights forward by main-force of chest.<sup>104</sup> <br> They clashed together, and then at that point <br> Each one turned backward, rolling retrograde, <br> Crying, “Why keepest?” and, “Why squanderest thou?” <br> Thus they returned along the lurid circle <br> On either hand unto the opposite point, <br> Shouting their shameful metre evermore. <br> Then each, when he arrived there, wheeled about <br> Through his half-circle to another joust; <br> And I, who had my heart pierced as it were, <br> Exclaimed: “My Master, now declare to me <br> What people these are, and if all were clerks, <br> These shaven crowns upon the left of us.”<sup>105</sup> <br> And he to me: “All of them were asquint <br> In intellect in the first life, so much <br> That there with measure they no spending made. <br> Clearly enough their voices bark it forth, <br> Whene’er they reach the two points of the circle, <br> Where sunders them the opposite defect. <br> Clerks those were who no hairy covering <br> Have on the head, and Popes and Cardinals, <br> In whom doth Avarice practise its excess.” <br> And I: “My Master, among such as these <br> I ought forsooth to recognise some few, <br> Who were infected with these maladies.” <br> And he to me: “Vain thought thou entertainest; <br> The undiscerning life which made them sordid <br> Now makes them unto all discernment dim. <br> Forever shall they come to these two buttings; <br> These from the sepulchre shall rise again <br> With the fist closed, and these with tresses shorn. <br> Ill giving and ill keeping the fair world<sup>106</sup> <br> Have ta’en from them, and placed them in this scuffle; <br> Whate’er it be, no words adorn I for it. <br> Now canst thou, Son, behold the transient farce <br> Of goods that are committed unto Fortune, <br> For which the human race each other buffet; <br> For all the gold that is beneath the moon, <br> Or ever has been, of these weary souls <br> Could never make a single one repose.” <br> “Master,” I said to him, “now tell me also <br> What is this Fortune which thou speakest of,<sup>107</sup> <br> That has the world’s goods so within its clutches?” <br> And he to me: “O creatures imbecile, <br> What ignorance is this which doth beset you? <br> Now will I have thee learn my judgment of her. <br> He whose omniscience everything transcends <br> The heavens created, and gave who should guide them,<sup>108</sup> <br> That every part to every part may shine, <br> Distributing the light in equal measure; <br> He in like manner to the mundane splendors <br> Ordained a general ministress and guide, <br> That she might change at times the empty treasures <br> From race to race, from one blood to another, <br> Beyond resistance of all human wisdom. <br> Therefore one people triumphs, and another <br> Languishes, in pursuance of her judgment, <br> Which hidden is, as in the grass a serpent. <br> Your knowledge has no counterstand against her; <br> She makes provision, judges, and pursues <br> Her governance, as theirs the other gods. <br> Her permutations have not any truce; <br> Necessity makes her precipitate, <br> So often cometh who his turn obtains. <br> And this is she who is so crucified <br> Even by those who ought to give her praise, <br> Giving her blame amiss, and bad repute. <br> But she is blissful, and she hears it not; <br> Among the other primal creatures gladsome <br> She turns her sphere, and blissful she rejoices. <br> Let us descend now unto greater woe; <br> Already sinks each star that was ascending<sup>109</sup> <br> When I set out, and loitering is forbidden.” <br> We crossed the circle to the other bank, <br> Near to a fount that boils, and pours itself <br> Along a gully that runs out of it. <br> The water was more sombre far than perse;<sup>110</sup> <br> And we, in company with the dusky waves, <br> Made entrance downward by a path uncouth. <br> A marsh it makes, which has the name of Styx, <br> This tristful brooklet, when it has descended <br> Down to the foot of the malign gray shores. <br> And I, who stood intent upon beholding, <br> Saw people mud-besprent in that lagoon, <br> All of them naked and with angry look. <br> They smote each other not alone with hands, <br> But with the head and with the breast and feet, <br> Tearing each other piecemeal with their teeth. <br> Said the good Master: “Son, thou now beholdest<sup>111</sup> <br> The souls of those whom anger overcame; <br> And likewise I would have thee know for certain <br> Beneath the water people are who sigh <br> And make this water bubble at the surface, <br> As the eye tells thee wheresoe’er it turns. <br> Fixed in the mire they say, ‘We sullen were <br> In the sweet air, which by the sun is gladdened, <br> Bearing within ourselves the sluggish reek; <br> Now we are sullen in this sable mire.’ <br> This hymn do they keep gurgling in their throats, <br> For with unbroken words they cannot say it.” <br> Thus we went circling round the filthy fen <br> A great arc ’twixt the dry bank and the swamp, <br> With eyes turned unto those who gorge the mire; <br> Unto the foot of a tower we came at last.</p>
