<p class="lede">The Tree of Knowledge.</p>
<p class="verse">So steadfast and attentive were mine eyes<sup>1147</sup> <br> In satisfying their decennial thirst,<sup>1148</sup> <br> That all my other senses were extinct, <br> And upon this side and on that they had <br> Walls of indifference, so the holy smile <br> Drew them unto itself with the old net; <br> When forcibly my sight was turned away <br> Towards my left hand by those goddesses, <br> Because I heard from them a “Too intently!” <br> And that condition of the sight which is<sup>1149</sup> <br> In eyes but lately smitten by the sun <br> Bereft me of my vision some short while; <br> But to the less when sight reshaped itself, <br> I say the less in reference to the greater <br> Splendor from which perforce I had withdrawn, <br> I saw upon its right wing wheeled about <br> The glorious host returning with the sun <br> And with the sevenfold flames upon their faces. <br> As underneath its shields, to save itself, <br> A squadron turns, and with its banner wheels, <br> Before the whole thereof can change its front, <br> That soldiery of the celestial kingdom <br> Which marched in the advance had wholly passed us <br> Before the chariot had turned its pole. <br> Then to the wheels the maidens turned themselves, <br> And the Griffin moved his burden benedight, <br> But so that not a feather of him fluttered. <br> The lady fair who drew me through the ford <br> Followed with Statius and myself the wheel <br> Which made its orbit with the lesser arc. <br> So passing through the lofty forest, vacant <br> By fault of her who in the serpent trusted, <br> Angelic music made our steps keep time. <br> Perchance as great a space had in three flights <br> An arrow loosened from the string o’erpassed,<sup>1150</sup> <br> As we had moved when Beatrice descended. <br> I heard them murmur altogether, “Adam!” <br> Then circled they about a tree despoiled<sup>1151</sup> <br> Of blooms and other leafage on each bough. <br> Its tresses, which so much the more dilate <br> As higher they ascend, had been by Indians<sup>1152</sup> <br> Among their forests marvelled at for height. <br> “Blessed art thou, O Griffin, who dost not<sup>1153</sup> <br> Pluck with thy beak these branches sweet to taste, <br> Since appetite by this was turned to evil.” <br> After this fashion round the tree robust <br> The others shouted; and the twofold creature: <br> “Thus is preserved the seed of all the just.” <br> And turning to the pole which he had dragged, <br> He drew it close beneath the widowed bough, <br> And what was of it unto it left bound.<sup>1154</sup> <br> In the same manner as our trees (when downward <br> Falls the great light, with that together mingled <br> Which after the celestial Lasca shines)<sup>1155</sup> <br> Begin to swell, and then renew themselves, <br> Each one with its own color, ere the Sun <br> Harness his steeds beneath another star:<sup>1156</sup> <br> Less than of rose and more than violet <br> A hue disclosing, was renewed the tree <br> That had erewhile its boughs so desolate. <br> I never heard, nor here below is sung, <br> The hymn which afterward that people sang, <br> Nor did I bear the melody throughout. <br> Had I the power to paint how fell asleep <br> Those eyes compassionless, of Syrinx hearing,<sup>1157</sup> <br> Those eyes to which more watching cost so dear, <br> Even as a painter who from model paints <br> I would portray how I was lulled asleep; <br> He may, who well can picture drowsihood. <br> Therefore I pass to what time I awoke, <br> And say a splendor rent from me the veil <br> Of slumber, and a calling: “Rise, what dost thou?” <br> As to behold the apple-tree in blossom<sup>1158</sup> <br> Which makes the Angels greedy for its fruit, <br> And keeps perpetual bridals in the Heaven, <br> Peter and John and James conducted were, <br> And, overcome, recovered at the word<sup>1159</sup> <br> By which still greater slumbers have been broken, <br> And saw their school diminished by the loss <br> Not only of Elias, but of Moses, <br> And the apparel of their Master changed; <br> So I revived, and saw that piteous one<sup>1160</sup> <br> Above me standing, who had been conductress <br> Aforetime of my steps beside the river, <br> And all in doubt I said, “Where’s Beatrice?” <br> And she: “Behold her seated underneath <br> The leafage new, upon the root of it. <br> Behold the company that circles her; <br> The rest behind the Griffin are ascending <br> With more melodious song, and more profound.” <br> And if her speech were more diffuse I know not, <br> Because already in my sight was she <br> Who from the hearing of aught else had shut me. <br> Alone she sat upon the very earth, <br> Left there as guardian of the chariot <br> Which I had seen the biform monster fasten. <br> Encircling her, a cloister made themselves <br> The seven Nymphs, with those lights in their hands<sup>1161</sup> <br> Which are secure from Aquilon and Auster. <br> “Short while shalt thou be here a forester, <br> And thou shalt be with me for evermore <br> A citizen of that Rome where Christ is Roman. <br> Therefore, for that world’s good which liveth ill, <br> Fix on the car thine eyes, and what thou seest, <br> Having returned to earth, take heed thou write.” <br> Thus Beatrice; and I, who at the feet <br> Of her commandments all devoted was, <br> My mind and eyes directed where she willed. <br> Never descended with so swift a motion <br> Fire from a heavy cloud, when it is raining <br> From out the region which is most remote, <br> As I beheld the bird of Jove descend<sup>1162</sup> <br> Down through the tree, rending away the bark, <br> As well as blossoms and the foliage new, <br> And he with all his might the chariot smote,<sup>1163</sup> <br> Whereat it reeled, like vessel in a tempest <br> Tossed by the waves, now starboard and now larboard. <br> Thereafter saw I leap into the body <br> Of the triumphal vehicle a Fox,<sup>1164</sup> <br> That seemed unfed with any wholesome food. <br> But for his hideous sins upbraiding him, <br> My Lady put him to as swift a flight <br> As such a fleshless skeleton could bear. <br> Then by the way that it before had come, <br> Into the chariot’s chest I saw the Eagle <br> Descend, and leave it feathered with his plumes.<sup>1165</sup> <br> And such as issues from a heart that mourns, <br> A voice from Heaven there issued, and it said: <br> “My little bark, how badly art thou freighted!” <br> Methought, then, that the earth did yawn between <br> Both wheels, and I saw rise from it a Dragon,<sup>1166</sup> <br> Who through the chariot upward fixed his tail, <br> And as a wasp that draweth back its sting, <br> Drawing unto himself his tail malign, <br> Drew out the floor, and went his way rejoicing. <br> That which remained behind, even as with grass <br> A fertile region, with the feathers, offered <br> Perhaps with pure intention and benign, <br> Reclothed itself, and with them were reclothed <br> The pole and both the wheels so speedily, <br> A sigh doth longer keep the lips apart. <br> Transfigured thus the holy edifice <br> Thrust forward heads upon the parts of it, <br> Three on the pole and one at either corner.<sup>1167</sup> <br> The first were horned like oxen; but the four <br> Had but a single horn upon the forehead; <br> A monster such had never yet been seen! <br> Firm as a rock upon a mountain high, <br> Seated upon it, there appeared to me <br> A shameless whore, with eyes swift glancing round,<sup>1168</sup> <br> And, as if not to have her taken from him, <br> Upright beside her I beheld a giant;<sup>1169</sup> <br> And ever and anon they kissed each other. <br> But because she her wanton, roving eye <br> Turned upon me, her angry paramour <br> Did scourge her from her head unto her feet.<sup>1170</sup> <br> Then full of jealousy, and fierce with wrath, <br> He loosed the monster, and across the forest <br> Dragged it so far, he made of that alone<sup>1171</sup> <br> A shield unto the whore and the strange beast.</p>
