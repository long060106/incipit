<p class="lede">Dante’s dream of the Siren⁠—The Fifth Circle⁠—The Avaricious and Prodigal⁠—Pope Adrian V.</p>
<p class="verse">It was the hour when the diurnal heat<sup>886</sup> <br> No more can warm the coldness of the moon, <br> Vanquished by earth, or peradventure Saturn,<sup>887</sup> <br> When geomancers their Fortuna Major<sup>888</sup> <br> See in the orient before the dawn <br> Rise by a path that long remains not dim,<sup>889</sup> <br> There came to me in dreams a stammering woman,<sup>890</sup> <br> Squint in her eyes, and in her feet distorted, <br> With hands dissevered and of sallow hue. <br> I looked at her; and as the sun restores <br> The frigid members which the night benumbs, <br> Even thus my gaze did render voluble <br> Her tongue, and made her all erect thereafter <br> In little while, and the lost countenance <br> As love desires it so in her did color. <br> When in this wise she had her speech unloosed, <br> She ’gan to sing so, that with difficulty <br> Could I have turned my thoughts away from her. <br> “I am,” she sang, “I am the Siren sweet <br> Who mariners amid the main unman, <br> So full am I of pleasantness to hear. <br> I drew Ulysses from his wandering way <br> Unto my song, and he who dwells with me <br> Seldom departs so wholly I content him.” <br> Her mouth was not yet closed again, before <br> Appeared a Lady saintly and alert <br> Close at my side to put her to confusion. <br> “Virgilius, O Virgilius! who is this?” <br> Sternly she said; and he was drawing near <br> With eyes still fixed upon that modest one. <br> She seized the other and in front laid open, <br> Rending her garments, and her belly showed me; <br> This waked me with the stench that issued from it. <br> I turned mine eyes, and good Virgilius said: <br> “At least thrice have I called thee; rise and come; <br> Find we the opening by which thou mayst enter.” <br> I rose; and full already of high day <br> Were all the circles of the Sacred Mountain, <br> And with the new sun at our back we went. <br> Following behind him, I my forehead bore <br> Like unto one who has it laden with thought, <br> Who makes himself the half arch of a bridge, <br> When I heard say, “Come, here the passage is,” <br> Spoken in a manner gentle and benign, <br> Such as we hear not in this mortal region. <br> With open wings, which of a swan appeared, <br> Upward he turned us who thus spake to us, <br> Between the two walls of the solid granite. <br> He moved his pinions afterwards and fanned us, <br> Affirming those <i>qui lugent</i> to be blessed, <br> For they shall have their souls with comfort filled.<sup>891</sup> <br> “What aileth thee, that aye to earth thou gazest?” <br> To me my Guide began to say, we both <br> Somewhat beyond the Angel having mounted. <br> And I: “With such misgiving makes me go <br> A vision new, which bends me to itself, <br> So that I cannot from the thought withdraw me.” <br> “Didst thou behold,” he said, “that old enchantress, <br> Who sole above us henceforth is lamented?<sup>892</sup> <br> Didst thou behold how man is freed from her? <br> Suffice it thee, and smite earth with thy heels,<sup>893</sup> <br> Thine eyes lift upward to the lure, that whirls <br> The Eternal King with revolutions vast.” <br> Even as the hawk, that first his feet surveys, <br> Then turns him to the call and stretches forward, <br> Through the desire of food that draws him thither, <br> Such I became, and such, as far as cleaves <br> The rock to give a way to him who mounts, <br> Went on to where the circling doth begin. <br> On the fifth circle when I had come forth, <br> People I saw upon it who were weeping, <br> Stretched prone upon the ground, all downward turned. <br> “<i>Adhaesit pavimento anima mea</i>,”<sup>894</sup> <br> I heard them say with sighings so profound, <br> That hardly could the words be understood. <br> “O ye elect of God, whose sufferings <br> Justice and Hope both render less severe, <br> Direct ye us towards the high ascents.” <br> “If ye are come secure from this prostration, <br> And wish to find the way most speedily, <br> Let your right hands be evermore outside.” <br> Thus did the Poet ask, and thus was answered <br> By them somewhat in front of us; whence I <br> In what was spoken divined the rest concealed, <br> And unto my Lord’s eyes mine eyes I turned; <br> Whence he assented with a cheerful sign <br> To what the sight of my desire implored. <br> When of myself I could dispose at will, <br> Above that creature did I draw myself, <br> Whose words before had caused me to take note, <br> Saying: “O Spirit, in whom weeping ripens <br> That without which to God we cannot turn, <br> Suspend awhile for me thy greater care. <br> Who wast thou, and why are your backs turned upwards, <br> Tell me, and if thou wouldst that I procure thee <br> Anything there whence living I departed.” <br> And he to me: “Wherefore our backs the heaven <br> Turns to itself, know shalt thou; but beforehand <br>  <i>Scias quod ego fui successor Petri.</i><sup>895</sup>  <br> Between Siestri and Chiaveri descends <br> A river beautiful, and of its name <br> The title of my blood its summit makes. <br> A month and little more essayed I how <br> Weighs the great cloak on him from mire who keeps it; <br> For all the other burdens seem a feather. <br> Tardy, ah woe is me! was my conversion; <br> But when the Roman Shepherd I was made, <br> Then I discovered life to be a lie. <br> I saw that there the heart was not at rest, <br> Nor farther in that life could one ascend; <br> Whereby the love of this was kindled in me. <br> Until that time a wretched soul and parted <br> From God was I, and wholly avaricious; <br> Now, as thou seest, I here am punished for it. <br> What avarice does is here made manifest <br> In the purgation of these souls converted, <br> And no more bitter pain the Mountain has. <br> Even as our eye did not uplift itself <br> Aloft, being fastened upon earthly things, <br> So justice here has merged it in the earth. <br> As avarice had extinguished our affection <br> For every good, whereby was action lost, <br> So justice here doth hold us in restraint, <br> Bound and imprisoned by the feet and hands; <br> And so long as it pleases the just Lord <br> Shall we remain immovable and prostrate.” <br> I on my knees had fallen, and wished to speak; <br> But even as I began, and he was ’ware, <br> Only by listening, of my reverence, <br> “What cause,” he said, “has downward bent thee thus?” <br> And I to him: “For your own dignity, <br> Standing, my conscience stung me with remorse.” <br> “Straighten thy legs, and upward raise thee, brother,” <br> He answered: “Err not, fellow-servant am I<sup>896</sup> <br> With thee and with the others to one power. <br> If e’er that holy, evangelic sound, <br> Which sayeth <i>neque nubent</i>, thou hast heard,<sup>897</sup> <br> Well canst thou see why in this wise I speak. <br> Now go; no longer will I have thee linger, <br> Because thy stay doth incommode my weeping, <br> With which I ripen that which thou hast said.<sup>898</sup> <br> On earth I have a grandchild named Alagia,<sup>899</sup> <br> Good in herself, unless indeed our house <br> Malevolent may make her by example, <br> And she alone remains to me on earth.”</p>
