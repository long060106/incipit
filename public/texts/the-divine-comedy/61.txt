<p class="lede">Discourse of Statius on generation⁠—The Seventh Circle⁠—The Wanton.</p>
<p class="verse">Now was it the ascent no hindrance brooked,<sup>1019</sup> <br> Because the sun had his meridian circle <br> To Taurus left, and night to Scorpio;<sup>1020</sup> <br> Wherefore as doth a man who tarries not, <br> But goes his way, whate’er to him appear, <br> If of necessity the sting transfix him, <br> In this wise did we enter through the gap, <br> Taking the stairway, one before the other, <br> Which by its narrowness divides the climbers. <br> And as the little stork that lifts its wing <br> With a desire to fly, and does not venture <br> To leave the nest, and lets it downward droop, <br> Even such was I, with the desire of asking <br> Kindled and quenched, unto the motion coming <br> He makes who doth address himself to speak.<sup>1021</sup> <br> Not for our pace, though rapid it might be, <br> My father sweet forbore, but said: “Let fly <br> The bow of speech thou to the barb hast drawn.” <br> With confidence I opened then my mouth, <br> And I began: “How can one meagre grow <br> There where the need of nutriment applies not?” <br> “If thou wouldst call to mind how Meleager<sup>1022</sup> <br> Was wasted by the wasting of a brand, <br> This would not,” said he, “be to thee so sour; <br> And wouldst thou think how at each tremulous motion <br> Trembles within a mirror your own image; <br> That which seems hard would mellow seem to thee. <br> But that thou mayst content thee in thy wish <br> Lo Statius here; and him I call and pray <br> He now will be the healer of thy wounds.” <br> “If I unfold to him the eternal vengeance,” <br> Responded Statius, “where thou present art, <br> Be my excuse that I can naught deny thee.” <br> Then he began: “Son, if these words of mine <br> Thy mind doth contemplate and doth receive, <br> They’ll be thy light unto the How thou sayest. <br> The perfect blood, which never is drunk up<sup>1023</sup> <br> Into the thirsty veins, and which remaineth <br> Like food that from the table thou removest, <br> Takes in the heart for all the human members <br> Virtue informative, as being that <br> Which to be changed to them goes through the veins. <br> Again digest, descends it where ’tis better <br> Silent to be than say; and then drops thence <br> Upon another’s blood in natural vase. <br> There one together with the other mingles, <br> One to be passive meant, the other active <br> By reason of the perfect place it springs from;<sup>1024</sup> <br> And being conjoined, begins to operate, <br> Coagulating first, then vivifying <br> What for its matter it had made consistent. <br> The active virtue, being made a soul<sup>1025</sup> <br> As of a plant, (in so far different, <br> This on the way is, that arrived already,) <br> Then works so much, that now it moves and feels<sup>1026</sup> <br> Like a sea-fungus, and then undertakes <br> To organize the powers whose seed it is. <br> Now, Son, dilates and now distends itself <br> The virtue from the generator’s heart, <br> Where nature is intent on all the members. <br> But how from animal it man becomes <br> Thou dost not see as yet; this is a point <br> Which made a wiser man than thou once err <br> So far, that in his doctrine separate <br> He made the soul from possible intellect,<sup>1027</sup> <br> For he no organ saw by this assumed. <br> Open thy breast unto the truth that’s coming, <br> And know that, just as soon as in the foetus <br> The articulation of the brain is perfect, <br> The primal Motor turns to it well pleased<sup>1028</sup> <br> At so great art of nature, and inspires <br> A spirit new with virtue all replete, <br> Which what it finds there active doth attract <br> Into its substance, and becomes one soul, <br> Which lives, and feels, and on itself revolves.<sup>1029</sup> <br> And that thou less may wonder at my word, <br> Behold the sun’s heat, which becometh wine, <br> Joined to the juice that from the vine distils. <br> Whenever Lachesis has no more thread,<sup>1030</sup> <br> It separates from the flesh, and virtually <br> Bears with itself the human and divine;<sup>1031</sup> <br> The other faculties are voiceless all; <br> The memory, the intelligence, and the will <br> In action far more vigorous than before. <br> Without a pause it falleth of itself <br> In marvellous way on one shore or the other;<sup>1032</sup> <br> There of its roads it first is cognizant. <br> Soon as the place there circumscribeth it, <br> The virtue informative rays round about, <br> As, and as much as, in the living members. <br> And even as the air, when full of rain, <br> By alien rays that are therein reflected, <br> With divers colors shows itself adorned, <br> So there the neighboring air doth shape itself <br> Into that form which doth impress upon it <br> Virtually the soul that has stood still. <br> And then in manner of the little flame, <br> Which followeth the fire where’er it shifts, <br> After the spirit followeth its new form. <br> Since afterwards it takes from this its semblance, <br> It is called shade; and thence it organizes <br> Thereafter every sense, even to the sight. <br> Thence is it that we speak, and thence we laugh;<sup>1033</sup> <br> Thence is it that we form the tears and sighs, <br> That on the mountain thou mayhap hast heard. <br> According as impress us our desires <br> And other affections, so the shade is shaped, <br> And this is cause of what thou wonderest at.” <br> And now unto the last of all the circles <br> Had we arrived, and to the right hand turned, <br> And were attentive to another care. <br> There the embankment shoots forth flames of fire, <br> And upward doth the cornice breathe a blast <br> That drives them back, and from itself sequesters. <br> Hence we must needs go on the open side, <br> And one by one; and I did fear the fire <br> On this side, and on that the falling down. <br> My Leader said: “Along this place one ought <br> To keep upon the eyes a tightened rein, <br> Seeing that one so easily might err.” <br> “<i>Summae Deus clementiae</i>,” in the bosom<sup>1034</sup> <br> Of the great burning chanted then I heard, <br> Which made me no less eager to turn round; <br> And spirits saw I walking through the flame; <br> Wherefore I looked, to my own steps and theirs <br> Apportioning my sight from time to time. <br> After the close which to that hymn is made, <br> Aloud they shouted, “<i>Virum non cognosco</i>”;<sup>1035</sup> <br> Then recommenced the hymn with voices low. <br> This also ended, cried they: “To the wood <br> Diana ran, and drove forth Helice<sup>1036</sup> <br> Therefrom, who had of Venus felt the poison.” <br> Then to their song returned they; then the wives <br> They shouted, and the husbands who were chaste, <br> As virtue and the marriage vow imposes. <br> And I believe that them this mode suffices, <br> For all the time the fire is burning them; <br> With such care is it needful, and such food, <br> That the last wound of all should be closed up.</p>
