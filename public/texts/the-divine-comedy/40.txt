<p class="lede">Farther ascent of the mountain⁠—The Negligent, who postponed repentance till the last hour⁠—Belacqua.</p>
<p class="verse">Whenever by delight or else by pain, <br> That seizes any faculty of ours, <br> Wholly to that the soul collects itself, <br> It seemeth that no other power it heeds; <br> And this against that error is which thinks <br> One soul above another kindles in us.<sup>572</sup> <br> And hence, whenever aught is heard or seen <br> Which keeps the soul intently bent upon it, <br> Time passes on, and we perceive it not, <br> Because one faculty is that which listens, <br> And other that which the soul keeps entire; <br> This is as if in bonds, and that is free. <br> Of this I had experience positive <br> In hearing and in gazing at that spirit; <br> For fifty full degrees uprisen was<sup>573</sup> <br> The sun, and I had not perceived it, when <br> We came to where those souls with one accord <br> Cried out unto us: “Here is what you ask.” <br> A greater opening ofttimes hedges up <br> With but a little forkful of his thorns <br> The villager, what time the grape imbrowns, <br> Than was the passage-way through which ascended <br> Only my Leader and myself behind him, <br> After that company departed from us. <br> One climbs Sanleo and descends in Noli,<sup>574</sup> <br> And mounts the summit of Bismantova, <br> With feet alone; but here one needs must fly; <br> With the swift pinions and the plumes I say <br> Of great desire, conducted after him <br> Who gave me hope, and made a light for me. <br> We mounted upward through the rifted rock, <br> And on each side the border pressed upon us, <br> And feet and hands the ground beneath required. <br> When we were come upon the upper rim <br> Of the high bank, out on the open slope, <br> “My Master,” said I, “what way shall we take?”<sup>575</sup> <br> And he to me: “No step of thine descend; <br> Still up the mount behind me win thy way, <br> Till some sage escort shall appear to us.” <br> The summit was so high it vanquished sight, <br> And the hillside precipitous far more <br> Than line from middle quadrant to the centre.<sup>576</sup> <br> Spent with fatigue was I, when I began: <br> “O my sweet Father! turn thee and behold <br> How I remain alone, unless thou stay!” <br> “O son,” he said, “up yonder drag thyself,” <br> Pointing me to a terrace somewhat higher, <br> Which on that side encircles all the hill. <br> These words of his so spurred me on, that I <br> Strained every nerve, behind him scrambling up, <br> Until the circle was beneath my feet. <br> Thereon ourselves we seated both of us <br> Turned to the East, from which we had ascended, <br> For all men are delighted to look back. <br> To the low shores mine eyes I first directed, <br> Then to the sun uplifted them, and wondered <br> That on the left hand we were smitten by it. <br> The Poet well perceived that I was wholly <br> Bewildered at the chariot of the light, <br> Where ’twixt us and the Aquilon it entered. <br> Whereon he said to me: “If Castor and Pollux<sup>577</sup> <br> Were in the company of yonder mirror, <br> That up and down conducteth with its light, <br> Thou wouldst behold the zodiac’s jagged wheel<sup>578</sup> <br> Revolving still more near unto the Bears, <br> Unless it swerved aside from its old track. <br> How that may be wouldst thou have power to think, <br> Collected in thyself, imagine Zion <br> Together with this mount on earth to stand, <br> So that they both one sole horizon have, <br> And hemispheres diverse; whereby the road<sup>579</sup> <br> Which Phaeton, alas! knew not to drive, <br> Thou’lt see how of necessity must pass<sup>580</sup> <br> This on one side, when that upon the other, <br> If thine intelligence right clearly heed.” <br> “Truly, my Master,” said I, “never yet <br> Saw I so clearly as I now discern, <br> There where my wit appeared incompetent, <br> That the mid-circle of supernal motion, <br> Which in some art is the Equator called, <br> And aye remains between the Sun and Winter, <br> For reason which thou sayest, departeth hence <br> Tow’rds the Septentrion, what time the Hebrews<sup>581</sup> <br> Beheld it tow’rds the region of the heat. <br> But, if it pleaseth thee, I fain would learn <br> How far we have to go; for the hill rises <br> Higher than eyes of mine have power to rise.” <br> And he to me: “This mount is such, that ever <br> At the beginning down below ’tis tiresome, <br> And aye the more one climbs, the less it hurts. <br> Therefore, when it shall seem so pleasant to thee, <br> That going up shall be to thee as easy <br> As going down the current in a boat, <br> Then at this pathway’s ending thou wilt be; <br> There to repose thy panting breath expect; <br> No more I answer; and this I know for true.” <br> And as he finished uttering these words, <br> A voice close by us sounded: “Peradventure <br> Thou wilt have need of sitting down ere that.” <br> At sound thereof each one of us turned round, <br> And saw upon the left hand a great rock, <br> Which neither I nor he before had noticed. <br> Thither we drew; and there were persons there <br> Who in the shadow stood behind the rock, <br> As one through indolence is wont to stand. <br> And one of them, who seemed to me fatigued, <br> Was sitting down, and both his knees embraced, <br> Holding his face low down between them bowed. <br> “O my sweet Lord,” I said, “do turn thine eye<sup>582</sup> <br> On him who shows himself more negligent <br> Than even if Sloth herself his sister were.” <br> Then he turned round to us, and he gave heed, <br> Just lifting up his eyes above his thigh, <br> And said: “Now go thou up, for thou art valiant.” <br> Then knew I who he was; and the distress, <br> That still a little did my breathing quicken, <br> My going to him hindered not; and after <br> I came to him he hardly raised his head, <br> Saying: “Hast thou seen clearly how the sun <br> O’er thy left shoulder drives his chariot?” <br> His sluggish attitude and his curt words <br> A little unto laughter moved my lips; <br> Then I began: “Belacqua, I grieve not<sup>583</sup> <br> For thee henceforth; but tell me, wherefore seated <br> In this place art thou? Waitest thou an escort? <br> Or has thy usual habit seized upon thee?” <br> And he: “O brother, what’s the use of climbing? <br> Since to my torment would not let me go <br> The Angel of God, who sitteth at the gate. <br> First heaven must needs so long revolve me round <br> Outside thereof, as in my life it did, <br> Since the good sighs I to the end postponed, <br> Unless, e’er that, some prayer may bring me aid<sup>584</sup> <br> Which rises from a heart that lives in grace; <br> What profit others that in heaven are heard not?” <br> Meanwhile the Poet was before me mounting, <br> And saying: “Come now; see the sun has touched <br> Meridian, and from the shore the night <br> Covers already with her foot Morocco.”</p>
