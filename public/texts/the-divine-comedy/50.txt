<p class="lede">Guido del Duca and Renier da Calboli.</p>
<p class="verse">“Who is this one that goes about our mountain,<sup>781</sup> <br> Or ever Death has given him power of flight, <br> And opes his eyes and shuts them at his will?” <br> “I know not who, but know he’s not alone; <br> Ask him thyself, for thou art nearer to him, <br> And gently, so that he may speak, accost him.” <br> Thus did two spirits, leaning tow’rds each other,<sup>782</sup> <br> Discourse about me there on the right hand; <br> Then held supine their faces to address me. <br> And said the one: “O soul, that, fastened still <br> Within the body, tow’rds the heaven art going, <br> For charity console us, and declare <br> Whence comest and who art thou; for thou mak’st us <br> As much to marvel at this grace of thine <br> As must a thing that never yet has been.” <br> And I: “Through midst of Tuscany there wanders <br> A streamlet that is born in Falterona,<sup>783</sup> <br> And not a hundred miles of course suffice it; <br> From thereupon do I this body bring. <br> To tell you who I am were speech in vain, <br> Because my name as yet makes no great noise.” <br> “If well thy meaning I can penetrate <br> With intellect of mine,” then answered me <br> He who first spake, “thou speakest of the Arno.” <br> And said the other to him: “Why concealed <br> This one the appellation of that river, <br> Even as a man doth of things horrible?” <br> And thus the shade that questioned was of this <br> Himself acquitted: “I know not; but truly <br> ’Tis fit the name of such a valley perish; <br> For from its fountain-head (where is so pregnant <br> The Alpine mountain whence is cleft Peloro<sup>784</sup> <br> That in few places it that mark surpasses) <br> To where it yields itself in restoration <br> Of what the heaven doth of the sea dry up, <br> Whence have the rivers that which goes with them, <br> Virtue is like an enemy avoided <br> By all, as is a serpent, through misfortune <br> Of place, or through bad habit that impels them; <br> On which account have so transformed their nature<sup>785</sup> <br> The dwellers in that miserable valley, <br> It seems that Circe had them in her pasture. <br> ’Mid ugly swine, of acorns worthier<sup>786</sup> <br> Than other food for human use created, <br> It first directeth its impoverished way. <br> Curs findeth it thereafter, coming downward,<sup>787</sup> <br> More snarling than their puissance demands, <br> And turns from them disdainfully its muzzle. <br> It goes on falling, and the more it grows, <br> The more it finds the dogs becoming wolves,<sup>788</sup> <br> This maledict and misadventurous ditch. <br> Descended then through many a hollow gulf, <br> It finds the foxes so replete with fraud,<sup>789</sup> <br> They fear no cunning that may master them. <br> Nor will I cease because another hears me; <br> And well ’twill be for him, if still he mind him <br> Of what a truthful spirit to me unravels.<sup>790</sup> <br> Thy grandson I behold, who doth become<sup>791</sup> <br> A hunter of those wolves upon the bank <br> Of the wild stream, and terrifies them all. <br> He sells their flesh, it being yet alive; <br> Thereafter slaughters them like ancient beeves; <br> Many of life, himself of praise, deprives. <br> Blood-stained he issues from the dismal forest;<sup>792</sup> <br> He leaves it such, a thousand years from now <br> In its primeval state ’tis not re-wooded.” <br> As at the announcement of impending ills <br> The face of him who listens is disturbed, <br> From whate’er side the peril seize upon him; <br> So I beheld that other soul, which stood <br> Turned round to listen, grow disturbed and sad, <br> When it had gathered to itself the word. <br> The speech of one and aspect of the other <br> Had me desirous made to know their names, <br> And question mixed with prayers I made thereof, <br> Whereat the spirit which first spake to me <br> Began again: “Thou wishest I should bring me <br> To do for thee what thou’lt not do for me; <br> But since God willeth that in thee shine forth <br> Such grace of his, I’ll not be chary with thee; <br> Know, then, that I Guido del Duca am.<sup>793</sup> <br> My blood was so with envy set on fire, <br> That if I had beheld a man make merry, <br> Thou wouldst have seen me sprinkled o’er with pallor. <br> From my own sowing such the straw I reap! <br> O human race! why dost thou set thy heart <br> Where interdict of partnership must be?<sup>794</sup> <br> This is Renier; this is the boast and honor<sup>795</sup> <br> Of the house of Calboli, where no one since <br> Has made himself the heir of his desert. <br> And not alone his blood is made devoid, <br> ’Twixt Po and mount, and sea-shore and the Reno,<sup>796</sup> <br> Of good required for truth and for diversion;<sup>797</sup> <br> For all within these boundaries is full <br> Of venomous roots, so that too tardily <br> By cultivation now would they diminish. <br> Where is good Lizio, and Arrigo Manardi,<sup>798</sup> <br> Pier Traversaro, and Guido di Carpigna,<sup>799</sup> <br> O Romagnuoli into bastards turned? <br> When in Bologna will a Fabbro rise?<sup>800</sup> <br> When in Faenza a Bernardin di Fosco,<sup>801</sup> <br> The noble scion of ignoble seed? <br> Be not astonished, Tuscan, if I weep, <br> When I remember, with Guido da Prata,<sup>802</sup> <br> Ugolin d’ Azzo, who was living with us, <br> Frederick Tignoso and his company,<sup>803</sup> <br> The house of Traversara, and th’ Anastagi,<sup>804</sup> <br> And one race and the other is extinct; <br> The dames and cavaliers, the toils and ease<sup>805</sup> <br> That filled our souls with love and courtesy, <br> There where the hearts have so malicious grown! <br> O Brettinoro! why dost thou not flee,<sup>806</sup> <br> Seeing that all thy family is gone, <br> And many people, not to be corrupted? <br> Bagnacaval does well in not begetting<sup>807</sup> <br> And ill does Castrocaro, and Conio worse, <br> In taking trouble to beget such Counts. <br> Will do well the Pagani, when their Devil<sup>808</sup> <br> Shall have departed; but not therefore pure <br> Will testimony of them e’er remain. <br> O Ugolin de’ Fantoli, secure<sup>809</sup> <br> Thy name is, since no longer is awaited <br> One who, degenerating, can obscure it! <br> But go now, Tuscan, for it now delights me <br> To weep far better than it does to speak, <br> So much has our discourse my mind distressed.” <br> We were aware that those beloved souls <br> Heard us depart; therefore, by keeping silent, <br> They made us of our pathway confident. <br> When we became alone by going onward, <br> Thunder, when it doth cleave the air, appeared <br> A voice, that counter to us came, exclaiming:<sup>810</sup> <br> “Shall slay me whosoever findeth me!”<sup>811</sup> <br> And fled as the reverberation dies <br> If suddenly the cloud asunder bursts. <br> As soon as hearing had a truce from this, <br> Behold another, with so great a crash, <br> That it resembled thunderings following fast: <br> “I am Aglaurus, who became a stone!”<sup>812</sup> <br> And then, to press myself close to the Poet, <br> I backward, and not forward, took a step. <br> Already on all sides the air was quiet; <br> And said he to me: “That was the hard curb <br> That ought to hold a man within his bounds; <br> But you take in the bait so that the hook <br> Of the old Adversary draws you to him, <br> And hence availeth little curb or call.<sup>813</sup> <br> The heavens are calling you, and wheel around you,<sup>814</sup> <br> Displaying to you their eternal beauties, <br> And still your eye is looking on the ground;<sup>815</sup> <br> Whence He, who all discerns, chastises you.”</p>
