<p class="lede">Count Ugolino and the Archbishop Ruggieri⁠—Third division of the Ninth Circle, Ptolomaea: traitors to their friends⁠—Friar Alberigo, Branco d’ Oria.</p>
<p class="verse">His mouth uplifted from his grim repast,<sup>499</sup> <br> That sinner, wiping it upon the hair <br> Of the same head that he behind had wasted. <br> Then he began: “Thou wilt that I renew <br> The desperate grief, which wrings my heart already <br> To think of only, ere I speak of it; <br> But if my words be seed that may bear fruit <br> Of infamy to the traitor whom I gnaw, <br> Speaking and weeping shalt thou see together. <br> I know not who thou art, nor by what mode <br> Thou hast come down here; but a Florentine <br> Thou seemest to me truly, when I hear thee. <br> Thou hast to know I was Count Ugolino,<sup>500</sup> <br> And this one was Ruggieri the Archbishop; <br> Now I will tell thee why I am such a neighbor. <br> That, by effect of his malicious thoughts, <br> Trusting in him I was made prisoner, <br> And after put to death, I need not say; <br> But ne’ertheless what thou canst not have heard, <br> That is to say, how cruel was my death, <br> Hear shalt thou, and shalt know if he has wronged me. <br> A narrow perforation in the mew,<sup>501</sup> <br> Which bears because of me the title of Famine, <br> And in which others still must be locked up, <br> Had shown me through its opening many moons <br> Already, when I dreamed the evil dream <br> Which of the future rent for me the veil. <br> This one appeared to me as lord and master, <br> Hunting the wolf and whelps upon the mountain <br> For which the Pisans cannot Lucca see.<sup>502</sup> <br> With sleuth-hounds gaunt, and eager, and well trained,<sup>503</sup> <br> Gualandi with Sismondi and Lanfranchi <br> He had sent out before him to the front. <br> After brief course seemed unto me forespent <br> The father and the sons, and with sharp tushes <br> It seemed to me I saw their flanks ripped open. <br> When I before the morrow was awake, <br> Moaning amid their sleep I heard my sons <br> Who with me were, and asking after bread. <br> Cruel indeed art thou, if yet thou grieve not, <br> Thinking of what my heart foreboded me, <br> And weep’st thou not, what art thou wont to weep at? <br> They were awake now, and the hour drew nigh <br> At which our food used to be brought to us, <br> And through his dream was each one apprehensive; <br> And I heard locking up the under door<sup>504</sup> <br> Of the horrible tower; whereat without a word <br> I gazed into the faces of my sons. <br> I wept not, I within so turned to stone; <br> They wept; and darling little Anselm mine <br> Said: ‘Thou dost gaze so, father, what doth ail thee?’ <br> Still not a tear I shed, nor answer made <br> All of that day, nor yet the night thereafter, <br> Until another sun rose on the world. <br> As now a little glimmer made its way <br> Into the dolorous prison, and I saw <br> Upon four faces my own very aspect, <br> Both of my hands in agony I bit; <br> And, thinking that I did it from desire <br> Of eating, on a sudden they uprose, <br> And said they: ‘Father, much less pain ’twill give us <br> If thou do eat of us; thyself didst clothe us <br> With this poor flesh, and do thou strip it off.’ <br> I calmed me then, not to make them more sad. <br> That day we all were silent, and the next. <br> Ah! obdurate earth, wherefore didst thou not open? <br> When we had come unto the fourth day, Gaddo <br> Threw himself down outstretched before my feet, <br> Saying, ‘My father, why dost thou not help me?’ <br> And there he died; and, as thou seest me, <br> I saw the three fall one by one, between <br> The fifth day and the sixth; whence I betook me, <br> Already blind, to groping over each, <br> And three days called them after they were dead; <br> Then hunger did what sorrow could not do.” <br> When he had said this, with his eyes distorted, <br> The wretched skull resumed he with his teeth, <br> Which, as a dog’s, upon the bone were strong. <br> Ah! Pisa, thou opprobrium of the people <br> Of the fair land there where the <i>Sì</i> doth sound,<sup>505</sup> <br> Since slow to punish thee thy neighbors are, <br> Let the Capraia and Gorgona move,<sup>506</sup> <br> And make a hedge across the mouth of Arno, <br> That every person in thee it may drown! <br> For if Count Ugolino had the fame <br> Of having in thy castles thee betrayed,<sup>507</sup> <br> Thou shouldst not on such cross have put his sons.<sup>508</sup> <br> Guiltless of any crime, thou modern Thebes! <br> Their youth made Uguccione and Brigata, <br> And the other two my song doth name above! <br> We passed still farther onward, where the ice <br> Another people ruggedly enswathes, <br> Not downward turned, but all of them reversed. <br> Weeping itself there does not let them weep, <br> And grief that finds a barrier in the eyes <br> Turns itself inward to increase the anguish; <br> Because the earliest tears a cluster form, <br> And, in the manner of a crystal visor, <br> Fill all the cup beneath the eyebrow full. <br> And notwithstanding that, as in a callus, <br> Because of cold all sensibility <br> Its station had abandoned in my face, <br> Still it appeared to me I felt some wind; <br> Whence I: “My Master, who sets this in motion? <br> Is not below here every vapor quenched?” <br> Whence he to me: “Full soon shalt thou be where <br> Thine eye shall answer make to thee of this, <br> Seeing the cause which raineth down the blast.” <br> And one of the wretches of the frozen crust <br> Cried out to us: “O souls so merciless <br> That the last post is given unto you, <br> Lift from mine eyes the rigid veils, that I <br> May vent the sorrow which impregns my heart <br> A little, e’er the weeping recongeal.” <br> Whence I to him: “If thou wouldst have me help thee, <br> Say who thou wast; and if I free thee not, <br> May I go to the bottom of the ice.” <br> Then he replied: “I am Friar Alberigo;<sup>509</sup> <br> He am I of the fruit of the bad garden, <br> Who here a date am getting for my fig.”<sup>510</sup> <br> “O,” said I to him, “now art thou, too, dead?” <br> And he to me: “How may my body fare <br> Up in the world, no knowledge I possess. <br> Such an advantage has this Ptolomaea,<sup>511</sup> <br> That oftentimes the soul descendeth here <br> Sooner than Atropos in motion sets it.<sup>512</sup> <br> And, that thou mayest more willingly remove <br> From off my countenance these glassy tears, <br> Know that as soon as any soul betrays <br> As I have done, his body by a demon <br> Is taken from him, who thereafter rules it, <br> Until his time has wholly been revolved. <br> Itself down rushes into such a cistern; <br> And still perchance above appears the body <br> Of yonder shade, that winters here behind me. <br> This thou shouldst know, if thou hast just come down; <br> It is Ser Branca d’ Oria, and many years<sup>513</sup> <br> Have passed away since he was thus locked up.” <br> “I think,” said I to him, “thou dost deceive me; <br> For Branca d’ Oria is not dead as yet, <br> And eats, and drinks, and sleeps, and puts on clothes.” <br> “In moat above,” said he, “of Malebranche, <br> There where is boiling the tenacious pitch, <br> As yet had Michel Zanche not arrived, <br> When this one left a devil in his stead <br> In his own body and one near of kin, <br> Who made together with him the betrayal. <br> But hitherward stretch out thy hand forthwith, <br> Open mine eyes”;⁠—and open them I did not, <br> And to be rude to him was courtesy. <br> Ah, Genoese! ye men at variance<sup>514</sup> <br> With every virtue, full of every vice! <br> Wherefore are ye not scattered from the world? <br> For with the vilest spirit of Romagna<sup>515</sup> <br> I found of you one such, who for his deeds <br> In soul already in Cocytus bathes, <br> And still above in body seems alive!</p>
