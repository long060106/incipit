<p class="lede">Dante’s dream of anger⁠—The Fourth Circle⁠—The Slothful.</p>
<p class="verse">Remember, Reader, if e’er in the Alps<sup>851</sup> <br> A mist o’ertook thee, through which thou couldst see<sup>852</sup> <br> Not otherwise than through its membrane mole, <br> How, when the vapors humid and condensed <br> Begin to dissipate themselves, the sphere <br> Of the sun feebly enters in among them, <br> And thy imagination will be swift <br> In coming to perceive how I re-saw <br> The sun at first, that was already setting. <br> Thus, to the faithful footsteps of my Master <br> Mating mine own, I issued from that cloud <br> To rays already dead on the low shores. <br> O thou, Imagination, that dost steal us <br> So from without sometimes, that man perceives not, <br> Although around may sound a thousand trumpets, <br> Who moveth thee, if sense impel thee not? <br> Moves thee a light, which in the heaven takes form, <br> By self, or by a will that downward guides it. <br> Of her impiety, who changed her form<sup>853</sup> <br> Into the bird that most delights in singing, <br> In my imagining appeared the trace; <br> And hereupon my mind was so withdrawn <br> Within itself, that from without there came <br> Nothing that then might be received by it. <br> Then rained within my lofty fantasy<sup>854</sup> <br> One crucified, disdainful and ferocious <br> In countenance, and even thus was dying. <br> Around him were the great Ahasuerus, <br> Esther his wife, and the just Mordecai, <br> Who was in word and action so entire. <br> And even as this image burst asunder <br> Of its own self, in fashion of a bubble <br> In which the water it was made of fails, <br> There rose up in my vision a young maiden<sup>855</sup> <br> Bitterly weeping, and she said: “O queen, <br> Why hast thou wished in anger to be naught? <br> Thou’st slain thyself, Lavinia not to lose; <br> Now hast thou lost me; I am she who mourns, <br> Mother, at thine ere at another’s ruin.” <br> As sleep is broken, when upon a sudden <br> New light strikes in upon the eyelids closed, <br> And broken quivers e’er it dieth wholly, <br> So this imagining of mine fell down <br> As soon as the effulgence smote my face, <br> Greater by far than what is in our wont. <br> I turned me round to see where I might be, <br> When said a voice, “Here is the passage up”; <br> Which from all other purposes removed me, <br> And made my wish so full of eagerness <br> To look and see who was it that was speaking, <br> It never rests till meeting face to face; <br> But as before the sun, which quells the sight, <br> And in its own excess its figure veils,<sup>856</sup> <br> Even so my power was insufficient here. <br> “This is a spirit divine, who in the way <br> Of going up directs us without asking, <br> And who with his own light himself conceals. <br> He does with us as man doth with himself; <br> For he who sees the need, and waits the asking, <br> Malignly leans already tow’rds denial. <br> Accord we now our feet to such inviting, <br> Let us make haste to mount ere it grow dark; <br> For then we could not till the day return.” <br> Thus my Conductor said; and I and he <br> Together turned our footsteps to a stairway; <br> And I, as soon as the first step I reached, <br> Near me perceived a motion as of wings, <br> And fanning in the face, and saying, “<i>Beati</i><sup>857</sup> <br> <i>Pacifici</i>, who are without ill anger.” <br> Already over us were so uplifted <br> The latest sunbeams, which the night pursues, <br> That upon many sides the stars appeared. <br> “O manhood mine, why dost thou vanish so?” <br> I said within myself; for I perceived <br> The vigor of my legs was put in truce. <br> We at the point were where no more ascends <br> The stairway upward, and were motionless, <br> Even as a ship, which at the shore arrives; <br> And I gave heed a little, if I might hear <br> Aught whatsoever in the circle new; <br> Then to my Master turned me round and said: <br> “Say, my sweet Father, what delinquency <br> Is purged here in the circle where we are? <br> Although our feet may pause, pause not thy speech.” <br> And he to me: “The love of good, remiss<sup>858</sup> <br> In what it should have done, is here restored; <br> Here plied again the ill-belated oar; <br> But still more openly to understand, <br> Turn unto me thy mind, and thou shalt gather <br> Some profitable fruit from our delay. <br> Neither Creator nor a creature ever, <br> Son,” he began, “was destitute of love <br> Natural or spiritual; and thou knowest it. <br> The natural was ever without error; <br> But err the other may by evil object, <br> Or by too much, or by too little vigor. <br> While in the first it well directed is,<sup>859</sup> <br> And in the second moderates itself, <br> It cannot be the cause of sinful pleasure; <br> But when to ill it turns, and, with more care <br> Or lesser than it ought, runs after good, <br> ’Gainst the Creator works his own creation. <br> Hence thou mayst comprehend that love must be <br> The seed within yourselves of every virtue, <br> And every act that merits punishment. <br> Now inasmuch as never from the welfare <br> Of its own subject can love turn its sight, <br> From their own hatred all things are secure; <br> And since we cannot think of any being <br> Standing alone, nor from the First divided, <br> Of hating Him is all desire cut off. <br> Hence if, discriminating, I judge well, <br> The evil that one loves is of one’s neighbor, <br> And this is born in three modes in your clay. <br> There are, who, by abasement of their neighbor, <br> Hope to excel, and therefore only long <br> That from his greatness he may be cast down; <br> There are, who power, grace, honor, and renown <br> Fear they may lose because another rises, <br> Thence are so sad that the reverse they love; <br> And there are those whom injury seems to chafe, <br> So that it makes them greedy for revenge, <br> And such must needs shape out another’s harm. <br> This threefold love is wept for down below;<sup>860</sup> <br> Now of the other will I have thee hear, <br> That runneth after good with measure faulty. <br> Each one confusedly a good conceives <br> Wherein the mind may rest, and longeth for it; <br> Therefore to overtake it each one strives. <br> If languid love to look on this attract you, <br> Or in attaining unto it, this cornice, <br> After just penitence, torments you for it. <br> There’s other good that does not make man happy; <br> ’Tis not felicity, ’tis not the good <br> Essence, of every good the fruit and root. <br> The love that yields itself too much to this<sup>861</sup> <br> Above us is lamented in three circles; <br> But how tripartite it may be described, <br> I say not, that thou seek it for thyself.”</p>
