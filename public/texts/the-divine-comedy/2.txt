<p class="lede">The dark forest⁠—The hill of Difficulty⁠—The panther, the lion, and the wolf⁠—Virgil.</p>
<p class="verse">Midway upon the journey of our life<sup>2</sup> <br> I found myself within a forest dark,<sup>3</sup> <br> For the straightforward pathway had been lost. <br> Ah me! how hard a thing it is to say <br> What was this forest savage, rough, and stern, <br> Which in the very thought renews the fear. <br> So bitter is it, death is little more; <br> But of the good to treat, which there I found, <br> Speak will I of the other things I saw there. <br> I cannot well repeat how there I entered, <br> So full was I of slumber at the moment <br> In which I had abandoned the true way. <br> But after I had reached a mountain’s foot,<sup>4</sup> <br> At that point where the valley terminated,<sup>5</sup> <br> Which had with consternation pierced my heart, <br> Upward I looked, and I beheld its shoulders, <br> Vested already with that planet’s rays<sup>6</sup> <br> Which leadeth others right by every road. <br> Then was the fear a little quieted <br> That in my heart’s lake had endured throughout<sup>7</sup> <br> The night, which I had passed so piteously. <br> And even as he, who, with distressful breath, <br> Forth issued from the sea upon the shore, <br> Turns to the water perilous and gazes; <br> So did my soul, that still was fleeing onward, <br> Turn itself back to re-behold the pass <br> Which never yet a living person left.<sup>8</sup> <br> After my weary body I had rested, <br> The way resumed I on the desert slope, <br> So that the firm foot ever was the lower.<sup>9</sup> <br> And lo! almost where the ascent began,<sup>10</sup> <br> A panther light and swift exceedingly,<sup>11</sup> <br> Which with a spotted skin was covered o’er! <br> And never moved she from before my face, <br> Nay, rather did impede so much my way, <br> That many times I to return had turned.<sup>12</sup> <br> The time was the beginning of the morning, <br> And up the sun was mounting with those stars<sup>13</sup> <br> That with him were, what time the Love Divine <br> At first in motion set those beauteous things; <br> So were to me occasion of good hope, <br> The variegated skin of that wild beast, <br> The hour of time, and the delicious season; <br> But not so much, that did not give me fear <br> A lion’s aspect which appeared to me.<sup>14</sup> <br> He seemed as if against me he were coming <br> With head uplifted, and with ravenous hunger, <br> So that it seemed the air was afraid of him;<sup>15</sup> <br> And a she-wolf, that with all hungerings<sup>16</sup> <br> Seemed to be laden in her meagreness, <br> And many folk has caused to live forlorn! <br> She brought upon me so much heaviness, <br> With the affright that from her aspect came, <br> That I the hope relinquished of the height. <br> And as he is who willingly acquires, <br> And the time comes that causes him to lose, <br> Who weeps in all his thoughts and is despondent, <br> E’en such made me that beast withouten peace, <br> Which, coming on against me by degrees <br> Thrust me back thither where the sun is silent.<sup>17</sup> <br> While I was rushing downward to the lowland, <br> Before mine eyes did one present himself, <br> Who seemed from long-continued silence hoarse.<sup>18</sup> <br> When I beheld him in the desert vast, <br> “Have pity on me,” unto him I cried, <br> “Whiche’er thou art, or shade or real man!” <br> He answered me: “Not man; man once I was, <br> And both my parents were of Lombardy, <br> And Mantuans by country both of them. <br> <i>Sub Julio</i> was I born, though it was late,<sup>19</sup> <br> And lived at Rome under the good Augustus, <br> During the time of false and lying gods. <br> A Poet was I, and I sang that just <br> Son of Anchises, who came forth from Troy, <br> After that Ilion the superb was burned. <br> But thou, why goest thou back to such annoyance? <br> Why climb’st thou not the Mount Delectable, <br> Which is the source and cause of every joy?” <br> “Now, art thou that Virgilius and that fountain<sup>20</sup> <br> Which spreads abroad so wide a river of speech?” <br> I made response to him with bashful forehead. <br> “O, of the other poets honor and light, <br> Avail me the long study and great love <br> That have impelled me to explore thy volume! <br> Thou art my master, and my author thou, <br> Thou art alone the one from whom I took <br> The beautiful style that has done honor to me.<sup>21</sup> <br> Behold the beast, for which I have turned back; <br> Do thou protect me from her, famous Sage, <br> For she doth make my veins and pulses tremble.” <br> “Thee it behoves to take another road,” <br> Responded he, when he beheld me weeping, <br> “If from this savage place thou wouldst escape; <br> Because this beast, at which thou criest out, <br> Suffers not any one to pass her way, <br> But so doth harass him, that she destroys him; <br> And has a nature so malign and ruthless, <br> That never doth she glut her greedy will, <br> And after food is hungrier than before. <br> Many the animals with whom she weds, <br> And more they shall be still, until the Greyhound<sup>22</sup> <br> Comes, who shall make her perish in her pain. <br> He shall not feed on either earth or pelf, <br> But upon wisdom, and on love and virtue; <br> ’Twixt Feltro and Feltro shall his nation be; <br> Of that low Italy shall he be the saviour,<sup>23</sup> <br> On whose account the maid Camilla died, <br> Euryalus, Turnus, Nisus, of their wounds; <br> Through every city shall he hunt her down, <br> Until he shall have driven her back to Hell, <br> There from whence envy first did let her loose. <br> Therefore I think and judge it for thy best <br> Thou follow me, and I will be thy guide, <br> And lead thee hence through the eternal place, <br> Where thou shalt hear the desperate lamentations, <br> Shalt see the ancient spirits disconsolate,<sup>24</sup> <br> Who cry out each one for the second death; <br> And thou shalt see those who contented are <br> Within the fire, because they hope to come, <br> Whene’er it may be, to the blessed people; <br> To whom, then, if thou wishest to ascend, <br> A soul shall be for that than I more worthy;<sup>25</sup> <br> With her at my departure I will leave thee; <br> Because that Emperor, who reigns above, <br> In that I was rebellious to his law, <br> Wills that through me none come into his city. <br> He governs everywhere, and there he reigns; <br> There is his city and his lofty throne; <br> O happy he whom thereto he elects!” <br> And I to him: “Poet, I thee entreat, <br> By that same God whom thou didst never know, <br> So that I may escape this woe and worse, <br> Thou wouldst conduct me there where thou hast said, <br> That I may see the portal of Saint Peter, <br> And those thou makest so disconsolate.” <br> Then he moved on, and I behind him followed.</p>
