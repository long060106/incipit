<p class="lede">The Second Circle⁠—The Envious⁠—Sapia of Siena.</p>
<p class="verse">We were upon the summit of the stairs,<sup>759</sup> <br> Where for the second time is cut away <br> The mountain, which ascending shriveth all. <br> There in like manner doth a cornice bind <br> The hill all round about, as does the first, <br> Save that its arc more suddenly is curved. <br> Shade is there none, nor sculpture that appears; <br> So seems the bank, and so the road seems smooth, <br> With but the livid color of the stone.<sup>760</sup> <br> “If to inquire we wait for people here,” <br> The Poet said, “I fear that peradventure <br> Too much delay will our election have.” <br> Then steadfast on the sun his eyes he fixed, <br> Made his right side the centre of his motion,<sup>761</sup> <br> And turned the left part of himself about. <br> “O thou sweet light! with trust in whom I enter<sup>762</sup> <br> Upon this novel journey, do thou lead us,” <br> Said he, “as one within here should be led. <br> Thou warmest the world, thou shinest over it; <br> If other reason prompt not otherwise, <br> Thy rays should evermore our leaders be!” <br> As much as here is counted for a mile, <br> So much already there had we advanced <br> In little time, by dint of ready will; <br> And tow’rds us there were heard to fly, albeit <br> They were not visible, spirits uttering <br> Unto Love’s table courteous invitations, <br> The first voice that passed onward in its flight, <br> “<i>Vinum non habent</i>,” said in accents loud,<sup>763</sup> <br> And went reiterating it behind us. <br> And ere it wholly grew inaudible <br> Because of distance, passed another, crying, <br> “I am Orestes!” and it also stayed not.<sup>764</sup> <br> “O,” said I, “Father, these, what voices are they?” <br> And even as I asked, behold the third, <br> Saying: “Love those from whom ye have had evil!”<sup>765</sup> <br> And the good Master said: “This circle scourges <br> The sin of envy, and on that account <br> Are drawn from love the lashes of the scourge.<sup>766</sup> <br> The bridle of another sound shall be; <br> I think that thou wilt hear it, as I judge, <br> Before thou comest to the Pass of Pardon.<sup>767</sup> <br> But fix thine eyes athwart the air right steadfast, <br> And people thou wilt see before us sitting, <br> And each one close against the cliff is seated.” <br> Then wider than at first mine eyes I opened; <br> I looked before me, and saw shades with mantles <br> Not from the color of the stone diverse. <br> And when we were a little farther onward, <br> I heard a cry of, “Mary, pray for us!” <br> A cry of, “Michael, Peter, and all Saints!”<sup>768</sup> <br> I do not think there walketh still on earth <br> A man so hard, that he would not be pierced <br> With pity at what afterward I saw. <br> For when I had approached so near to them <br> That manifest to me their acts became, <br> Drained was I at the eyes by heavy grief. <br> Covered with sackcloth vile they seemed to me, <br> And one sustained the other with his shoulder, <br> And all of them were by the bank sustained. <br> Thus do the blind, in want of livelihood, <br> Stand at the doors of churches asking alms, <br> And one upon another leans his head, <br> So that in others pity soon may rise, <br> Not only at the accent of their words, <br> But at their aspect, which no less implores. <br> And as unto the blind the sun comes not, <br> So to the shades, of whom just now I spake, <br> Heaven’s light will not be bounteous of itself; <br> For all their lids an iron wire transpierces, <br> And sews them up, as to a sparhawk wild <br> Is done, because it will not quiet stay. <br> To me it seemed, in passing, to do outrage, <br> Seeing the others without being seen; <br> Wherefore I turned me to my counsel sage. <br> Well knew he what the mute one wished to say, <br> And therefore waited not for my demand, <br> But said: “Speak, and be brief, and to the point.” <br> I had Virgilius upon that side <br> Of the embankment from which one may fall, <br> Since by no border ’tis engarlanded; <br> Upon the other side of me I had <br> The shades devout, who through the horrible seam <br> Pressed out the tears so that they bathed their cheeks. <br> To them I turned me, and, “O people, certain,” <br> Began I, “of beholding the high light, <br> Which your desire has solely in its care, <br> So may grace speedily dissolve the scum <br> Upon your consciences, that limpidly <br> Through them descend the river of the mind, <br> Tell me, for dear ’twill be to me and gracious, <br> If any soul among you here is Latian,<sup>769</sup> <br> And ’twill perchance be good for him I learn it.” <br> “O brother mine, each one is citizen <br> Of one true city; but thy meaning is, <br> Who may have lived in Italy a pilgrim.” <br> By way of answer this I seemed to hear <br> A little farther on than where I stood, <br> Whereat I made myself still nearer heard. <br> Among the rest I saw a shade that waited <br> In aspect, and should anyone ask how, <br> Its chin it lifted upward like a blind man. <br> “Spirit,” I said, “who stoopest to ascend, <br> If thou art he who did reply to me, <br> Make thyself known to me by place or name.” <br> “Sienese was I,” it replied, “and with <br> The others here recleanse my guilty life, <br> Weeping to Him to lend himself to us. <br> Sapient I was not, although I Sapia<sup>770</sup> <br> Was called, and I was at another’s harm<sup>771</sup> <br> More happy far than at my own good fortune. <br> And that thou mayst not think that I deceive thee, <br> Hear if I was as foolish as I tell thee. <br> The arc already of my years descending,<sup>772</sup> <br> My fellow-citizens near unto Colle <br> Were joined in battle with their adversaries, <br> And I was praying God for what he willed. <br> Routed were they, and turned into the bitter <br> Passes of flight; and I, the chase beholding, <br> A joy received unequalled by all others; <br> So that I lifted upward my bold face <br> Crying to God, ‘Henceforth I fear thee not,’<sup>773</sup> <br> As did the blackbird at the little sunshine. <br> Peace I desired with God at the extreme <br> Of my existence, and as yet would not <br> My debt have been by penitence discharged, <br> Had it not been that in remembrance held me <br> Pier Pettignano in his holy prayers,<sup>774</sup> <br> Who out of charity was grieved for me. <br> But who art thou, that into our conditions <br> Questioning goest, and hast thine eyes unbound <br> As I believe, and breathing dost discourse?” <br> “Mine eyes,” I said, “will yet be here ta’en from me, <br> But for short space; for small is the offence <br> Committed by their being turned with envy. <br> Far greater is the fear, wherein suspended <br> My soul is, of the torment underneath, <br> For even now the load down there weighs on me.”<sup>775</sup> <br> And she to me: “Who led thee, then, among us <br> Up here, if to return below thou thinkest?” <br> And I: “He who is with me, and speaks not; <br> And living am I; therefore ask of me, <br> Spirit elect, if thou wouldst have me move <br> O’er yonder yet my mortal feet for thee.”<sup>776</sup> <br> “O, this is such a novel thing to hear,” <br> She answered, “that great sign it is God loves thee; <br> Therefore with prayer of thine sometimes assist me. <br> And I implore, by what thou most desirest, <br> If e’er thou treadest the soil of Tuscany, <br> Well with my kindred reinstate my fame. <br> Them wilt thou see among that people vain<sup>777</sup> <br> Who hope in Talamone, and will lose there<sup>778</sup> <br> More hope than in discovering the Diana;<sup>779</sup> <br> But there still more the admirals will lose.”<sup>780</sup></p>
