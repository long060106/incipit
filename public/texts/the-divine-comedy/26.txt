<p class="lede">Agnello Brunelleschi, Buoso degli Abati, Puccio Sciancato, Cianfa de’ Donati, and Guercio Cavalcanti.</p>
<p class="verse">At the conclusion of his words, the thief<sup>356</sup> <br> Lifted his hands aloft with both the figs,<sup>357</sup> <br> Crying: “Take that, God, for at thee I aim them.” <br> From that time forth the serpents were my friends; <br> For one entwined itself about his neck <br> As if it said: “I will not thou speak more”; <br> And round his arms another, and rebound him, <br> Clinching itself together so in front, <br> That with them he could not a motion make. <br> Pistoia, ah, Pistoia! why resolve not<sup>358</sup> <br> To burn thyself to ashes and so perish, <br> Since in ill-doing thou thy seed excellest? <br> Through all the sombre circles of this Hell, <br> Spirit I saw not against God so proud, <br> Not he who fell at Thebes down from the walls!<sup>359</sup> <br> He fled away, and spake no further word; <br> And I beheld a Centaur full of rage <br> Come crying out: “Where is, where is the scoffer?” <br> I do not think Maremma has so many<sup>360</sup> <br> Serpents as he had all along his back, <br> As far as where our countenance begins. <br> Upon the shoulders, just behind the nape, <br> With wings wide open was a dragon lying, <br> And he sets fire to all that he encounters. <br> My Master said: “That one is Cacus, who<sup>361</sup> <br> Beneath the rock upon Mount Aventine <br> Created oftentimes a lake of blood. <br> He goes not on the same road with his brothers,<sup>362</sup> <br> By reason of the fraudulent theft he made <br> Of the great herd, which he had near to him; <br> Whereat his tortuous actions ceased beneath <br> The mace of Hercules, who peradventure <br> Gave him a hundred, and he felt not ten.” <br> While he was speaking thus, he had passed by, <br> And spirits three had underneath us come,<sup>363</sup> <br> Of which nor I aware was, nor my Leader, <br> Until what time they shouted: “Who are you?” <br> On which account our story made a halt,<sup>364</sup> <br> And then we were intent on them alone. <br> I did not know them; but it came to pass, <br> As it is wont to happen by some chance, <br> That one to name the other was compelled, <br> Exclaiming: “Where can Cianfa have remained?”<sup>365</sup> <br> Whence I, so that the Leader might attend, <br> Upward from chin to nose my finger laid. <br> If thou art, Reader, slow now to believe <br> What I shall say, it will no marvel be, <br> For I who saw it hardly can admit it. <br> As I was holding raised on them my brows, <br> Behold! a serpent with six feet darts forth <br> In front of one, and fastens wholly on him. <br> With middle feet it bound him round the paunch, <br> And with the forward ones his arms it seized; <br> Then thrust its teeth through one cheek and the other; <br> The hindermost it stretched upon his thighs, <br> And put its tail through in between the two, <br> And up behind along the reins outspread it. <br> Ivy was never fastened by its barbs <br> Unto a tree so, as this horrible reptile <br> Upon the other’s limbs entwined its own. <br> Then they stuck close, as if of heated wax <br> They had been made, and intermixed their color; <br> Nor one nor other seemed now what he was; <br> E’en as proceedeth on before the flame <br> Upward along the paper a brown color,<sup>366</sup> <br> Which is not black as yet, and the white dies. <br> The other two looked on, and each of them <br> Cried out: “O me, Agnello, how thou changest! <br> Behold, thou now art neither two nor one.” <br> Already the two heads had one become, <br> When there appeared to us two figures mingled <br> Into one face, wherein the two were lost. <br> Of the four lists were fashioned the two arms,<sup>367</sup> <br> The thighs and legs, the belly and the chest <br> Members became that never yet were seen. <br> Every original aspect there was cancelled;<sup>368</sup> <br> Two and yet none did the perverted image <br> Appear, and such departed with slow pace. <br> Even as a lizard, under the great scourge <br> Of days canicular, exchanging hedge, <br> Lightning appeareth if the road it cross; <br> Thus did appear, coming towards the bellies <br> Of the two others, a small fiery serpent,<sup>369</sup> <br> Livid and black as is a peppercorn. <br> And in that part whereat is first received <br> Our aliment, it one of them transfixed; <br> Then downward fell in front of him extended. <br> The one transfixed looked at it, but said naught; <br> Nay, rather with feet motionless he yawned, <br> Just as if sleep or fever had assailed him. <br> He at the serpent gazed, and it at him; <br> One through the wound, the other through the mouth <br> Smoked violently, and the smoke commingled. <br> Henceforth be silent Lucan, where he mentions <br> Wretched Sabellus and Nassidius,<sup>370</sup> <br> And wait to hear what now shall be shot forth. <br> Be silent Ovid, of Cadmus and Arethusa;<sup>371</sup> <br> For if him to a snake, her to fountain, <br> Converts he fabling, that I grudge him not; <br> Because two natures never front to front <br> Has he transmuted, so that both the forms <br> To interchange their matter ready were. <br> Together they responded in such wise, <br> That to a fork the serpent cleft his tail, <br> And eke the wounded drew his feet together. <br> The legs together with the thighs themselves <br> Adhered so, that in little time the juncture <br> No sign whatever made that was apparent. <br> He with the cloven tail assumed the figure <br> The other one was losing, and his skin <br> Became elastic, and the other’s hard. <br> I saw the arms draw inward at the armpits, <br> And both feet of the reptile, that were short, <br> Lengthen as much as those contracted were. <br> Thereafter the hind feet, together twisted, <br> Became the member that a man conceals, <br> And of his own the wretch had two created. <br> While both of them the exhalation veils <br> With a new color, and engenders hair <br> On one of them and depilates the other, <br> The one uprose and down the other fell, <br> Though turning not away their impious lamps, <br> Underneath which each one his muzzle changed. <br> He who was standing drew it tow’rds the temples, <br> And from excess of matter, which came thither, <br> Issued the ears from out the hollow cheeks; <br> What did not backward run and was retained <br> Of that excess made to the face a nose, <br> And the lips thickened far as was befitting. <br> He who lay prostrate thrusts his muzzle forward, <br> And backward draws the ears into his head, <br> In the same manner as the snail its horns; <br> And so the tongue, which was entire and apt <br> For speech before, is cleft, and the bi-forked <br> In the other closes up, and the smoke ceases. <br> The soul, which to a reptile had been changed, <br> Along the valley hissing takes to flight, <br> And after him the other speaking sputters. <br> Then did he turn upon him his new shoulders, <br> And said to the other: “I’ll have Buoso run, <br> Crawling as I have done, along this road.” <br> In this way I beheld the seventh ballast <br> Shift and reshift, and here be my excuse <br> The novelty, if aught my pen transgress.<sup>372</sup> <br> And notwithstanding that mine eyes might be <br> Somewhat bewildered, and my mind dismayed, <br> They could not flee away so secretly <br> But that I plainly saw Puccio Sciancato; <br> And he it was who sole of three companions, <br> Which came in the beginning, was not changed; <br> The other was he whom thou, Gaville, weepest.<sup>373</sup></p>
