<p class="lede">The Third Circle⁠—Cerberus⁠—The gluttonous⁠—The eternal rain⁠—Ciacco.</p>
<p class="verse">At the return of consciousness, that closed <br> Before the pity of those two relations,<sup>92</sup> <br> Which utterly with sadness had confused me, <br> New torments I behold, and new tormented <br> Around me, whichsoever way I move, <br> And whichsoever way I turn, and gaze. <br> In the third circle am I of the rain<sup>93</sup> <br> Eternal, maledict, and cold, and heavy; <br> Its law and quality are never new. <br> Huge hail, and water sombre-hued, and snow, <br> Athwart the tenebrous air pour down amain; <br> Noisome the earth is, that receiveth this. <br> Cerberus, monster cruel and uncouth, <br> With his three gullets like a dog is barking <br> Over the people that are there submerged. <br> Red eyes he has, and unctuous beard and black, <br> And belly large, and armed with claws his hands; <br> He rends the spirits, flays, and quarters them. <br> Howl the rain maketh them like unto dogs; <br> One side they make a shelter for the other; <br> Oft turn themselves the wretched reprobates. <br> When Cerberus perceived us, the great worm! <br> His mouths he opened, and displayed his tusks; <br> Not a limb had he that was motionless. <br> And my Conductor, with his spans extended, <br> Took of the earth, and with his fists well filled, <br> He threw it into those rapacious gullets. <br> Such as that dog is, who by barking craves, <br> And quiet grows soon as his food he gnaws, <br> For to devour it he but thinks and struggles, <br> The like became those muzzles filth-begrimed <br> Of Cerberus the demon, who so thunders <br> Over the souls that they would fain be deaf. <br> We passed across the shadows, which subdues <br> The heavy rainstorm, and we placed our feet <br> Upon their vanity that person seems. <br> They all were lying prone upon the earth, <br> Excepting one, who sat upright as soon <br> As he beheld us passing on before him. <br> “O thou that art conducted through this Hell,” <br> He said to me, “recall me, if thou canst; <br> Thyself wast made before I was unmade.” <br> And I to him: “The anguish which thou hast <br> Perhaps doth draw thee out of my remembrance, <br> So that it seems not I have ever seen thee. <br> But tell me who thou art, that in so doleful <br> A place art put, and in such punishment, <br> If some are greater, none is so displeasing.” <br> And he to me: “Thy city, which is full <br> Of envy so that now the sack runs over, <br> Held me within it in the life serene. <br> You citizens were wont to call me Ciacco;<sup>94</sup> <br> For the pernicious sin of gluttony <br> I, as thou seest, am battered by this rain. <br> And I, sad soul, am not the only one, <br> For all these suffer the like penalty <br> For the like sin”; and word no more spake he. <br> I answered him: “Ciacco, thy wretchedness <br> Weighs on me so that it to weep invites me; <br> But tell me, if thou knowest, to what shall come <br> The citizens of the divided city; <br> If any there be just; and the occasion <br> Tell me why so much discord has assailed it.” <br> And he to me: “They, after long contention, <br> Will come to bloodshed; and the rustic party<sup>95</sup> <br> Will drive the other out with much offence. <br> Then afterwards behoves it this one fall <br> Within three suns, and rise again the other <br> By force of him who now is on the coast.<sup>96</sup> <br> High will it hold its forehead a long while, <br> Keeping the other under heavy burdens, <br> Howe’er it weeps thereat and is indignant. <br> The just are two, and are not understood there;<sup>97</sup> <br> Envy and Arrogance and Avarice <br> Are the three sparks that have all hearts enkindled.” <br> Here ended he his tearful utterance; <br> And I to him: “I wish thee still to teach me, <br> And make a gift to me of further speech. <br> Farinata and Tegghiaio, once so worthy, <br> Jacopo Rusticucci, Arrigo, and Mosca,<sup>98</sup> <br> And others who on good deeds set their thoughts, <br> Say where they are, and cause that I may know them; <br> For great desire constraineth me to learn <br> If Heaven doth sweeten them, or Hell envenom.” <br> And he: “They are among the blacker souls; <br> A different sin downweighs them to the bottom; <br> If thou so far descendest, thou canst see them. <br> But when thou art again in the sweet world, <br> I pray thee to the mind of others bring me; <br> No more I tell thee and no more I answer.” <br> Then his straightforward eyes he turned askance, <br> Eyed me a little, and then bowed his head; <br> He fell therewith prone like the other blind. <br> And the Guide said to me: “He wakes no more <br> This side the sound of the angelic trumpet; <br> When shall approach the hostile Potentate, <br> Each one shall find again his dismal tomb, <br> Shall reassume his flesh and his own figure, <br> Shall hear what through eternity reechoes.” <br> So we passed onward o’er the filthy mixture <br> Of shadows and of rain with footsteps slow, <br> Touching a little on the future life. <br> Wherefore I said: “Master, these torments here, <br> Will they increase after the mighty sentence, <br> Or lesser be, or will they be as burning?” <br> And he to me: “Return unto thy science,<sup>99</sup> <br> Which wills, that as the thing more perfect is, <br> The more it feels of pleasure and of pain. <br> Albeit that this people maledict <br> To true perfection never can attain, <br> Hereafter more than now they look to be.” <br> Round in a circle by that road we went, <br> Speaking much more, which I do not repeat; <br> We came unto the point where the descent is; <br> There we found Plutus the great enemy.<sup>100</sup></p>
