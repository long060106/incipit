<p class="lede">Guido da Montefeltro.</p>
<p class="verse">Already was the flame erect and quiet,<sup>393</sup> <br> To speak no more, and now departed from us <br> With the permission of the gentle Poet; <br> When yet another, which behind it came, <br> Caused us to turn our eyes upon its top <br> By a confused sound that issued from it. <br> As the Sicilian bull (that bellowed first<sup>394</sup> <br> With the lament of him, and that was right, <br> Who with his file had modulated it) <br> Bellowed so with the voice of the afflicted, <br> That, notwithstanding it was made of brass, <br> Still it appeared with agony transfixed; <br> Thus, by not having any way or issue <br> At first from out the fire, to its own language <br> Converted were the melancholy words. <br> But afterwards, when they had gathered way <br> Up through the point, giving it that vibration <br> The tongue had given them in their passage out, <br> We heard it said: “O thou, at whom I aim <br> My voice, and who but now wast speaking Lombard, <br> Saying, ‘Now go thy way, no more I urge thee,’<sup>395</sup> <br> Because I come perchance a little late, <br> To stay and speak with me let it not irk thee; <br> Thou seest it irks not me, and I am burning. <br> If thou but lately into this blind world <br> Hast fallen down from that sweet Latian land, <br> Wherefrom I bring the whole of my transgression, <br> Say, have the Romagnuoli peace or war,<sup>396</sup> <br> For I was from the mountains there between<sup>397</sup> <br> Urbino and the yoke whence Tiber bursts.” <br> I still was downward bent and listening, <br> When my Conductor touched me on the side, <br> Saying: “Speak thou: this one a Latian is.” <br> And I, who had beforehand my reply <br> In readiness, forthwith began to speak: <br> “O soul, that down below there art concealed, <br> Romagna thine is not and never has been <br> Without war in the bosom of its tyrants; <br> But open war I none have left there now. <br> Ravenna stands as it long years has stood;<sup>398</sup> <br> The Eagle of Polenta there is brooding,<sup>399</sup> <br> So that she covers Cervia with her vans. <br> The city which once made the long resistance,<sup>400</sup> <br> And of the French a sanguinary heap, <br> Beneath the Green Paws finds itself again;<sup>401</sup> <br> Verrucchio’s ancient Mastiff and the new,<sup>402</sup> <br> Who made such bad disposal of Montagna, <br> Where they are wont make wimbles of their teeth. <br> The cities of Lamone and Santerno<sup>403</sup> <br> Governs the Lioncel of the white lair, <br> Who changes sides ’twixt summer-time and winter; <br> And that of which the Savio bathes the flank,<sup>404</sup> <br> Even as it lies between the plain and mountain, <br> Lives between tyranny and a free state. <br> Now I entreat thee tell us who thou art; <br> Be not more stubborn than the rest have been, <br> So may thy name hold front there in the world.” <br> After the fire a little more had roared <br> In its own fashion, the sharp point it moved <br> This way and that, and then gave forth such breath: <br> “If I believed that my reply were made <br> To one who to the world would e’er return, <br> This flame without more flickering would stand still; <br> But inasmuch as never from this depth <br> Did any one return, if I hear true, <br> Without the fear of infamy I answer, <br> I was a man of arms, then Cordelier,<sup>405</sup> <br> Believing thus begirt to make amends; <br> And truly my belief had been fulfilled <br> But for the High Priest, whom may ill betide,<sup>406</sup> <br> Who put me back into my former sins; <br> And how and wherefore I will have thee hear. <br> While I was still the form of bone and pulp <br> My mother gave to me, the deeds I did <br> Were not those of a lion, but a fox. <br> The machinations and the covert ways <br> I knew them all, and practised so their craft, <br> That to the ends of earth the sound went forth. <br> When now unto that portion of mine age <br> I saw myself arrived, when each one ought <br> To lower the sails, and coil away the ropes,<sup>407</sup> <br> That which before had pleased me then displeased me; <br> And penitent and confessing I surrendered, <br> Ah woe is me! and it would have bestead me; <br> The Leader of the modern Pharisees <br> Having a war near unto Lateran,<sup>408</sup> <br> And not with Saracens nor with the Jews, <br> For each one of his enemies was Christian, <br> And none of them had been to conquer Acre, <br> Nor merchandising in the Sultan’s land, <br> Nor the high office, nor the sacred orders, <br> In him regarded, nor in me that cord <br> Which used to make those girt with it more meagre; <br> But even as Constantine sought out Sylvester<sup>409</sup> <br> To cure his leprosy, within Soracte, <br> So this one sought me out as an adept<sup>410</sup> <br> To cure him of the fever of his pride. <br> Counsel he asked of me, and I was silent, <br> Because his words appeared inebriate. <br> And then he said: ‘Be not thy heart afraid; <br> Henceforth I thee absolve; and thou instruct me <br> How to raze Palestrina to the ground.<sup>411</sup> <br> Heaven have I power to lock and to unlock, <br> As thou dost know; therefore the keys are two, <br> The which my predecessor held not dear.’<sup>412</sup> <br> Then urged me on his weighty arguments <br> There, where my silence was the worst advice; <br> And said I: ‘Father, since thou washest me <br> Of that sin into which I now must fall, <br> The promise long with the fulfilment short <br> Will make thee triumph in thy lofty seat.’ <br> Francis came afterward, when I was dead, <br> For me; but one of the black Cherubim <br> Said to him: ‘Take him not; do me no wrong; <br> He must come down among my servitors, <br> Because he gave the fraudulent advice <br> From which time forth I have been at his hair; <br> For who repents not cannot be absolved,<sup>413</sup> <br> Nor can one both repent and will at once, <br> Because of the contradiction which consents not.’ <br> O miserable me! how I did shudder <br> When he seized on me, saying: ‘Peradventure <br> Thou didst not think that I was a logician!’ <br> He bore me unto Minos, who entwined <br> Eight times his tail about his stubborn back, <br> And after he had bitten it in great rage, <br> Said: ‘Of the thievish fire a culprit this’; <br> Wherefore, here where thou seest, am I lost, <br> And vested thus in going I bemoan me.” <br> When it had thus completed its recital, <br> The flame departed uttering lamentations, <br> Writhing and flapping its sharp-pointed horn. <br> Onward we passed, both I and my Conductor, <br> Up o’er the crag above another arch, <br> Which the moat covers, where is paid the fee <br> By those who, sowing discord, win their burden.</p>
