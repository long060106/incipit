<p class="lede">Cacciaguida⁠—Florence in the olden time.</p>
<p class="verse">A will benign, in which reveals itself<sup>1573</sup> <br> Ever the love that righteously inspires, <br> As in the iniquitous, cupidity, <br> Silence imposed upon that dulcet lyre, <br> And quieted the consecrated chords, <br> That Heaven’s right hand doth tighten and relax. <br> How unto just entreaties shall be deaf <br> Those substances, which, to give me desire <br> Of praying them, with one accord grew silent? <br> ’Tis well that without end he should lament, <br> Who for the love of thing that doth not last <br> Eternally despoils him of that love! <br> As through the pure and tranquil evening air <br> There shoots from time to time a sudden fire, <br> Moving the eyes that steadfast were before, <br> And seems to be a star that changeth place, <br> Except that in the part where it is kindled <br> Nothing is missed, and this endureth little; <br> So from the horn that to the right extends <br> Unto that cross’s foot there ran a star <br> Out of the constellation shining there; <br> Nor was the gem dissevered from its ribbon,<sup>1574</sup> <br> But down the radiant fillet ran along, <br> So that fire seemed it behind alabaster.<sup>1575</sup> <br> Thus piteous did Anchises’ shade reach forward,<sup>1576</sup> <br> If any faith our greatest Muse deserve, <br> When in Elysium he his son perceived. <br> “<i>O sanguis meus, O superinfusa</i><sup>1577</sup> <br>  <i>Gratia Dei, sicut tibi, cui</i>  <br> <i>Bis unquam Coeli janua reclusa?</i>” <br> Thus that effulgence; whence I gave it heed; <br> Then round unto my Lady turned my sight, <br> And on this side and that was stupefied; <br> For in her eyes was burning such a smile <br> That with mine own methought I touched the bottom <br> Both of my grace and of my Paradise! <br> Then, pleasant to the hearing and the sight, <br> The spirit joined to its beginning things <br> I understood not, so profound it spake; <br> Nor did it hide itself from me by choice, <br> But by necessity; for its conception <br> Above the mark of mortals set itself. <br> And when the bow of burning sympathy <br> Was so far slackened, that its speech descended <br> Towards the mark of our intelligence, <br> The first thing that was understood by me <br> Was “Benedight be Thou, O Trine and One, <br> Who hast unto my seed so courteous been!” <br> And it continued: “Hunger long and grateful,<sup>1578</sup> <br> Drawn from the reading of the mighty volume<sup>1579</sup> <br> Wherein is never changed the white nor dark, <br> Thou hast appeased, my son, within this light <br> In which I speak to thee, by grace of her <br> Who to this lofty flight with plumage clothed thee. <br> Thou thinkest that to me thy thought doth pass <br> From Him who is the first, as from the unit,<sup>1580</sup> <br> If that be known, ray out the five and six; <br> And therefore who I am thou askest not, <br> And why I seem more joyous unto thee <br> Than any other of this gladsome crowd. <br> Thou think’st the truth; because the small and great<sup>1581</sup> <br> Of this existence look into the mirror <br> Wherein, before thou think’st, thy thought thou showest. <br> But that the sacred love, in which I watch <br> With sight perpetual, and which makes me thirst <br> With sweet desire, may better be fulfilled, <br> Now let thy voice secure and frank and glad <br> Proclaim the wishes, the desire proclaim, <br> To which my answer is decreed already.” <br> To Beatrice I turned me, and she heard <br> Before I spake, and smiled to me a sign, <br> That made the wings of my desire increase; <br> Then in this wise began I: “Love and knowledge, <br> When on you dawned the first Equality,<sup>1582</sup> <br> Of the same weight for each of you became; <br> For in the Sun, which lighted you and burned <br> With heat and radiance, they so equal are, <br> That all similitudes are insufficient. <br> But among mortals will and argument,<sup>1583</sup> <br> For reason that to you is manifest, <br> Diversely feathered in their pinions are. <br> Whence I, who mortal am, feel in myself <br> This inequality; so give not thanks, <br> Save in my heart, for this paternal welcome. <br> Truly do I entreat thee, living topaz!<sup>1584</sup> <br> Set in this precious jewel as a gem, <br> That thou wilt satisfy me with thy name.” <br> “O leaf of mine, in whom I pleasure took <br> E’en while awaiting, I was thine own root!”<sup>1585</sup> <br> Such a beginning he in answer made me. <br> Then said to me: “That one from whom is named<sup>1586</sup> <br> Thy race, and who a hundred years and more <br> Has circled round the mount on the first cornice, <br> A son of mine and thy great-grandsire was; <br> Well it behoves thee that the long fatigue <br> Thou shouldst for him make shorter with thy works. <br> Florence, within the ancient boundary<sup>1587</sup> <br> From which she taketh still her tierce and nones,<sup>1588</sup> <br> Abode in quiet, temperate and chaste.<sup>1589</sup> <br> No golden chain she had, nor coronal,<sup>1590</sup> <br> Nor ladies shod with sandal shoon, nor girdle <br> That caught the eye more than the person did.<sup>1591</sup> <br> Not yet the daughter at her birth struck fear <br> Into the father, for the time and dower <br> Did not o’errun this side or that the measure. <br> No houses had she void of families, <br> Not yet had thither come Sardanapalus <br> To show what in a chamber can be done;<sup>1592</sup> <br> Not yet surpassed had Montemalo been<sup>1593</sup> <br> By your Uccellatojo, which surpassed<sup>1594</sup> <br> Shall in its downfall be as in its rise. <br> Bellincion Berti saw I go begirt<sup>1595</sup> <br> With leather and with bone, and from the mirror <br> His dame depart without a painted face; <br> And him of Nerli saw, and him of Vecchio,<sup>1596</sup> <br> Contented with their simple suits of buff <br> And with the spindle and the flax their dames. <br> O fortunate women! and each one was certain <br> Of her own burial-place, and none as yet <br> For sake of France was in her bed deserted.<sup>1597</sup> <br> One o’er the cradle kept her studious watch, <br> And in her lullaby the language used <br> That first delights the fathers and the mothers; <br> Another, drawing tresses from her distaff, <br> Told o’er among her family the tales <br> Of Trojans and of Fesole and Rome. <br> As great a marvel then would have been held <br> A Lapo Salterello, a Cianghella,<sup>1598</sup> <br> As Cincinnatus or Cornelia now.<sup>1599</sup> <br> To such a quiet, such a beautiful <br> Life of the citizen, to such a safe <br> Community, and to so sweet an inn, <br> Did Mary give me, with loud cries invoked,<sup>1600</sup> <br> And in your ancient Baptistery at once<sup>1601</sup> <br> Christian and Cacciaguida I became.<sup>1602</sup> <br> Moronto was my brother, and Eliseo; <br> From Val di Pado came to me my wife,<sup>1603</sup> <br> And from that place thy surname was derived. <br> I followed afterward the Emperor Conrad,<sup>1604</sup> <br> And he begirt me of his chivalry,<sup>1605</sup> <br> So much I pleased him with my noble deeds. <br> I followed in his train against that law’s <br> Iniquity, whose people doth usurp<sup>1606</sup> <br> Your just possession, through your Pastor’s fault. <br> There by that execrable race was I <br> Released from bonds of the fallacious world, <br> The love of which defileth many souls, <br> And came from martyrdom unto this peace.”</p>
