<p class="lede">Questionings of the soul and of broken vows.</p>
<p class="verse">Between two viands, equally removed<sup>1242</sup> <br> And tempting, a free man would die of hunger<sup>1243</sup> <br> Ere either he could bring unto his teeth. <br> So would a lamb between the ravenings <br> Of two fierce wolves stand fearing both alike; <br> And so would stand a dog between two does.<sup>1244</sup> <br> Hence, if I held my peace, myself I blame not, <br> Impelled in equal measure by my doubts, <br> Since it must be so, nor do I commend.<sup>1245</sup> <br> I held my peace; but my desire was painted <br> Upon my face, and questioning with that <br> More fervent far than by articulate speech. <br> Beatrice did as Daniel had done<sup>1246</sup> <br> Relieving Nebuchadnezzar from the wrath <br> Which rendered him unjustly merciless, <br> And said: “Well see I how attracteth thee <br> One and the other wish, so that thy care <br> Binds itself so that forth it does not breathe. <br> Thou arguest, if good will be permanent, <br> The violence of others, for what reason <br> Doth it decrease the measure of my merit? <br> Again for doubting furnish thee occasion <br> Souls seeming to return unto the stars, <br> According to the sentiment of Plato.<sup>1247</sup> <br> These are the questions which upon thy wish <br> Are thrusting equally; and therefore first<sup>1248</sup> <br> Will I treat that which hath the most of gall. <br> He of the Seraphim most absorbed in God,<sup>1249</sup> <br> Moses, and Samuel, and whichever John <br> Thou mayst select, I say, and even Mary, <br> Have not in any other heaven their seats, <br> Than have those spirits that just appeared to thee, <br> Nor of existence more or fewer years; <br> But all make beautiful the primal circle, <br> And have sweet life in different degrees, <br> By feeling more or less the eternal breath. <br> They showed themselves here, not because allotted <br> This sphere has been to them, but to give sign <br> Of the celestial which is least exalted. <br> To speak thus is adapted to your mind, <br> Since only through the sense it apprehendeth <br> What then it worthy makes of intellect.<sup>1250</sup> <br> On this account the Scripture condescends <br> Unto your faculties, and feet and hands <br> To God attributes, and means something else; <br> And Holy Church under an aspect human <br> Gabriel and Michael represent to you, <br> And him who made Tobias whole again.<sup>1251</sup> <br> That which Timaeus argues of the soul<sup>1252</sup> <br> Doth not resemble that which here is seen, <br> Because it seems that as he speaks he thinks.<sup>1253</sup> <br> He says the soul unto its star returns, <br> Believing it to have been severed thence <br> Whenever nature gave it as a form.<sup>1254</sup> <br> Perhaps his doctrine is of other guise <br> Than the words sound, and possibly may be <br> With meaning that is not to be derided. <br> If he doth mean that to these wheels return <br> The honor of their influence and the blame, <br> Perhaps his bow doth hit upon some truth. <br> This principle ill understood once warped <br> The whole world nearly, till it went astray <br> Invoking Jove and Mercury and Mars.<sup>1255</sup> <br> The other doubt which doth disquiet thee<sup>1256</sup> <br> Less venom has, for its malevolence <br> Could never lead thee otherwhere from me. <br> That as unjust our justice should appear <br> In eyes of mortals, is an argument <br> Of faith, and not of sin heretical. <br> But still, that your perception may be able <br> To thoroughly penetrate this verity, <br> As thou desirest, I will satisfy thee. <br> If it be violence when he who suffers <br> Cooperates not with him who uses force, <br> These souls were not on that account excused; <br> For will is never quenched unless it will, <br> But operates as nature doth in fire <br> If violence a thousand times distort it. <br> Hence, if it yieldeth more or less, it seconds <br> The force; and these have done so, having power <br> Of turning back unto the holy place. <br> If their will had been perfect, like to that <br> Which Lawrence fast upon his gridiron held,<sup>1257</sup> <br> And Mutius made severe to his own hand,<sup>1258</sup> <br> It would have urged them back along the road <br> Whence they were dragged, as soon as they were free; <br> But such a solid will is all too rare. <br> And by these words, if thou hast gathered them <br> As thou shouldst do, the argument is refuted <br> That would have still annoyed thee many times. <br> But now another passage runs across <br> Before thine eyes, and such that by thyself <br> Thou couldst not thread it ere thou wouldst be weary. <br> I have for certain put into thy mind <br> That soul beatified could never lie, <br> For it is near the primal Truth, <br> And then thou from Piccarda might’st have heard <br> Costanza kept affection for the veil, <br> So that she seemeth here to contradict me. <br> Many times, brother, has it come to pass, <br> That, to escape from peril, with reluctance <br> That has been done it was not right to do, <br> E’en as Alcmaeon (who, being by his father<sup>1259</sup> <br> Thereto entreated, his own mother slew) <br> Not to lose pity pitiless became. <br> At this point I desire thee to remember <br> That force with will commingles, and they cause <br> That the offences cannot be excused. <br> Will absolute consenteth not to evil; <br> But in so far consenteth as it fears, <br> If it refrain, to fall into more harm. <br> Hence when Piccarda uses this expression, <br> She meaneth the will absolute, and I <br> The other, so that both of us speak truth.” <br> Such was the flowing of the holy river <br> That issued from the fount whence springs all truth; <br> This put to rest my wishes one and all. <br> “O love of the first lover, O divine,”<sup>1260</sup> <br> Said I forthwith, “whose speech inundates me <br> And warms me so, it more and more revives me, <br> My own affection is not so profound <br> As to suffice in rendering grace for grace; <br> Let Him, who sees and can, thereto respond. <br> Well I perceive that never sated is <br> Our intellect unless the Truth illume it, <br> Beyond which nothing true expands itself. <br> It rests therein, as wild beast in his lair, <br> When it attains it; and it can attain it; <br> If not, then each desire would frustrate be. <br> Therefore springs up, in fashion of a shoot, <br> Doubt at the foot of truth; and this is nature,<sup>1261</sup> <br> Which to the top from height to height impels us. <br> This doth invite me, this assurance give me <br> With reverence, Lady, to inquire of you <br> Another truth, which is obscure to me. <br> I wish to know if man can satisfy you <br> For broken vows with other good deeds, so <br> That in your balance they will not be light.” <br> Beatrice gazed upon me with her eyes<sup>1262</sup> <br> Full of the sparks of love, and so divine, <br> That, overcome my power, I turned my back <br> And almost lost myself with eyes downcast.</p>
