<p class="lede">Virgil’s discourse of love⁠—The abbot of San Zeno.</p>
<p class="verse">An end had put unto his reasoning<sup>862</sup> <br> The lofty Teacher, and attent was looking <br> Into my face, if I appeared content; <br> And I, whom a new thirst still goaded on, <br> Without was mute, and said within: “Perchance <br> The too much questioning I make annoys him.” <br> But that true Father, who had comprehended <br> The timid wish, that opened not itself, <br> By speaking gave me hardihood to speak. <br> Whence I: “My sight is, Master, vivified <br> So in thy light, that clearly I discern <br> Whate’er thy speech importeth or describes. <br> Therefore I thee entreat, sweet Father dear, <br> To teach me love, to which thou dost refer <br> Every good action and its contrary.” <br> “Direct,” he said, “towards me the keen eyes <br> Of intellect, and clear will be to thee <br> The error of the blind, who would be leaders. <br> The soul, which is created apt to love, <br> Is mobile unto everything that pleases, <br> Soon as by pleasure she is waked to action. <br> Your apprehension from some real thing<sup>863</sup> <br> An image draws, and in yourselves displays it <br> So that it makes the soul turn unto it. <br> And if, when turned, towards it she incline, <br> Love is that inclination; it is nature, <br> Which is by pleasure bound in you anew.<sup>864</sup> <br> Then even as the fire doth upward move <br> By its own form, which to ascend is born, <br> Where longest in its matter it endures,<sup>865</sup> <br> So comes the captive soul into desire, <br> Which is a motion spiritual, and ne’er rests <br> Until she doth enjoy the thing beloved. <br> Now may apparent be to thee how hidden <br> The truth is from those people, who aver <br> All love is in itself a laudable thing; <br> Because its matter may perchance appear <br> Aye to be good; but yet not each impression <br> Is good, albeit good may be the wax.” <br> “Thy words, and my sequacious intellect,” <br> I answered him, “have love revealed to me; <br> But that has made me more impregned with doubt; <br> For if love from without be offered us, <br> And with another foot the soul go not,<sup>866</sup> <br> If right or wrong she go, ’tis not her merit.” <br> And he to me: “What reason seeth here, <br> Myself can tell thee; beyond that await <br> For Beatrice, since ’tis a work of faith. <br> Every substantial form, that segregate<sup>867</sup> <br> From matter is, and with it is united, <br> Specific power has in itself collected, <br> Which without act is not perceptible, <br> Nor shows itself except by its effect, <br> As life does in a plant by the green leaves. <br> But still, whence cometh the intelligence <br> Of the first notions, man is ignorant, <br> And the affection for the first allurements, <br> Which are in you as instinct in the bee <br> To make its honey; and this first desire <br> Merit of praise or blame containeth not. <br> Now, that to this all others may be gathered,<sup>868</sup> <br> Innate within you is the power that counsels, <br> And it should keep the threshold of assent. <br> This is the principle, from which is taken <br> Occasion of desert in you, according <br> As good and guilty loves it takes and winnows.<sup>869</sup> <br> Those who, in reasoning, to the bottom went, <br> Were of this innate liberty aware, <br> Therefore bequeathed they Ethics to the world. <br> Supposing, then, that from necessity <br> Springs every love that is within you kindled, <br> Within yourselves the power is to restrain it. <br> The noble virtue Beatrice understands<sup>870</sup> <br> By the free will; and therefore see that thou <br> Bear it in mind, if she should speak of it.” <br> The moon, belated almost unto midnight,<sup>871</sup> <br> Now made the stars appear to us more rare, <br> Formed like a bucket, that is all ablaze, <br> And counter to the heavens ran through those paths <br> Which the sun sets aflame, when he of Rome<sup>872</sup> <br> Sees it ’twixt Sardes and Corsicans go down; <br> And that patrician shade, for whom is named <br> Pietola more than any Mantuan town,<sup>873</sup> <br> Had laid aside the burden of my lading;<sup>874</sup> <br> Whence I, who reason manifest and plain <br> In answer to my questions had received, <br> Stood like a man in drowsy reverie. <br> But taken from me was this drowsiness <br> Suddenly by a people, that behind <br> Our backs already had come round to us. <br> And as, of old, Ismenus and Asopus<sup>875</sup> <br> Beside them saw at night the rush and throng, <br> If but the Thebans were in need of Bacchus, <br> So they along that circle curve their step,<sup>876</sup> <br> From what I saw of those approaching us, <br> Who by good-will and righteous love are ridden. <br> Full soon they were upon us, because running <br> Moved onward all that mighty multitude, <br> And two in the advance cried out, lamenting, <br> “Mary in haste unto the mountain ran,<sup>877</sup> <br> And Caesar, that he might subdue Ilerda,<sup>878</sup> <br> Thrust at Marseilles, and then ran into Spain.” <br> “Quick! quick! so that the time may not be lost <br> By little love!” forthwith the others cried, <br> “For ardor in well-doing freshens grace!” <br> “O folk, in whom an eager fervor now <br> Supplies perhaps delay and negligence, <br> Put by you in well-doing, through lukewarmness, <br> This one who lives, and truly I lie not, <br> Would fain go up, if but the sun relight us; <br> So tell us where the passage nearest is.” <br> These were the words of him who was my Guide; <br> And someone of those spirits said: “Come on <br> Behind us, and the opening shalt thou find; <br> So full of longing are we to move onward, <br> That stay we cannot; therefore pardon us, <br> If thou for churlishness our justice take. <br> I was San Zeno’s Abbot at Verona,<sup>879</sup> <br> Under the empire of good Barbarossa,<sup>880</sup> <br> Of whom still sorrowing Milan holds discourse; <br> And he has one foot in the grave already,<sup>881</sup> <br> Who shall erelong lament that monastery, <br> And sorry be of having there had power, <br> Because his son, in his whole body sick, <br> And worse in mind, and who was evil-born, <br> He put into the place of its true pastor.” <br> If more he said, or silent was, I know not, <br> He had already passed so far beyond us; <br> But this I heard, and to retain it pleased me. <br> And he who was in every need my succor <br> Said: “Turn thee hitherward; see two of them <br> Come fastening upon slothfulness their teeth.”<sup>882</sup> <br> In rear of all they shouted: “Sooner were <br> The people dead to whom the sea was opened, <br> Than their inheritors the Jordan saw;<sup>883</sup> <br> And those who the fatigue did not endure <br> Unto the issue, with Anchises’ son,<sup>884</sup> <br> Themselves to life withouten glory offered.” <br> Then when from us so separated were <br> Those shades, that they no longer could be seen, <br> Within me a new thought did entrance find, <br> Whence others many and diverse were born; <br> And so I lapsed from one into another, <br> That in a reverie mine eyes I closed, <br> And meditation into dream transmuted.<sup>885</sup></p>
